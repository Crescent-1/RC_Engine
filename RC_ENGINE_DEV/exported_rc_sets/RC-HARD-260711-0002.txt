[PASSAGE]

In legal interpretation, in philosophical semantics, and in the ordinary quarrels of speakers who accuse one another of twisting words, the phrase "literal meaning" is entered as though it named a settled account. It functions as a standard of correctness: the meaning a sentence carries before intention, before circumstance, before the interested reading of any party. Judges reach for it to fix a contract's terms; philosophers deposit it as the semantic core on which pragmatic surplus later accrues; a witness is asked what a word "really" means. The concept presents itself as native to language, an unremovable floor. One might suspect the whole difficulty is verbal—that a sharper technical term would clear the ledger. The record does not support so tidy a settlement.

Granted that "literal meaning" was not discovered but manufactured, assembled in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries alongside a specific ambition—a language purged of ambiguity, its signs answering to fixed referents, its propositions transparent to any competent mind—the concept inherits the assumptions of that workshop. The Port-Royal logicians, and later the early analytic project, wrote as though words attached to things with minimal slippage, as though the situation of utterance added nothing a careful grammar could not subtract. This was not error awaiting correction; it was a design premised on conditions its designers took for granted, and the premise is what the concept still carries.

The criteria of literalness follow from that design: a meaning must be decontextualized, indifferent to who speaks and when, exhaustively definable in advance. Each criterion presupposes a linguistic world—stable publics, fixed social roles, indexicals held to a minimum, referents that stay put. Twentieth-century pragmatics dismantled the first; sociolinguistics recorded how thoroughly role and register saturate reference; computational semantics, attempting to specify meaning exhaustively for machines, ran aground on precisely the context the criteria promised to ignore. The evidence converges: the world the criteria describe is not the world in which speakers operate. It does not follow that the concept is therefore void wherever it appears.

For it may still hold. Consider the narrow room where variation is suppressed by force—a statute's defined terms, a tax schedule, a protocol whose vocabulary is stipulated and policed. There, context is not absent but artificially flattened, and literalness earns its keep. The record permits this much and no more. Whether the room is representative, whether law and interpretation may borrow a standard bred in it and apply it to ordinary speech, whether the flattening scales—these the evidence does not decide, and prudence forbids deciding them by assertion. What survives, then, is a shrunken claim: literal meaning is coherent where its conditions are manufactured, and only where they are.
