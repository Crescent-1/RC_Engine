[PASSAGE]

Consider the Victorian ideal in its cleanest form. The self-made man rises through the world by a logic almost geometric: effort produces skill, skill produces prosperity, prosperity confirms virtue, and virtue justifies station. Each rung of the ladder implies the one before it, so that a man's position becomes legible as a moral biography rather than an accident. It is a satisfying architecture, this ladder—every rail load-bearing, every step accounted for. Samuel Smiles could write it into a doctrine because it required so little external scaffolding; the causal chain seemed to hold itself up.

But look again, more slowly, at what the ladder assumes. It assumes a level floor to plant its feet on—some starting point from which effort begins to count. To a first approximation this is a small omission. Looked at again, it is the whole question: what happens to the man who has will in abundance but no capital to leverage it, no floor at all beneath the first rung? That gap, incidentally, between the theory's tidy causality and the unequal ground it stands on, turns out to be the more interesting object than either the ladder or the man climbing it.

The practitioners have their answer ready. Census returns, bankruptcy filings, probate records: three generations of English fortune show the same surnames recurring at the top, the same clustering by inheritance rather than industry. Merit, on this reckoning, predicts almost nothing.

So the theory is refuted. Except it isn't, quite. A theory of moral formation is not falsified by a distribution of wealth, any more than a ladder's design is disproved by the fact that most people never reach it. The practitioner's numbers are real. The rungs are real too. Both descriptions hold at once, describing different floors of the same building.

We have, in fact, been standing the whole time where the Victorians themselves stood—inside the collision, not above it. They read Smiles and audited their own account books in the same week, endorsing the ladder's logic while watching capital settle along bloodlines it could not touch. Their refusal to abandon either conviction was not muddle; it was the only honest response available to people who needed the ladder to be climbable and could see, in the same glance, that most of them were standing on no floor at all. To hold both is not evasion but the more accurate report of what the evidence, taken whole, actually permits us to conclude.
