The will instructs that the letters be burned, the house pulled down, the money spent in perpetuity on a chair in Sanskrit metrics. The executors comply, and mostly feel that they must, though it is not easy to say to whom the duty runs. The man who wrote the clause has no interests left to frustrate and no experience left to spoil. He cannot be disappointed, since disappointment requires somebody to have it. Yet the sense of obligation does not weaken when the mourners disperse; if anything, it hardens.

Two defences of that sense are standard. The first holds that the dead can be wronged: an interest survives its holder, and breaking faith harms a person who is simply not around to feel the harm. It is a doctrine one can state but not easily inhabit, and it purchases the obligation at the cost of a metaphysics few of its users would defend anywhere else. The second holds that the duty is really owed to the living — to the survivors who are watching, whose grief would be injured by the breach. This is more sober, and it proves too little. Brod's fault, if it was one, was not an injury to Kafka's mourners; there were hardly any, and none of them wanted the manuscripts burned.

Consider instead what a promise would amount to if it lapsed at the promisee's death. It would not be a promise. It would be a lease, terminating on a date the promisor cannot control, and everyone would price it accordingly while he was still alive. But the ability to reach past one's own ending — to found something, to bequeath, to have the letters burned, to be trusted about the disposition of a life's work — is a power the living want and cannot exercise on their own. It exists only because the practice is reliable in advance, and the practice is reliable in advance only because the dead man's grip is not routinely pried loose when he is no longer there to hold on.

So the dead are owed nothing, and must nonetheless be treated much as if they were owed everything. The price of that is real and should be named rather than softened. The practice will sometimes bind the living to preferences the world has finished with, and there is no clean rule saying when the binding may be broken — not because the rule is hard to find, but because a rule that named its exceptions would be read, in advance, by everyone contemplating a will, and the promise would become a lease again the moment the exceptions were legible. The credibility that makes the power worth having is bought precisely by tolerating the absurd cases.

The committee meets each spring, reads the clause again, and appoints another lecturer in Sanskrit metrics, to four students who registered by mistake.
