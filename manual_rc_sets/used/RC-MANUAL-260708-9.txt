[PASSAGE]

Every chemist is taught, early and firmly, to assign oxidation numbers — to pretend that the electrons in a compound belong wholly to one atom or another, so that each atom carries a tidy integer charge. Every chemist is also taught, usually in the same term, that this is not what the electrons do. Bonds are shared; charge smears; the integers correspond to nothing you could measure with an instrument. I have watched a hundred students discover the fraud and wait, briefly, for the correction to follow. It does not follow. We keep the fiction, examine on it, and build a career's worth of reasoning atop a quantity we have already conceded does not exist.

The reflex of the philosopher is to call this a convenient falsehood and move on, filing it beside the frictionless plane. That misses what is actually being stored. The value of the oxidation number is not that it is roughly right most of the time; it is that we know, with unusual precision, exactly where and how it goes wrong. It strains at the peroxides, where oxygen misbehaves; it fractures at the mixed-valence oxides, where the bookkeeping demands a fractional charge and the atom obliges by being, in effect, two atoms at once; it collapses in the organometallics, where whole schools of chemistry have organized themselves around the point of collapse. The error comes with a map of its own failures, and that map is not incidental to it — the map is the knowledge. A student who has memorized only the correct answer has learned nothing; a student who knows the places the answer breaks has learned the bonding.

So the temptation, periodically indulged by reformers, is to replace the integers with the truth — with the continuous, computed charge densities that are, after all, what the electrons really do. Here the mistake is a mistake about levels. The reformer imagines he is upgrading a false claim to a true one. What he is actually doing is discarding a coordinate system in which the failures are legible for one in which they are merely present. The smeared density is more faithful and tells you less; it does not announce where it becomes surprising, because it has flattened the reference against which surprise was defined. To be disenchanted about the oxidation number is not to believe it — I do not, and neither, when pressed, does anyone who uses it well — but to see that its wrongness is doing work no right answer has been shown to do.

I will not pretend this settles into a principle. The defense holds only while the failure modes stay taught alongside the rule, and the moment they are dropped — the moment a cohort learns the integers as facts rather than as a well-charted lie — the justification lapses, and we are merely propagating an error like any discipline too tired to revise. Keep the fiction, then, but keep it the way one keeps a fault line marked: not because the ground is solid there, but because that is where you have learned to read the trembling. What the reformer and I disagree about is not the chemistry. It is what a discipline is for, and I am not sure the disagreement reduces further than that.
