[PASSAGE]

The case for the forger deserves a better hearing than its champions usually give it. Put crudely, it says that a panel no eye can tell from a Vermeer is as good as a Vermeer, and that the fuss over provenance is auction-house theology. Put well, it is harder to wave off. Any property that counts toward a picture's aesthetic worth, the argument runs, must be a property one can locate in the picture; a virtue that leaves no visible trace — one lodged entirely in a signature, a ledger, a chain of ownership — is not an aesthetic virtue but a certificate, and certificates are the business of notaries, not of critics. Stated so, the position has the inconvenient merit of being largely correct.

I am willing to grant it more than its own advocates dare. The history of expert authentication is not a flattering one: the studied cases show connoisseurs certifying, with the full solemnity of the trained eye, works later traced to a workshop in Delft or a hand in the present century. More damning, the celebrated "touch" of the master is, in a good many documented episodes, assigned after the label is known and not before — the attribution arrives first, and the sensitive description hurries after, dressed as its cause. To this extent the forger's friends are right, and righter than they tend to argue: much of what passes for perception is retrospection with a good tailor.

And yet the same premise, pressed, declines to stay on their side. If only the visible counts, then everything visible counts — including the properties a stroke takes on when one sees it as a first venture rather than a patient copy. The identical mark reads as hesitation in the maker and as caution in the mimic; boldness and mere confidence occupy the same pigment. What one knows does not wait outside the picture, decently apart from the seeing; it enters the seeing and rearranges it. The autonomist wins the field only by fixing "the visible" at the thickness of the varnish, and the eye he appeals to has never been so thin.

So the verdict, when it comes, keeps something for each party and flatters neither. Authorship earns its aesthetic keep exactly where it alters what is seen, and nowhere else — which surrenders a wide territory the connoisseur has long billed for. In the decorative panel, the competent minor landscape, the object whose interest never turned on whose hand it was, the forgery loses nothing by being a forgery, and the expert who exacts a premium there is charging rent on an empty room. The price of saying that authorship matters, said honestly, is admitting how seldom it does.

Which leaves the criterion in the opponent's hands, where it is safer. Let the burden fall on the attributionist to exhibit, canvas by canvas, what the name makes newly visible; where he can show it, the label has earned its aesthetic wage, and where he cannot, the panel is exactly as good as it looks. It is not the concession they wanted, but it is a real one, and the test I am handing back — show, canvas by canvas, what the name makes visible — is one they are free to turn on my own attributions.
