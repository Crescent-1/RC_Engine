[PASSAGE]
Every collection of proverbs is a collection of contradictions, and the collectors have always known it. Look before you leap; he who hesitates is lost. Many hands make light work; too many cooks spoil the broth. Absence makes the heart grow fonder, and out of sight, out of mind. Two responses have been available for as long as the collections have. The debunker concludes that folk wisdom is not wisdom at all but a heap, from which anything and its opposite can be drawn. The apologist replies that each saying is true within its domain, and that the wisdom lies in knowing which one applies.
The apologist's reply does less work than it appears to. Grant that each proverb carries a silent condition — look before you leap when the risk is unbounded and irreversible. The condition is not in the proverb. It is supplied by whoever quotes it, and supplying it requires having already judged the case. No proverb tells you which proverb applies, and there is no proverb about that, either. Whatever guidance is being provided, the proverb is not the thing providing it.
Watch how they are actually used. Nobody consults a proverb in advance, the way one consults a timetable. The saying arrives afterwards: when the cautious man has already declined the risk and wants his caution ratified, when the neighbour at the funeral must say something and cannot say nothing. It closes deliberation rather than informing it, and it closes it by lending a private decision the authority of nobody in particular — the anonymous, unanswerable dead. On this description the contradictory twin is not a defect in the corpus. The corpus has to stock both verdicts, or it could not ratify either.
Which means that asking whether folk wisdom is true has been asking of it something it never held out. "Many hands make light work," said to a man struggling with a table, is not a hypothesis about the productivity of labour; it is a summons, and summonses are not false. The question does not get answered. It stops being a question — and it should be said plainly what that costs, because the cost is not small. When the saying is cruel — the nail that stands up gets hammered down, delivered to a child who has been singled out — the natural reply is that this is simply not true, and that reply is exactly what the analysis has taken away. What remains is to contest the move: to ask who is being ratified, and against whom, and by what authority a dead consensus speaks. That is slower, it sounds like evasion, and it forfeits the great advantage the other side enjoys.
The advantage is that they were only quoting. The child, meanwhile, has to answer in the form the saying was delivered, and has nothing to hand but another saying.
