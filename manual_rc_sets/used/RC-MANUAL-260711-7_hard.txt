[PASSAGE]
There are two stories about technical standards, and they have been quarrelling for decades. In the first, the better one wins: the shipping container, the four-foot-eight-and-a-half-inch gauge, the plug that does not electrocute you, prevail because they work. In the second, they win by accident and are then stuck — QWERTY, the gauge that a cart rut chose, the format that got to market first. The second story now carries most of the prestige, having the pleasing shape of a debunking. What is less often noticed is that both describe adoption as a decision: something an industry got right, or got wrong.
The container did not persuade anybody. Once the ships were built to the box, the cranes calibrated to the box, the insurers pricing anything else as exotic cargo, the port that preferred loose stowage was not out-argued. It was made to pay for its preference, and went on paying until the preference became a luxury. What spread was not a verdict on merit but a change in the price of refusal — and the price of refusal is set by everyone else's compliance, not by the standard's own qualities.
This is why the quarrel has stayed alive so long. To the small port, the odd-shaped machine, the crate of live goats, the standard is a constraint, and the constraint is not imaginary. To the network — the thing that lets a bolt from one country fit a nut from another, that lets a box leave Ningbo without anyone in Rotterdam being told what is inside — it is the enabling condition of movement itself. Both descriptions hold, at their own scales, and one can spend a career oscillating between them with the sensation of having discovered something.
The oscillation is not a hard problem. It is a question whose answer is "both," and its persistence conceals a better one. Whatever cannot be boxed does not disappear when the box wins. It is displaced, and it lands somewhere: on the handlers who repack odd cargo at the edge of the yard, on the small port that now trucks its freight three hundred miles to a large one, on the shipper who pays a surcharge for the exception and passes it to whoever cannot refuse it in turn. That residue is where a standard's costs are actually settled, and unlike the quarrel it has an address. Follow the exception; see who is billed for it. The answer is determinate, it varies from standard to standard, and it is the thing neither story was built to see, since one is busy admiring the box and the other is busy explaining that the box need not have won.
At the gate the surcharge appears on the invoice as a line marked handling, and at the edge of the yard the goats are repacked by hand.
