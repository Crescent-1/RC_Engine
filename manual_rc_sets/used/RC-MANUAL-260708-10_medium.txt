[PASSAGE]

It has become second nature to talk about our machines as though they bred. The smartphone has ancestors; the camera and the pocket calculator are among its parents; ill-adapted designs die out while fitter ones proliferate, and the market is the environment doing the selecting. I will confess at once that I find this clarifying. The biologists have lent the historians of technology a genuinely good lamp, and much of what it shows was invisible before anyone switched it on.

Still, a borrowed lamp lights some things by leaving others in the dark, and it is worth saying plainly what this one costs. Evolutionary language earns its keep by explaining change that no one intended; but it buys that power by translating every choice into a pressure and every decision into an adaptation, until the people who actually preferred one design to another dissolve into the "environment" that is said to have selected them. My verdict is that the lens is worth keeping. It is not worth forgetting the price on the tag.

Grant the illumination first, because it is real. The familiar keyboard was never chosen by anyone as the best arrangement of letters; it accumulated, got locked in, and outlived every reason that first recommended it. No committee designed that drift, and older vocabularies of plan and purpose simply miss it. Talk of variation, inheritance, and selection catches exactly this — the way tools acquire a momentum that answers to no one's blueprint. On this terrain the imported frame does not merely decorate the history; it sees what the native categories could not.

But say that a design "was selected by the market" and you have quietly filed the people who chose it under weather. Each such sentence retires a question it no longer seems to need: who decided, on what grounds, and might have decided otherwise. The evolutionary frame is right that outcomes routinely exceed intentions; its charge is that it makes the surviving remainder of intention — the deciding that is still, in fact, ours — progressively harder to notice, and harder to defend once noticed. So we keep the lens, and we pay in a specific coin: a growing difficulty in seeing the next choice as a choice, at just the moment it is still open to be made.
