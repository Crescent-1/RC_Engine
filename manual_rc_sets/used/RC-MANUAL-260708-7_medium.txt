[PASSAGE]

Two rival processions arrive at the same tomb. One lays its wreath for the Smith of the invisible hand, apostle of self-interest and the unplanned market; the other for the Smith of sympathy and the impartial spectator, a moralist embarrassed by his libertarian afterlife. Each delegation carries its own volume — the Wealth of Nations against the Theory of Moral Sentiments — and each accuses the other of holding a forgery. The dispute has the shape of a custody battle: two claimants, one estate, and a judge expected to name the legitimate heir.

I want to say the judge should decline the case, and not for want of evidence. The custody frame assumes the inheritance is a doctrine — a body of conclusions to be owned, defended, and handed down intact. But what Smith left was not a doctrine; it was a tool built to work on a particular problem, and a tool is not inherited the way a fortune is. Ask what the two volumes were for, together, and the fight over which is the real will dissolves — not because both sides are right, but because both have mistaken the object.

The problem was specific and, in the 1750s, urgent. A commercial society was dissolving the old bonds — kinship, rank, the mutual obligations of a settled hierarchy — that had once done the work of holding conduct in place. The question Smith set himself was how a society of strangers trading for gain could remain a moral order at all, once the feudal ligatures were cut. Sympathy and the market were not two philosophies for two heirs to divide; they were two halves of one answer to that single question — the spectator supplying the restraint the market could not, the market supplying the coordination sympathy could not reach. Split them and you do not get two Smiths. You get two fragments of a mechanism, each useless for the work it was cut to do.

Return now to the tomb. The delegations are not fighting over an heir; they are each hoping the dead man will settle a quarrel he never posed — our quarrel, over whether markets need a conscience bolted on or run clean without one. That is a live and serious question, but it is ours, and Smith's authority cannot adjudicate it, because he was answering a different one. What lies in the estate, unclaimed by either party, is the question itself: how strangers hold together once the old bonds are cut. It is still open, and neither wreath is laid on it.
