[PASSAGE]
We keep our ruins as ruins. The abbey stays roofless, the bombed church keeps its open sky, and the fort on the hill is held at the degree of collapse at which somebody found it. Nobody proposes rebuilding these; the proposal would be received as vandalism. What we tell ourselves we are doing is keeping the past — arresting a further loss, holding what came down to us before more of it goes.

But nothing came down to us in a state. A ruin is not an object; it is a process caught in the act. It was falling when we found it, and it would have gone on falling. The abbey of 1750 was not the abbey of 1890, and neither was the abbey of 1215. The collapse had no privileged frame, no moment at which it was most itself. When we stabilize a ruin we do not stop time, and we do not recover a past. We select a frame from a sequence that has no natural stopping point. We hold it there, at cost, with steel. The frame we select is ours. Preservation, done to a ruin, is composition.

And the composing goes all the way down, into decisions no one records as decisions. This ivy is picturesque and stays; that ivy is doing structural damage and is stripped. The wall is propped, but the prop is recessed so it will not be seen — and the not being seen is a choice about how ruined the ruin should look. The rubble at the base is cleared, because rubble reads as neglect. Yet rubble is what a collapse actually leaves. Every conserved ruin carries two dates and admits to one: the century it was built in, and the century that decided how far down it had got.

The argument that usually breaks out here is not the interesting one. It is whether a ruin held up by hidden steel is still authentic, whether we are preserving a thing or making one. Its uninterestingness is a finding — not a dodge. Authenticity was never available. There was no true state to be faithful to, only a slope. What can be asked, and answered, is which moment on the slope was chosen, and by whom, and what they wanted the past to look like when they picked it — a question about 1946, or 1890, answerable from the minutes of the committee that voted to leave the tower at the height the bomb had left it.

Q1. Which of the following best captures the passage's central claim?
