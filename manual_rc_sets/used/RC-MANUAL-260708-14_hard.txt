[PASSAGE]
When a scientific theory finally carries the day, we reach for one of two explanations, and they have quarreled for a century. The first is that the evidence compelled it: the world spoke, the rival accounts failed to fit, and any honest observer had to yield. The second holds that assent was manufactured — that interests and the rhetorical skill of a theory's partisans did the work the evidence could not. The two stories share a stage. Both take the decisive contest to have occurred among the people competent to judge: the specialists at the frontier who could weigh the rival accounts.
What neither story explains is the rest of us. A consensus is not the verdict of the dozen who could referee the dispute; it is a condition of the whole field, thousands who never refereed anything and never could have. The interesting question is not how the competent few were persuaded but how their persuasion became everyone's, including those in no position to check it.
The answer lies downstream of the argument, in the years after it. A theory passes into consensus not when the competent are convinced but when it becomes teachable — when it can be compressed into a textbook chapter and set as a problem undergraduates can finish before lunch. What is inherited this way is not held as a claim once contested; it arrives as the settled floor of the discipline, what you learn in order to do the work at all. The generation that receives it never weighed it against the rivals, which by then have not been refuted so much as dropped from the syllabus. Selection here runs on a criterion no evidentialist and no debunker names: not which theory is truest, and not which serves whom, but which is easiest to reproduce in a mind that will never assess it.
It would be easy to hear in this the old debunking: consensus as an accident of pedagogy, belief without warrant. That is not the claim. Teachability is a real property of theories, and not unrelated to truth — a true account tends to be teachable because the world keeps vindicating it in the exercises, the problems come out, the predictions land. But the relation is loose at the joints. A theory can be eminently teachable for reasons indifferent to whether it is right: because it is tidy, or because it spares the student ambiguities a truer account would force him to sit with.
So the consensus is earned, and the word should stand. But it is earned by a test that quarrel has no room for — neither the evidence at the frontier nor the interests behind it, but whether a theory can be got into the next mind without distortion. That test tracks truth often enough that calling that agreement baseless would be a slander. That it tracks truth only roughly, by a mechanism indifferent to the gap when the two come apart, is the part worth refusing to forget.
