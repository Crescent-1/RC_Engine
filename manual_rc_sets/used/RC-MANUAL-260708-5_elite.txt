[PASSAGE]

A concert of Persian classical song ends, and the listeners filing out do not ask whether the singer was skilled. They ask whether hāl came. The word is Sufi in ancestry and common in Tehran green rooms; it names whatever makes one evening an event and another, note for note its equal, a recital. At home the word works without friction. Trouble begins at the border, when a critic writing in English reaches for an equivalent and finds the shelves stocked with the wrong kind of goods.

The first consignment offered in exchange: "atmosphere," "mood," "presence." Each is a property word. Where the London reviewer writes that a performance possessed presence, the Tehran listener says that hāl arrived — and the grammar is the tell. Properties are possessed; hāl visits. A property belongs to the performance and can be praised in the singer's absence; hāl is said to come, to descend, occasionally to refuse. Property words travel well precisely because they fasten to objects, and objects can be shipped. What hāl names spoils in transit, like fruit picked for a market it will never reach.

The laboratory tradition makes the second attempt. "Flow" — the psychologist's term for absorbed, self-forgetting performance — at least treats an occurrence rather than a thing. But flow is filed under the performer's mind, and hāl declines the filing: veterans of the repertoire insist it can desert a technically flawless night and descend, unearned, on a singer whose voice is breaking. It seems to belong to the room, if it belongs anywhere. So the deadlock: one customs house registers the evening under the work, the other under the psyche. Neither ledger has a column for what the listeners were pointing at.

A third tradition breaks the tie. Sanskrit poetics built its edifice on rasa — "taste," "savour" — and its theorists spent centuries insisting, against exactly these two filings, that rasa resides neither in the dramatic work nor in the spectator's private feeling but in the tasting itself, an event requiring both and reducible to neither. Two traditions with no common ancestor arriving at the same refusal suggest the refusal is load-bearing. The resistance of hāl to translation is not a lexical gap awaiting a cleverer dictionary; it marks a disagreement about where aesthetic value lives. A critical vocabulary is an inventory of locations, and the inventory English criticism inherited was drawn up for goods that hold still: the painting, the printed poem. Performance got appraised with instruments calibrated for objects.

None of this convicts property-criticism of error on its own ground; for the things that hold still, it holds. The verdict is narrower and firmer: judgment of performance, in any tradition, needs terms for events, and borrowing object-terms smuggles in a metaphysics along with the vocabulary. Until reviewing learns to grade evenings rather than works, hāl will stay where it is. Some words refuse the crossing not because the road is poor but because what they name has no address on the far side.
