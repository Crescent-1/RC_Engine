The case against measurement was assembled slowly, and at first politely. Economists observed that any indicator adopted as a target soon ceased to indicate anything: schools taught to the test, hospitals timed discharges to flatter mortality figures, police forces reclassified offences rather than prevent them. Anthropologists gave the pattern a cultural history, tracing how audit had migrated out of accountancy and into universities, clinics, and ministries, until institutions appeared to exist chiefly to generate evidence of their own performance. By the century's end the critique possessed a canon, a working vocabulary — gaming, proxy failure, audit culture — and a settled tone of weary prophecy. Its authors understood themselves as insurgents, and they expected, reasonably enough, to be resisted.
They were not resisted. They were quoted. Within a decade the critique was appearing in the strategy papers of the very bodies it had described, recited fluently by the administrators it had cast as its antagonists, sometimes with page references.
What followed was not a retreat from measurement but its renovation. Organisations announced that they had moved beyond crude metrics, and proved it by commissioning subtler ones: well-being indices, qualitative dashboards, narrative indicators, engagement scores weighted for context. A consulting industry grew up around the promise of measuring holistically, its brochures quoting the critics with evident affection. The perimeter of quantification did not shrink under attack; it expanded, and the expansion now carried a certificate of self-awareness. An institution that could recite the case against its own instruments had, by that recitation, acquired a newer and better instrument. To many of the original critics this looked like simple defeat — their weapon picked up, examined, and mounted on the wall of the enemy's lobby.
That verdict is understandable and, I think, wrong, though not in the way the institutions would prefer. The critique's deepest claim was never that particular numbers mislead; it was that institutions metabolise whatever touches them into material for their own legitimation. Judged against that claim, the absorption of the critique is not a refutation but a demonstration — the theory's most complete confirming instance, performed by its own subject matter. What the critics misjudged was not the diagnosis but their exemption from it: they assumed that an argument about capture would itself stand beyond capture, that describing the machine placed one outside the machine. The decade proved otherwise, and in proving it, vindicated them.
The vindication has a price, and it should be stated plainly rather than mourned. Because the critique's vocabulary now functions as a credential — because "audit culture" in a vice-chancellor's mouth signals sophistication rather than alarm — that vocabulary can no longer do critical work, whatever the intentions of the person using it. Anyone who still wants to say what the critics said must now say it without their words: in longer, slower, more local descriptions of particular schools and particular wards, descriptions too specific to be quoted in a strategy paper. The critique survives, but only on the condition that it gives up the idiom that made it famous, which means it must begin again, each time, from the evidence of a single case.
