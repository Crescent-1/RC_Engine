[PASSAGE]

Call a piece of work amateurish. You have not described it. You have sentenced it. In current usage the word does something. It does the job of a verdict. Unserious. Untrained. Not to be admitted as evidence. We pass it on a self-taught historian's book. A village society's excavation report. A diagnosis offered by someone who has merely read everything on the disease. The sentence feels timeless, as though the deficiency sat in the work itself, waiting for the label. It does not feel like something with a date of manufacture.

Dig deeper. The ground gives way. When the word entered English in the eighteenth century, an amateur was one who pursued a thing for love, and the working contrast was not with the expert but with the mercenary. The amateur you could trust. No salary bent his judgment. The contempt is not original to the word. It is sediment. Laid down in a single and datable stratum. The criteria by which we still convict amateur work were minted in that stratum, stamped for conditions that belonged to it. We handle the judgment daily without reading the date on the coin. The date, once read, is the argument.

The stratum can be excavated with precision. Between roughly 1870 and 1910, learning professionalized. Universities began certifying training. Journals installed referees. Laboratories and collections grew too costly for private means. By the 1890s the amateur had been redefined as a professional minus something. The new criteria were not a conspiracy. Credentials. Institutional affiliation. Answerability to peers. They were reasonable answers to a real problem, because serious work now required instruments only institutions could hold. In 1781 an amateur found a planet from a private garden. A century later. Discovery had migrated to mountaintop observatories with salaried staff. A garden could no longer see far enough. The verdict on the amateur was, in its own era, largely a verdict on his equipment.

Whether that era's conditions still hold is the question. I am declining to answer it, not from delicacy, but because the evidence in hand cannot. The excavation shows the criteria were answers to a problem of access. But it does not show what the present problem is. Acquitting the amateur on the old evidence would repeat the original error in reverse. New criteria would have to be minted before any new verdict could be read out. No one has done the minting. Meanwhile the major sky surveys and genome consortia have committed to placing their complete archives on the open web within the decade. When those collections stand open, the question will be forced again, on workers the 1890s criteria were never cut to sort.
