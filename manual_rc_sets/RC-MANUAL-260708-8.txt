[PASSAGE]

Ask a psycholinguist how long it takes to find a word and a number comes back: some six hundred milliseconds from seeing a picture of a dog to saying "dog." The number is not invented. It is measured, replicated, sensitive to how common the word is and how recently it was last used. And it answers a question that, examined closely, was asking after something that is not there.

The picture behind the number is a warehouse. Words sit on shelves; a signal goes out; the right item is located, pulled, delivered to the mouth. "Retrieval" is the standard term, and it is a fetching word. On this picture finding takes time because searching takes time, and the six hundred milliseconds is the length of the errand. The tip-of-the-tongue state seems to clinch it: there sits the word, on its shelf, and the clerk cannot reach it. What could be more plainly an object temporarily out of reach?

Watch those milliseconds and the shelf is not there to be found. From the first instant dozens of words are partly active at once — the near-synonyms, the words that merely sound alike, the word in the other language a bilingual keeps. Nothing sits still awaiting collection; a field of candidates rises and falls together, each inhibiting the rest, until one settles out. The mistake was never in the stopwatch. It was in the noun. There is no located item and no act of locating; the question "how long to find the word" presupposes a thing found and a moment of finding, and neither survives the look.

This is easy to over-read, and the over-reading is tempting: if no word is fetched, then perhaps nothing is stored and knowing a language is some formless flux. That does not follow, and the field's own data forbid it. Frequency effects, neighbourhood effects, the sheer lawfulness of the errors — a speaker's knowledge has structure, and the structure governs what settles. What crosses the winning threshold must cross it before the mouth moves; that much is necessary. But a candidate's crossing is not the sufficient event the warehouse promised, a single retrieval datable to the millisecond. The crossing is the last ripple of a settling that had no single seat and no starting gun.

What remains, once the warehouse is emptied, is not nothing. The reaction times remain, and they still track difficulty. The errors remain, and they still betray structure. What dissolves is the errand and its object — the stored word, the search, the moment of arrival — and what stands in their place is humbler and less tractable: a settling with no address, clocked all along by an instrument that was timing something other than a journey.
