[PASSAGE]

The reformers who spent the last century demanding that institutions open their books did not imagine themselves as utopians. Their premise was almost mechanical: concealment shelters incompetence and worse, and light disinfects. They borrowed their faith from a courtroom and a laboratory at once — testimony given in the open is harder to falsify, a specimen held to the light easier to judge. Against the closed committee, the sealed ledger, the decision reached by men whose reasons were never written down, they proposed one remedy: disclosure. Make the institution show its workings and the workings will improve, since no one behaves the same way watched as unwatched. It was a critique of power that took a curiously optical form, and for a long stretch it worked as advertised.

The victory is easy to read in the furniture of ordinary institutional life. Minutes are published, budgets posted, the audit and the records request now count as unremarkable fixtures rather than hard-won concessions. Whole professions grew up to service the expectation — compliance officers, disclosure counsel, the salaried officer whose very title is transparency. The demand became a norm and then a reflex; an agency that refused to publish today would seem not merely secretive but eccentric, a holdout against a settled expectation. Measured against its own stated aims, it is difficult to name a reform of the period that succeeded more thoroughly.

But a reflex is easy to feed. The institution obliged to disclose learns to disclose enormously — to meet the request for its reasons with ten thousand pages in which the reason, should it survive at all, drifts unmarked among procedural sediment. Nothing is concealed; nothing is locatable. Compliance by volume is still compliance, and it honours every letter of the rule while quietly draining the rule of its point.

The error, if there was one, lay in measuring the reform along the wrong axis. The question was never how much an institution reveals but in which direction the seeing runs. A posted budget makes the institution faintly legible to a citizen with no time to read it; that same institution, across the same years, learned to read the citizen with increasing exactness — his filings, his purchases, his movements composed into a profile far more usable than any minutes he might retrieve. Disclosure travelled one way and legibility the other, and the two were never equal quantities. The room did not open so much as turn: the glass that once shielded the institution from view now faces inward, and the citizen stands on its lit side.

So the question worth keeping is not whether the institution has been compelled to show itself. That contest is settled, and its settlement altered almost nothing about the arrangement that mattered. The reformers won the argument they were having; they did not notice the argument moving. The question is who, within the arrangement, has been rendered readable to whom, and at what resolution — a budget posted where no one can parse it and a dossier assembled where its subject will never see it are not opposite conditions but one asymmetry, reported from either end.
