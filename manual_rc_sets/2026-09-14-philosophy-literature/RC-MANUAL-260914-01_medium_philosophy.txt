[PASSAGE]

A promise is easily mistaken for a confident prediction. Both concern something that has yet to happen, and both can give another person a reason to make plans. Yet their resemblance hides a difference in what the speaker has done. Someone who predicts a clear afternoon offers information about the weather. Someone who promises to meet a friend at the station gives that friend a claim on a particular action. The prediction concerns how things are likely to turn out; the promise helps determine what the speaker owes, even if the promised action was already likely to occur.

This difference becomes visible when circumstances change. If the weather forecast improves, a prediction of rain becomes less credible. If an invitation to a more enjoyable evening arrives, a promise to collect someone does not become less binding. Its practical point is partly to prevent an attractive alternative from deciding the matter afresh. Promising would add little to ordinary intention if the speaker could withdraw whenever a better offer appeared. The friend waiting beside a suitcase is entitled to more than an updated account of the driver's preferences. A promise makes some reasons for changing one's mind insufficient.

That does not make every promise unconditional. The station may close, an emergency may require assistance elsewhere, or the person expecting collection may release the driver. These possibilities have different effects. An emergency can justify failing to perform while leaving something to explain or repair. A release removes the other person's claim itself. Permission therefore matters in a way that a favourable change in the driver's mood does not. The person to whom the commitment was made has a role in deciding whether it remains, rather than merely a strong interest in what eventually happens.

Reliance is important, but it cannot explain the whole obligation. A neighbour might expect help with moving a table because help has regularly been offered before. Disappointing that expectation may be inconsiderate without breaking a promise. Conversely, a friend might book an alternative ride despite a promise, perhaps because the driver has previously been unreliable. That precaution reduces the practical loss caused by another failure; it does not turn the promise into a prediction. The driver cannot make a commitment disappear by becoming so unreliable that nobody depends on it. Expectations concern what someone anticipates; commitments also concern what that person may demand.

The distinction helps explain why an apology sometimes remains appropriate even when a broken promise caused no inconvenience. The apology acknowledges that the speaker treated a shared arrangement as though it were still a private intention, available for unilateral revision. Compensation alone may miss that feature, just as an apology alone may leave an actual expense unpaid. Keeping a promise is therefore more than producing a desirable outcome. It respects the other person's standing within an arrangement the speaker helped create, including that person's authority to release the commitment when performance no longer matters to them. Where release has not occurred, the absence of measurable harm does not settle what is still owed.
