[PASSAGE]

A map records not danger but the encounter between danger and an office equipped to notice it. The 1984 flood-hazard sheet for Nepal makes the point involuntarily. Across the Terai, the southern lowland strip, the paper is busy to the point of congestion: hazard bands in graduated tints, braided channels drawn with the patience of a surveyor, symbols for embankment, gauge, spillway, settlement threatened. Move north and the sheet empties. The middle hills thin out; the high Himalaya arrives as something close to untouched paper, crossed only by contour and a few blue threads. The obvious reading of such a document is that it is faithful — that floods do in fact gather where the rivers slow and spread, and that the mountains, being steep, are simply not where people drown. The map carries itself with enough authority to make that reading feel like mere literacy. And the reading is not wrong so much as it is incomplete in a way the sheet itself declares; the density of the south is not only a claim about water but a claim about attention, and the two have been printed in the same ink.

The word survey does the damage. Borrowed from revenue assessment, where a surveyed district was a taxable one, it travelled into hydrology carrying its old sense intact, and the legend preserves the inheritance: one category for low hazard, another, separately keyed, for areas lacking survey or gauge record. Most of the northern blank sits in the second. That does not make the sheet deceptive. It makes it partial in a stated way.

The strongest case for the old reading survives the legend. Gradient disperses water rather than pooling it; narrow valleys hold fewer fields; settlement in the high districts is thinner, and drainage is quick. A planner could argue that instruments and embankment schemes clustered in the Terai because the exposure was genuinely there. Tempting, and mistaken — the argument treats a distribution of gauges as evidence for a distribution of risk, when the gauges were sited by the same offices that drew the sheet.

Follow what the paper does, step by step. Colour appears where a channel has a gauge upstream. Names appear on the same rivers: Kosi, Narayani, Bagmati, each lettered in full, while the torrents that feed them above the hills go unlettered or are given a number only. Gauges themselves sit near roads, near district headquarters, near irrigation works — places already written into files. So the sheet compounds. Observation follows administration; colour follows observation; and the finished image is read backwards as vulnerability. The two blanks are not alike, and treating them as one class is where the reading fails: an uncoloured stretch of plain means measured and found calm, an uncoloured mountain means unvisited. Nothing here shows an intent to conceal. Cartographers work with what the gauges give them. But a document of this kind explains a durable arrangement in which mountain communities can face debris floods and burst glacial lakes while remaining marginal to the budgets that measure and defend. The verdict is narrow and it holds: the sheet does not show where hazard stops, only where hazard had entered the state's field of vision by 1984, which is a smaller country than Nepal.
