[PASSAGE]
Boredom is a young word. It enters English around the middle of the nineteenth century, arrives late in most of its neighbours, and behind it there is only a scatter of distant relations: the monk's acedia, the courtier's ennui, the taedium Seneca describes as a queasiness at sameness. Historians of feeling have taken the lateness seriously, and the inference they draw from it comes in one of two shapes. Either the experience is not the plain, universal thing it appears to be, or our vocabulary is a far poorer record of inner life than we like to think.
The first has become the more attractive. On that account boredom is manufactured: an artifact of clock time, of the working week that invented the empty Sunday, of a childhood in which somebody else decides when the afternoon ends. Time must first be cut into units and then owed to someone before it can be felt as wasted; the word arrives with the condition and dates it. The rival account is duller and, for a while, more persuasive. The feeling is old, it says, and the lexicon is merely slow. Naming lags experience, particularly experience nobody thought worth dignifying with a name.
The evidence assembled for that second view is genuinely impressive. Seneca's nausea. Pascal's man who cannot sit quietly alone in a room. The marginalia of medieval copyists, who complain of thin ink, cold fingers, and pages that will not end. Read at speed, these are boredom in period costume, and the manufacture thesis begins to look like an argument that mistook a silence in the dictionary for a silence in the world.
They are not, however, the refutation they seem, because the manufacture theorist has no trouble absorbing them. Acedia, he can say, is a sin: a failure of the soul before God, with an addressee and a remedy. Boredom has no addressee; it is a grievance against the world at large, and its structure — not its unpleasantness — is what the modern conditions produced. The sceptic then absorbs that reply in turn: the difference, he says, is an interpretation laid over a constant feeling, and interpretations are precisely what change. Each account can eat the other's evidence without indigestion. Nor could it be otherwise, since the archive is a record of what was sayable, not of what was felt; both hypotheses predict exactly the texts we have, and no further text will separate them.
Suspension here is not a failure of nerve but the verdict the evidence supports, and it is not idle, because it withdraws the move both parties keep making: the youth of the word is no evidence about the age of the feeling, in either direction, and never was. What survives is smaller and firmer. The copyist at Reichenau stays where the parchment leaves him, complaining about the ink, in a condition we are not entitled to call ours and not entitled to call foreign.
