[PASSAGE]

A layer of white pumice sits over the streets of Akrotiri, on Santorini, sealing the doorways at the height a man's shoulder would reach. When did it fall? Radiocarbon runs on seeds carbonised in that ash cluster in the late seventeenth century BC. The pottery lying under the same ash, and the Egyptian objects that travel with such pottery, point a hundred years later. Neither method is vague. Both give tight intervals. The obvious answer, offered for forty years, is that the two scales are slightly misaligned and want only careful cross-tuning until they meet.

The tuning has been done, at length and with skill. Sequences of tree rings, counted one by one, have been matched to measured carbon fluctuations; Bayesian models have squeezed the intervals; the Aegean samples have been re-run in three laboratories. The gap did narrow, by perhaps a generation. But the narrowing came from fixing faults elsewhere in the calibration curve — a regional offset in the eastern Mediterranean growing season, an error in a section of Irish oak — not from any movement in the disputed material itself. Each improvement in precision has arrived and left the residual difference standing. That is the awkward part. A rounding error shrinks toward zero as instruments improve. This one has held its shape while everything around it sharpened, which is the behaviour of a feature rather than a fault, and forty years is long enough to notice.

So the question was probably wrong. Radiocarbon does not date the eruption. It dates the atmosphere a seed breathed while it was still a living plant, filtered through solar cycles and ocean exchange. Egyptian chronology dates the eruption only in the sense that it places a jar in a reign, and a reign in a king-list compiled by scribes with their own reasons for counting. Both are accurate accounts of what they measure. Both refer to the same physical morning on Thera — and that shared reference is what makes comparison seem obligatory, though it does not make the two scales commensurable. One is a record of carbon in air; the other, a record of what a bureaucracy chose to remember and in what order. Agreement on the event is the necessary condition for aligning them. It has been mistaken, quietly and for a long time, for a sufficient one.

Which leaves the harder question standing. What is a chronology for? If it is a neutral grid, two grids that disagree is a scandal. If it is an instrument, we may simply own two of them. The consolation available here is that better science will decide the matter, and that consolation should be set down; the evidence shows the old goal was wrong, not what should replace it. Perhaps some narratives are askable only under one calendar. That is not yet an argument. It is a suspicion, and it waits, along with the seeds — barley, and a few peas — still sealed in a jar in the storeroom at Akrotiri.
