[PASSAGE]

In 1911 a Sheffield firm engraved a donor's name on a brass plate above a hospital ward door. The plate came down in 2019. Between those dates the man acquired no new opinions, made no fresh investments, wrote no letters. He was already inert when the reassessment began. Yet the removal was argued in the same grammar we use for a colleague who has said something indefensible at a meeting: he must answer for this, the record must be corrected, the standard applies to him as it applies to everyone. That last clause has an honest ring. Standards that bend around the identity of the accused are not standards. Whatever else moral judgment is, it seems to require indifference to who is being judged, and to when. The dead do not get a discount for being dead.

But death changes something that indifference cannot reach. Blame, unlike a measurement, is aimed. It is addressed to an agent who might yet do otherwise, and its whole point is the *otherwise*. Direct it at someone who has stopped acting and the address fails. What remains is not a lie. It is a monument, which is a different sort of object, and it does not correct anyone.

Say this out loud and the concessions seem to follow on their own. If the living are granted endless reinterpretation—the second chance, the changed man, the acknowledgment that a person at fifty is not the person who acted at twenty-eight—then consistency appears to demand either the same generosity for the dead or a dignified silence about them. They cannot recant. They cannot be rehabilitated. To keep arraigning them looks like beating a locked door, and it is easy to conclude that the decent course is amnesty, or amnesia, or the historian's careful refusal to score. That conclusion moves too fast. It assumes blame is a transaction between judge and judged, so that when one party is absent the transaction voids.

It is not. Blame has an audience. Always did. The dead man is the occasion, not the recipient.

So the plate comes down, and it comes down rightly, though not because a Victorian ironmaster is being punished; he is beyond the reach of any sanction the ward committee can devise. It comes down because a hospital is a going concern with staff and patients and a future, and what it hangs on its walls instructs them. Read that way, the demand for universal standards in the first paragraph was never wrong so much as misdescribed: the standard is universal, the *sanction* is not, because sanction is an instrument and instruments require something to work on. Symmetry between past and future was a grammatical illusion, produced by a verb that takes the same form in both directions. Which is why no verdict on the dead can be final in the way verdicts on the living are, and why the arguments about the plates will keep restarting—not from indecision, but because the people they are actually addressed to keep being replaced.
