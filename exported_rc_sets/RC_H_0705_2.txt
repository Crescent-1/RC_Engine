# READING COMPREHENSION PASSAGE

When Sartre insisted in 1945 that "existence precedes essence," he meant to overturn a hierarchy older than philosophy itself: the assumption that human beings arrive with a purpose already stitched into them, waiting only to be realized. On his account, we are first thrown into being and only afterward, through the accumulation of choices, become anything at all. The doctrine is exhilarating in its austerity. It abolishes excuses, dignifies the trembling moment of decision, and hands each person the full weight of authorship over a life. Yet the very radicalism that made the lecture resonate in a Paris still smelling of liberation is also where its architecture begins to buckle.

Consider the move that Sartre treats as the linchpin of his ethics. Having stripped away God, human nature, and any pre-given values, he still wants to prevent his philosophy from collapsing into "anything goes." His device is a peculiar universalizing gesture: in choosing for myself, I choose "as if" I were legislating for all humanity, and this imagined constituency supposedly disciplines my will. But the gesture is borrowed. It is Kant's categorical imperative wearing existentialist clothing, and it sits awkwardly on a thinker who has just denied that humans share any common essence. If there is no shared nature, there is no shared standard by which my choice could be a model for anyone else. The egomaniac who authentically wills his own aggrandizement, and who cheerfully invites everyone to do likewise, passes Sartre's test as easily as the saint. Authenticity certifies the sincerity of a choice; it says nothing about its content.

This is not a minor lacuna. It reveals that Sartre's ethics smuggles in the very universality it claims to have earned from within. Simone de Beauvoir, writing two years later, saw the gap and refused the smuggling. Her wager in *The Ethics of Ambiguity* is that freedom is never abstract but always *situated* — lived by particular people under particular constraints of poverty, oppression, and denied education. For her, the obligation to others' freedom is not imported through a Kantian back door; it follows necessarily from taking seriously that my own freedom is exercised in conditions that others' choices have shaped, and that shape theirs. Where Sartre gestures thinly at "situation," Beauvoir insists on the concrete social architecture within which the anguish of choice is sometimes swallowed whole by the anguish of survival.

The difference is not merely emphasis. Sartre's version can be read, and has been read, as a self-help creed: seize your life, defy the categories others impose, remake yourself. That reading is not wrong, but it is incomplete, because it treats the chooser as unencumbered. Beauvoir's correction returns the body and the world to the picture. Willing one's own freedom, she argues, commits one to fighting for the conditions that make anyone else's freedom possible. Freedom that ignores its own conditions is, in the end, less a philosophy than a flattering wish.

# ANSWER KEY & ELIMINATION LOGIC

**1. Correct: (B)**
Structural bridge: The passage grants Sartre's power ("exhilarating," "resonate") but identifies the borrowed Kantian move as the point where "the architecture begins to buckle," then presents Beauvoir as the repair grounded in concrete conditions. (B) holds both halves.
- (A) *Scope distortion*: the passage praises Sartre and never calls for abandonment; it says his reading is "not wrong, but incomplete."
- (C) *Imported framework / causal reversal*: the passage criticizes Sartre's Kantian borrowing as ill-fitting; it does not endorse the categorical imperative as the "only defensible foundation."
- (D) *Half-truth*: postwar popularity is mentioned in passing but is not the central argument, which concerns the ethics's internal flaw and Beauvoir's correction.

**2. Correct: (C)**
Structural bridge: The egomaniac example directly follows the claim that authenticity "certifies the sincerity of a choice; it says nothing about its content" — the sentence is the illustration of that gap.
- (A) *Scope distortion*: the example is analytic, not a historical case study.
- (B) *Causal/attribution error*: it shows a flaw in Sartre's *test*, not Sartre's personal endorsement of aggrandizement.
- (D) *Scope distortion*: the passage targets Sartre's specific move, not "all universalizing systems."

**3. Correct: (B)**
Structural bridge: "Borrowed" is glossed by the passage as Kant "wearing existentialist clothing" that "sits awkwardly" because Sartre "denied that humans share any common essence" — i.e., the principle rests on assumptions Sartre rejects.
- (A) *Causal reversal*: "smuggling" and "back door" imply the debt was *not* openly credited.
- (C) *Imported framework*: the passage never claims the categorical imperative is incompatible with atheism as such.
- (D) *Scope distortion*: the passage identifies one borrowed move, not a wholesale inability to generate ethics.

**4. Correct: (B)**
Structural bridge: The consultant equates authentic ownership with guaranteed ethical conduct — precisely the error the passage exposes via the egomaniac: authenticity secures sincerity, not moral content.
- (A) *Wrong objection imported*: the situatedness critique is Beauvoir's separate point, not the authenticity/content gap the scenario exhibits.
- (C) *Half-truth*: the common-standard problem is real in the passage but concerns the universalizing move, not the sincerity-vs-content confusion the scenario shows.
- (D) *Scope distortion*: the self-help observation is descriptive of reception, not a criticism of the reasoning in the scenario.

**5. Correct: (A)**
Structural bridge: The passage says Beauvoir's obligation "follows necessarily" from freedom being "exercised in conditions that others' choices have shaped." For this to avoid "smuggling," the shaping relation itself must generate the obligation without an external principle — exactly (A).
- (B) *Contradiction*: Beauvoir's whole point is that situations *differ* by social position.
- (C) *Imported framework*: the passage contrasts Beauvoir's derivation with the Kantian smuggling; it does not reconcile them.
- (D) *Half-truth / distortion*: the passage says survival-anguish can *swallow* freedom-anguish, not that they are identical.

**6. Correct: (C)**
Structural bridge: The tone combines praise ("exhilarating," "dignifies," "not wrong") with structural critique ("buckle," "smuggles," "unearned") and endorsement of Beauvoir's "correction."
- (A) *Scope distortion*: too negative; the author affirms the doctrine's value and appeal.
- (B) *Half-truth*: admiration is present but qualified by serious criticism.
- (D) *Scope distortion*: the author clearly evaluates, siding with Beauvoir's repair.
