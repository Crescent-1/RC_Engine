[PASSAGE]

When a university votes to apologise for land taken from a displaced people, what follows the vote? The statement is drafted, read beneath a seal, placed on a website, and cited in the next admissions brochure. At a reception, someone folds the programme into quarters and slips it into a coat pocket. The institution has altered the public record: an injury once described as settlement or development now has an agent and a victim. That alteration matters. It is not nothing. But an apology cannot count as ethical action merely because it has been spoken; it must be joined to changes in ownership, access, or power that its own account of the past makes due. This does not mean that every sentence of regret must be followed by a cheque or a deed transfer before sunset. It means that speech which names a confiscation while leaving every resulting advantage undisturbed chiefly repairs the speaker’s moral portrait. The apparent subject is the fit between public words and restitution.

Acknowledgment is often said to come first, and properly so. Without it, later claims appear to be demands from nowhere; responsibility has not yet entered the grammar of the institution. A formal apology can therefore be a necessary first act. Names matter. Records matter. Admission matters. Consider a school that excluded a neighbourhood’s children for decades, then opens its archive and publishes the decisions that did so. Parents can now point to more than inherited suspicion. Still, the value of that publication lies in the claims it permits: altered admissions, funded transport, authority over the archive itself. Remorse and remedy are not parallel ceremonies. One identifies a wound; the other changes who must bear its consequences.

Historical injuries differ in source documents, surviving property, living descendants, and the powers an institution still possesses. No common tariff can settle them. A watchmaker does not repair every stopped watch by replacing the same spring. One has a cracked jewel; another a bent balance; a third has been wound until the mainspring has bitten itself. Such variation governs the design of a duty, not its disappearance. Difficulty is a bad solvent.

The harder fear should not be brushed aside. Grief is not a bill of sale, and a relationship scarred by removal or exclusion can be further demeaned when its history is translated into units, deadlines, and approved categories of loss. Private mourning, ceremony, and renewed human regard may be less degrading than an institutional bargain. But neither sentimentality nor accounting gets to close the file. The dispute is misdescribed when apologies are ranked by the apparent depth of their feeling. Their public language makes certain obligations harder to evade; that is why the language matters, and why its audience extends beyond those on the stage.

Apologies remain necessary because recognition is public work. The injured still must articulate remedies; institutions must make them measurable. Neither side owes reassurance. Recognition is not completion, though it may become one condition under which changed access, resources, and authority can be demanded.
