[PASSAGE]

A single natural event, counted twice by different apparatus, can return two figures that refuse to be reconciled, and the refusal is sometimes the most informative part of the record. Off one stretch of the Aberdeenshire coast in the summer of 1868, the same herring shoal was assigned two tonnages that stood far apart. The landing men worked from the net-haul tally entered at the curing yards as the boats came back — barrels counted, crews paid against them, a figure built out of what had actually been lifted from the water. The watching men worked from the harbourmaster's silvering log, kept from the cliff path in the hours before any boat put out, in which the sea's surface change of colour over a stretch of water was read off against landmarks and converted to bulk. Each camp held the other's number to be loose work.

The watching men's central claim can be granted more or less whole. A shoal shears, sounds, splits and drifts, and much of that motion happens before a net touches it; silvering registered mass that the tonnage books, by their construction, could never register, since those books opened only at the moment of landing. The colour on the water was evidence, and evidence of something. Yet the quarrel was not, at bottom, about which instrument held the shoal more faithfully in its grip, because the two records were never reaching for the same object. One recorded a catch — a quantity delimited by boats, gear, weather and the hours men could work. The other recorded an episode of massing, of which any catch is a sample taken under constraint.

Nineteenth-century Scottish fishery board returns of the period tend to print such divergent figures in adjacent columns, entered without annotation and without any attempt at reconciliation, the discrepancy treated as an ordinary feature of the season rather than as a scandal requiring inquiry — which is intelligible once it is seen that counting a shoal by its landings resembles counting a crowd by the gate receipts of the hall it later filled: the receipts are exact, auditable, and drawn around a boundary the hall's builders chose, not one the crowd ever had; people turned away at the door, people who drifted off down the street, people who were never going to enter but were unmistakably part of the gathering, all fall outside the count without falsifying it. The gap is not error. It is the width of the doorway.

Both numbers survived because each fed an administration with its own appetite. The curing yards needed tonnage for wages, cooperage and export duty; the harbour board needed early warning to allocate boats and berths across a short season. Under most configurations of this kind, a figure endures as long as something is downstream of it. And what kept the gap open was less the instruments than the clerks — no office in the chain was ever obliged to hold the two columns against each other, so nobody did, and the discrepancy sat in the ledgers as a feature of the paperwork rather than a problem in the world. A shoal, on this showing, was probably never one thing waiting to be measured twice, but two purposes each precipitating an object out of the same water.
