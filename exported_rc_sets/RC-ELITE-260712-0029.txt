[PASSAGE]

Signal-detection theory was never meant for the courtroom. It was built for men in dark rooms watching for the blip of an enemy submarine, a framework for deciding whether a faint disturbance on a screen was a real object or the sea's own static. The core is simple. A signal sits buried in noise; an operator, straining to see it, must set a threshold above which he calls "present" and below which he calls "absent," and from the resulting pattern of hits, misses, false alarms, and correct rejections the theory extracts two independent quantities — a sensitivity that measures how well the operator distinguishes signal from noise, and a criterion that measures how eager or reluctant he is to say yes. Psychophysicists loved it because it separated perception from disposition; it told you what the observer could see apart from what he wanted to declare. When the DNA exonerations began — when men who had spent decades in prison walked out because a laboratory finally overturned a fingerprint match that had once seemed unimpeachable — those of us who trained examiners went looking for a way to make our discipline accountable, and we found this apparatus waiting, elegant and quantitative and utterly foreign. We imported it wholesale. The examiner became a detector. The two prints, latent and known, became signal and noise. The examiner's declaration of identity became a threshold crossed. And for the first time we could speak of an examiner's performance in the numbers that science respects: a hit rate, a false-alarm rate, a curve you could plot and compare across a whole profession that had, until then, offered only the word of the credentialed.

The studies delivered. They showed that examiners possess a real, measurable sensitivity to ridge detail that survives every manipulation of their willingness to declare a match, and that a great many past "errors" were not failures of the eye at all but failures of the threshold — an examiner too cautious here, too confident there. That distinction was worth having.

But the model assumes a signal that exists independently of the man who reads it. A submarine is there or it is not, before any operator looks. A latent print is not so obliging. Its "signal" is partly built by the examiner's own prior exposure — the arrest report he glanced at, the prosecutor's confident framing, the way the print was lifted and enhanced and cropped into evidence. The noise floor moves with the context. And a theory whose entire arithmetic rests on a fixed distribution has no cell, no coordinate, no place to record a signal that the observation itself helps create.

So the numbers bought us something real, and we should not pretend otherwise: they exposed bias, they made calibration training possible, they gave the profession its first honest mirror. The price came quietly. Institutions took the validated sensitivity and stamped their examiners as certified detectors, and that certification became a wall behind which the contextual contamination — the thing the model cannot see and therefore cannot score — went unexamined. The curve does not settle what happened in the courtroom; it only settles what a detector could do in a lab. I no longer accept that these are the same question. Ask the Madrid bombing print, the one three examiners agreed matched Brandon Mayfield, an Oregon lawyer who had never been to Spain.
