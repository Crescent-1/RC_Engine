# THE RC PASSAGE

**Passage (≈540 words)**

The standard defense of expert authority runs through reliability. We defer to the cardiologist not because she commands us but because her judgments track truths we cannot ourselves verify; deference, on this view, is epistemic outsourcing, rational precisely to the degree that the expert's competence exceeds our own. The picture is clean. It is also, on inspection, circular in a way that matters. To know that an expert is reliable, I must already possess some independent purchase on the domain—enough to grade her track record. Where I lack that purchase entirely, I cannot assess reliability; I can only assess the social signals that stand in for it: credentials, institutional affiliation, the confidence of peers. So the layperson does not, strictly, defer to competence. He defers to a system that certifies competence, and his trust is directed one level up from where he imagines it to be.

This is usually treated as a minor embarrassment, patched by noting that certification systems are themselves reliable. But that patch relocates rather than dissolves the problem. The certifying institution now occupies the position the individual expert did—its trustworthiness must itself be assessed by someone, on some grounds, and the layperson is no better positioned to audit a licensing board than a surgeon. Trust, it seems, does not terminate in knowledge. It terminates in more trust.

One might accept this and remain untroubled.

For a functionalist, the regress is not a scandal but the whole point. Institutions exist precisely to convert unanswerable individual questions into answerable collective procedures; the system works not because anyone within it is individually trustworthy but because the arrangement of incentives, cross-checks, and reputational exposure makes systematic error costly. Nobody audits the licensing board because nobody needs to. The board is disciplined by the malpractice suit, the rival board, the journalist. Reliability, on this account, is an emergent property of friction, not a possession of any node. And this is genuinely powerful: it explains how bodies of knowledge remain broadly sound despite being staffed by fallible, self-interested people who could never individually justify the deference they receive.

Yet the functionalist buys stability at a price he tends not to name. His mechanism works only where the frictions are real—where suits are filed, rivals compete, journalists are free and funded. These conditions are contingent, unevenly distributed, and, crucially, invisible to the person doing the trusting. The layperson who trusts a captured regulator and one who trusts a well-disciplined one are, from the inside, doing exactly the same thing. Nothing in the phenomenology of trust distinguishes warranted deference from its counterfeit. The functionalist has explained why the system tends to work without giving any individual a way to know whether it is working in his case—which was the original question.

So we are returned, by a longer road, to the layperson's predicament, now sharpened. He was told his deference was rational because grounded in reliability. It was in fact grounded in institutional friction he cannot observe. And the friction, when present, is real enough to make the trust warranted—but its presence is exactly what he is not positioned to verify. The rationality of his trust and his access to that rationality have come apart. Whether that gap is a defect to be closed or simply the condition under which knowledge is possible at all is not something the argument, as it stands, can settle.
