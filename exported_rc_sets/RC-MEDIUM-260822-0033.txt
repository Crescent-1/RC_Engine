[PASSAGE]

A man I know keeps a spare key to a flat he has not been invited into for six years. That is the sort of fact we can record. Most friendships absorb small violations without anyone bothering to record them: a confidence passed on at a dinner, a birthday missed twice running, a loyalty quietly declined when it would have cost something. In the cases that have been studied — mostly interview studies, which means self-report, which means reconstruction — these injuries sort themselves along what looks like a scale. Trivial at one end, grave at the other. The scale invites a conclusion it cannot support, namely that if both ends are legible, the midpoint must be too, and somewhere along that line friendship crosses into its own absence.

But some breaches do not read as points on a line at all. After them, the bond is not a friendship carrying damage; it is something that has stopped being that kind of thing. What changes is not the quantity of injury but the category of the relation — the way a cracked lens and a shattered one are not two degrees of the same optical defect. The instrument still exists. It is no longer an instrument.

We should grant the counterevidence at close to face value. Friendships have survived what looks, from outside, unsurvivable: a confidence sold for advantage, a betrayal in court testimony, an affair with a spouse disclosed and, after years, absorbed. Most such reports come from the betrayed party, late, which is a known distortion — Judith Shklar noted that the injured are also the historians of the injury, and their chronologies are written backward from the outcome. Still, the cases are real. They suggest the threshold is not fixed but contingent: on remorse offered and believed, on shared history, on whether the two people have anywhere else to go. Which sounds like the discovery that there is no threshold, only choice. It is not that.

The temptation, then, is to locate the line by better criteria. Repair impossible. Trust unreconstitutable. Contrition insufficient. Each term sounds measurable until we ask who holds the gauge. Repair is impossible when one party judges it so; trust is unreconstitutable when it has not been reconstituted. The criteria do not measure the threshold. They restate it in a longer form.

And this dissolves the question of intent and magnitude rather than answering it. We wanted to say: a small betrayal done deliberately outweighs a large one done carelessly. But deliberateness is not a property we read off the act. It is attributed — no: more precisely, it is inferred by the person who was hurt, from evidence they cannot fully audit, about a mind they cannot enter. Magnitude travels with that inference. The two variables were never independent. So the narrowed claim is this, and only this: friendships do end, and the ending is observable, sometimes to the day. The crossing is not. Not merely unmeasured. Unmeasurable, because the quantity that would have to be measured does not exist prior to someone's reading of it. We can watch the light go. We cannot say where the shutter fell.
