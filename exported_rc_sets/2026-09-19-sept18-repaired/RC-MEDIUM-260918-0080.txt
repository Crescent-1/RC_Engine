[PASSAGE]

Summer Time, in the sense Parliament gave the phrase in May 1916, named a legal operation and nothing more: an instruction that clocks should read one hour later than the sun over Greenwich warranted, so that the hands moved while the light stayed where astronomy had left it. The Act fixed a prescribed period. On the appointed night, the twenty-first of May, two in the morning became three. After that instant every institution keeping official time — post offices, the courts, the whole apparatus of published hours — had to treat the advanced clock as the true one, and the country's evenings gained their famous extra hour of light.

The cause was coal, or the argument that it would be. Germany and Austria-Hungary went first, on the thirtieth of April 1916; Britain followed on the twenty-first of May; France came round on the fourteenth of June. William Willett had been pressing the scheme since 1907 and had got nowhere; he died the year before the Act that carried his idea. What changed was not the arithmetic but the war. A factory working to daylight burned less gas at the bench. A household that went to bed earlier lit fewer lamps. Every scuttle of coal not burned in a parlour was one the Admiralty might have.

The Act's operative words are worth reading slowly. During the prescribed period, it says, the time for general purposes shall be "one hour in advance of Greenwich mean time", and any hour named in an Act, deed, or instrument is to be read accordingly. The sentence works instantly wherever time is written down. Railway timetables, bank hours, court summonses, factory gates governed by printed rule — by the morning of the twenty-second the public face of the country agreed to the minute. Elsewhere the same sentence does nothing by itself. A statute cannot reach into a kitchen and turn the hands; somebody has to stand on a chair and do it.

The arrangement worked like an extra gear dropped into a running train of wheels. Parliament turned the first one. The railways meshed with it at once; they had to, since a timetable is only agreement in print. Factories took the next tooth, then offices, then shops adjusting their shutters. Households came last, and unevenly. A farm hand still went out when the dew lifted; the cows knew nothing of the prescribed period. Milk trains ran to the new hour and were met by men working to the old one. Appointments were missed. Some village clocks kept stubbornly to the sun, which had not moved.

What the summer of 1916 demonstrated, read back from the end of it, was the reach of a coordinating instrument. Institutions were swung onto a new hour overnight, held there nineteen weeks, and swung back, with a precision the fuel argument never had to match. Economy was the language of the debates, the renewals, the imitations abroad; economy is not a measurement. I have looked for a wartime figure that separates the hour from the blackout and the shortage running alongside it, and have not found one. Parliament could advance the clock by decree and compel every institution that published an hour to follow. The coal had to be weighed, and no statute has ever weighed anything.
