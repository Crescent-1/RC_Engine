[PASSAGE]

Fault, in divorce law, names a pleading requirement rather than a moral verdict: the petitioner's obligation to state and establish something the other spouse did before the court will act. The layer to dig at is 1969. California's Family Law Act, in force from January 1970, and England's Divorce Reform Act, in force from January 1971, both swept away a catalogue of matrimonial offences and left one ground: the marriage had broken down. Beneath the shared word the machinery differs. In California the petitioner states that irreconcilable differences have caused an irremediable breakdown; the court finds that they exist; the respondent's denial cannot defeat the finding; months, not years, then run before judgment is final. In England the petitioner pleaded irretrievable breakdown too, but breakdown by itself proved nothing. No decree could issue unless the petitioner established one of five statutory facts — adultery, behaviour the petitioner could not reasonably be expected to live with, two years' desertion, two years' separation with consent, five years' separation without it — and once a fact was proved, breakdown was presumed from it. Each fact reads like a separate ground and was argued over accordingly. The ground was breakdown; the facts were routes to it, and only two of the five stood open on the day a marriage collapsed. Whatever kept accusation alive in England, it was not a disagreement in principle about what dissolves a marriage.

A price list in time drove the rest. Adultery and behaviour were available at once; the quiet routes cost two years, or five. Accusation bought speed, so petitions carried mild, formulaic particulars that nobody tested. Mrs Owens, petitioning on behaviour in 2015, was refused at first instance, in the Court of Appeal in 2017 and in the Supreme Court in 2018, because her husband defended. The jam was contest, not principle.

Paperwork followed. The 'special procedure' of 1973, extended in 1977 to every undefended petition, sounds like a searching track for hard cases; it was the ordinary one, a file read without hearing or witness. Scotland copied the English list in 1976. Canada's 1985 Act cut its clean route from three years to one. Australia in 1975 and West Germany in 1977 let separation alone carry breakdown. Paper made accusation cheap; the clock made it wanted.

The Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020, in force from April 2022, abolished the facts. A statement of breakdown by one spouse or both is now conclusive; only jurisdiction or validity can be contested. Twenty weeks, then six, cannot be shortened. The old separation periods were evidence lived through before filing; this reflection period is a wait imposed after it, and it falls on all hundred thousand or so couples a year, where before about half pleaded behaviour and went fast.

If the law no longer asks what a spouse did but only how long a marriage must be held in suspense, whose interval is it and who pays? The 1969 divergence measured what each statute charged for leaving, not how much blame a society wished to keep. Blame is spent; the charge is what is left. Whether the reform worked will be settled by the fixed twenty-six weeks rather than by the accusation it retired, though a few years make a thin layer to read.
