[PASSAGE]

An old public ritual does not, of itself, determine what modern institution grows out of it. Those of us who explained Strasbourg's reach by its Christkindelsmärik's antiquity must answer: Nuremberg's market is documented from the sixteenth century and sits at the center of that city's sense of itself, yet it never spread. The mechanics differ plainly. In Nuremberg a visitor enters the Hauptmarkt, circulates among rows of uniform red-and-white stalls, watches the opening address from the Frauenkirche gallery, and leaves through barriers that mark where the market stops. In Strasbourg the visitor never quite leaves it.

Strasbourg adopted "Capitale de Noël" as an official designation in 1992, and the market since then has occupied Place Broglie, Place Kléber with its great tree, the cathedral square and a chain of smaller places, with hundreds of chalets and lit streets threading between them. Nuremberg kept its roughly two hundred stalls in one square, retained the Christkind's biennial proclamation, and enforced a design code and a list of locally characteristic goods. Anyone counting squares and lights would call the difference obvious. What actually diverged, though, was who held the right to define what the market was.

Strasbourg's city government treated the market as an instrument for moving people. Spread the chalets, spread the spending. Extend the illuminations down a shopping street and the street becomes market. Nuremberg's council treated its market as an object held in trust, something with edges that needed guarding, and its rules read accordingly: what may be sold, how a stall may look, who may hold a pitch. Regulation of that kind does keep a thing recognizable. It also fixes its size. Licensing, stall design and pedestrian routing are the ordinary levers by which an inherited celebration becomes either a network laid across a city or a performance staged inside a square.

None of this made Nuremberg marginal. The Christkindlesmarkt draws crowds that strain the old town for four weeks, including coach parties from across Europe, and it does so while remaining unmistakably itself — which disposes of the lazy claim that expansion alone produces success. But attendance and institutional transformation are different things. Nuremberg's enormous crowds are pulled into one guarded event and then released; the city around the Hauptmarkt is unchanged by December. Strasbourg's market reorganized the commercial geography of an entire island, and the retailers, hoteliers and tram operators arranged themselves around that fact.

The municipal documents say as much without meaning to. Strasbourg's tourist and city communications have for three decades built themselves around the "Capitale de Noël" label — a claim about the whole city, not about a square — while Nuremberg's market regulations specify permissible merchandise and stall presentation, which is a claim about the contents of a defined space. Set those side by side and the age argument stops doing work; both traditions are old, and only one was authorized to expand. Governance, not authenticity, produced the divergence. Urban historians and municipal archivists are better placed than I am to finish the account, since the budgets, permit registers and pedestrian-flow plans survive and can be read year by year. What those records should show is a city that exported its tradition across its streets and another that preserved the same kind of tradition by refusing to let it move.
