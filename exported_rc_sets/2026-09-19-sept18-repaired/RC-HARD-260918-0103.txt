[PASSAGE]

What must "independent" mean if the word is to carry the weight placed on it? Consider where the inspected party pays, picks, or supplies the inspector: manufacturer employees signing off airframe compliance for regulators, rating agencies paid by issuers, auditors appointed by the companies they audit, certifiers engaged by developers. Passengers, bondholders, and occupants absorb the errors. The tally is not an indictment; it specifies a job, which is that a finding must not bend toward what the inspected party would prefer to hear.

Take the first candidate: independence as separate legal employment, the inspector salaried by another firm. Follow the money rather than the payslip. The client selects the firm from a shortlist, the firm invoices the client, the engagement partner's standing rests on renewal, and renewal rests on a client who was not embarrassed. So the claim here is not that payroll location is decisive. Nor is it that inspectors are dishonest; nor that a signed conflicts declaration is absent. Something else is doing the damage.

Second candidate: independence as limited tenure. Rotate the team; let no relationship harden. Test that logic against one case. A fresh audit or certification team arrives at a complex product whose compliance basis — the accepted methods, negotiated equivalencies and reasons for allowing a particular analysis — exists in usable form only in the heads of the manufacturer's own engineers. The newcomers spend their first cycle being taught. They are taught by the party they are inspecting. Beneath the familiarity problem sits a harder one: the inspected holds nearly all the knowledge of the object, so every inspector's competence is borrowed from it, and rotation raises the interest rate on that loan. Rotation still cures something real. It simply cannot be the whole of the definition, because independence was never a matter of emotional distance.

Third candidate: public employment. A salaried state inspector owes the firm nothing, cannot be dismissed by it, and on who pays is as clean an answer as the problem admits. That much is not in dispute. But the two failure modes are not mirrored. Private inspectors fail by selection — the inspected chooses, so the pliant are chosen. Public inspectors fail by thin budgets, by headcount that never matches the volume of work, and by careers that drift toward the industry they supervise. Those are different injuries and they do not share a cure. The state's depth of technical expertise is also precisely what ran out; delegation began because it did. The argument is not that the work should simply be taken back.

What survives is narrower: the inspected party has no say in who inspects it and no power to reward or punish that choice. Fees go into a pooled fund; an outside allocator assigns the inspector; findings run to the regulator whether or not the client likes them. The price is real — fees higher and less predictable, long working relationships lost, poor matches between assignment and expertise, slower schedules, and an allocator who must himself be kept honest. Accepting that price is the position, and it leaves each party still owing something: firms owe access and disclosure, regulators owe the funding and the enforcement that make the pool credible, and inspectors owe findings that hold without the client's approval.

(D) Rotation combined with separate payroll removes the pressures that make an inspector's findings bend toward the paying client.

Q2. Regarding the rotation of inspection teams, the author asserts that:

(C) It cures something real but cannot define independence, since fresh teams must be taught by the party they inspect.
(D) It is the one candidate the author accepts outright, since no relationship is allowed to harden over time.

Q7. The author of the passage is likely to disagree with which one of the following statements?

[ANSWER KEY & ELIMINATION LOGIC]

Q1 — Correct answer: (A)
  (A) CORRECT — It holds the whole movement: the three candidate definitions are each tested and found partial, and the surviving definition in the final paragraph is structural control over selection, explicitly purchased at an accepted price.
  (B) scope_inflation — The opening tally is explicitly "not an indictment"; it specifies a job rather than condemning delegation or alleging corruption.
  (C) half_truth — Paragraph four grants the concession but adds that state expertise ran out and "the work should simply be taken back" is denied.
  (D) adjacent_answer — Paragraphs two and three test and reject both candidates as incomplete; neither addresses the client's power over selection and renewal.

Q2 — Correct answer: (C)
  (C) CORRECT — The third paragraph concedes rotation "still cures something real" while showing that newcomers are taught by the inspected, so it cannot be the whole definition.
  (A) half_truth — The passage keeps rotation's genuine benefit while limiting its scope; it never endorses hardened long relationships as the superior alternative.
  (B) level_confusion — The paragraph closes by denying this: "independence was never a matter of emotional distance," the knowledge asymmetry being the harder problem.
  (D) adjacent_answer — Rotation is accepted only partially; the same paragraph states it "cannot be the whole of the definition" of independence.

Q3 — Correct answer: (C)
  (C) CORRECT — The passage states directly that the two failure modes "are not mirrored" and that "those are different injuries and they do not share a cure," so shared remedy is a stance error.
  (A) passage_supported — Paragraph four lists "careers that drift toward the industry they supervise" among public failure modes.
  (B) passage_supported — Paragraph four says a salaried state inspector "owes the firm nothing, cannot be dismissed by it."
  (D) passage_supported — Paragraph four contrasts private failure by selection with public failure by thin budgets and inadequate headcount.

Q4 — Correct answer: (D)
  (D) CORRECT — Paragraph four concedes that public employment settles who pays, then distinguishes budget, headcount and career pressures from private selection pressures. It therefore denies that a simple public takeover is the answer.
  (A) half_truth — The concession about who pays is immediately qualified; the paragraph ends by denying that the work should be taken back.
  (B) adjacent_answer — The passage states delegation began because the state's depth of technical expertise "ran out," which is capacity, not cost.
  (C) level_confusion — Paragraph two already says payroll location is not decisive; the surviving definition turns on selection, not on salary source.

Q5 — Correct answer: (A)
  (A) CORRECT — The load-bearing move is that the inspected party has "no power to reward or punish" the choice of inspector; a complaint channel that removes inspectors restores exactly that power.
  (B) adjacent_answer — Poor matches between assignment and expertise are listed among the costs the author explicitly accepts, not a hidden defect.
  (C) level_confusion — This concerns inspectors' feelings, whereas the argument turns on structural control over selection, not on morale or comfort.
  (D) scope_inflation — The final paragraph already concedes higher, less predictable fees and slower schedules as the accepted price of the arrangement.

Q6 — Correct answer: (B)
  (B) CORRECT — Paragraph two isolates the mechanism the author blames — renewal resting on a client who was not embarrassed — while expressly denying that dishonesty is the charge.
  (A) level_confusion — The opening insists the tally "is not an indictment" of inspectors; the problem is structural incentive, not individual compromise.
  (C) adjacent_answer — Rotation addresses hardened relationships, not the selection power the passage identifies as the actual source of the bending.
  (D) scope_inflation — Paragraph four refuses the public-takeover conclusion, noting that state capacity ran out and the injuries do not share a cure.

Q7 — Correct answer: (D)
  (D) CORRECT — Paragraph two expressly withholds this: payroll location is not decisive and a signed conflicts declaration is not absent, yet "something else is doing the damage."
  (A) passage_supported — The final paragraph states "the price is real" and itemises lost working relationships and higher, less predictable fees.
  (B) passage_supported — Paragraph four grants exactly this, calling it "as clean an answer as the problem admits" and not in dispute.
  (C) passage_supported — This is the author's own formulation closing paragraph three, which concedes rotation's value while denying it definitional sufficiency.

Q8 — Correct answer: (B)
  (B) CORRECT — The final arrangement removes client selection and reward power, but paragraph three locates usable technical knowledge in the engineers. Disclosure and direct reporting do not themselves transfer that understanding.
  (A) missing_link — Reporting routes determine where findings go, not where technical understanding resides. Paragraph three distinguishes possessing records from a usable grasp of accepted methods and equivalencies.
  (C) level_confusion — Technical dependence and appointment control are different mechanisms. The scenario expressly removes the latter; dependence on explanations does not by itself restore selection or reward power.
  (D) causal_inversion — Rotation addresses familiarity but repeatedly requires newcomers to be taught by the inspected party. More frequent rotation therefore does not resolve the knowledge dependence described here.
