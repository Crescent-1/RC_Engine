[PASSAGE]

In the classrooms and locker rooms and open-plan offices where the tribe assembles, one story is told to the young before almost any other. The gifted, they are warned, often falter; the ordinary child who practices past exhaustion, who returns to the failed problem a hundredth time, will in the long accounting overtake her. Effort, sustained, outweighs endowment. Call this belief the grit story, and note first how little dispute it excites: coaches nod, teachers reprint it on posters, the retired executive builds his memoir upon it. The observer who arrives among these people finds a doctrine held with the calm of a thing no longer questioned. I will not question it either. Not yet, and not on the terms they offer.

Watch, though, how the doctrine is defended. The elders cite the same three or four strivers, always the ones who won. The founder who slept in the office. The runner who trained through injury. Where are the sleepless founders who went bankrupt? Uncounted. The studies once invoked were small; they have largely failed to replicate. And the word itself flexes. When a persistent man fails, the ritual redescribes him as merely stubborn. When a lucky man succeeds, grit is discovered in his past. A definition that bends to every outcome explains none. The arguments, examined one by one, do not hold.

Yet the conclusion need not fall with them. Set aside the anecdotes entirely. There remains separate work, gathered by people uninterested in the totem: on how habits consolidate, on the modest predictive weight of delayed gratification, on the ten-thousand-hour grind by which expertise is actually laid down. None of this rescues the poster. It supports something smaller and duller — that sustained practice matters at the margin, some of the time, for some kinds of skill. The grand claim is not vindicated. A quieter cousin of it merely survives, wearing the same name and almost none of the promises.

Here the observer's real puzzle opens. Suppose the modest claim is roughly true. It was not adopted because it was true. It was adopted through cherry-picked heroes and studies that dissolved on inspection, and it was then taught, printed, examined, and built into the promotion rites of institutions on exactly that rotten footing. Does the belief's truth clean the record? One instinct says yes: a conclusion owes nothing to the bad company it kept on the way in. Another says the debt is real — that a tribe which reasons this badly toward a true thing has not earned it, and will hold it carelessly, and will not know when it has drifted past the evidence into the poster again. I cannot settle which instinct is right. What remains, after the best surviving version is stated, is a residue of unearned confidence — the suspicion that the belief is trusted for reasons no one has retired, and that its truth, if it is a truth, is held more tightly than anything about how it was acquired could justify.
