At the edge of the runway, beside a low building where the air-conditioning never quite silences the engines, a foreign military facility is already enough to discredit the state offering a ceasefire channel. Add a large trade account with one combatant, or dependence on a neighbouring capital’s patronage, and the verdict seems available before the delegates arrive. By neutrality, in this usage, we mean not impartial conduct during talks but prior detachment from the parties’ fortunes. The distinction is often lost. A broker with interests is presumed to have chosen a side; access becomes evidence of corruption. The complaint has force because mediation asks parties to expose fears they cannot safely announce elsewhere.

The image behind that complaint belongs, roughly, to the neutral intermediary of nineteenth- and early twentieth-century diplomacy: a sheltered state outside the belligerents’ alliances, receiving a sealed dispatch and carrying it across a political distance that was also, often, a physical one. Governments did not speak continuously; contact was intermittent, formal, and costly. Under those conditions legal non-belligerence could serve as a practical method. Distance kept the messenger from being drawn into the quarrel. It also made the messenger look fair. Neutrality acquired its moral sheen through this arrangement, as a classroom rule acquires authority when the teacher can stand apart from the children’s factions.

But a ceasefire now may depend on permissions no distant messenger possesses: airspace for an evacuation, a bank able to move emergency funds, an intelligence contact who can verify a detention, relatives who will carry assurances into a besieged district, and reconstruction money whose promise changes calculations in the room. A state able to speak privately with enemies may do so because it has traded, armed, hosted, borrowed, guaranteed, or been useful to them. Its connections are not decorations around mediation; they are often the stairway by which mediation reaches the parties. That is too generous — no: more precisely, it mistakes access for innocence. The same stairway can direct pressure unevenly. A flight clearance can be withheld. Credit can be delayed. Information can be rationed. An offer of future aid can make one party’s assent look voluntary while narrowing every alternative. Influence works. Influence binds. Influence may serve its owner. The old demand for detachment therefore clashes with the means by which contemporary intervention becomes possible, without thereby making those means clean.

Seen from this changed ecology, the opening demand for a neutral broker applies an inherited category before examining the work the broker performs. The distant outsider may be free of entanglement and unable to move anyone. The embedded intermediary may possess leverage and exercise it as a regional ambition in diplomatic dress. Calling the latter effective does not certify it; calling the former neutral does not establish trust. The evidence lies in the terms offered, the pressures applied, and the settlement’s aftermath, not in presumed purity. A verdict reached earlier would confuse an old credential with a present judgment. Neutrality may be less a condition than a claim that must be tested.
