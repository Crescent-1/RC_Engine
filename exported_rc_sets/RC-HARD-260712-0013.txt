[PASSAGE]

I confess I came to the triage-scoring literature prepared to distrust it, and I was disarmed. The elegance is real. A severity index converts the shivering chaos of an emergency department into an ordering: assign weights to vitals, to age, to the mechanism of injury, and a queue resolves itself where before there was only the loudest voice or the most sympathetic face. Queuing-theory bed-allocation models go further, treating the ward as a system with arrival rates and service times, and they promise something genuinely worth wanting — that the child who arrives silent will not be outranked by the adult who arrives shouting, that a tired clinician's hunch will not quietly become a prejudice. The instrument does not get bored at 4 a.m.; it does not resent the patient it saw yesterday. Everything I am nostalgic about — the seasoned eye, the bedside instinct — is also everything that produces the inconsistency these systems were built to abolish. I should be honest that my suspicion of the calculation is partly a suspicion of my own displacement. The model solves a problem I would rather pretend does not exist.

And then the nurses override it. Constantly. A score says level three; the nurse moves the patient up. Why? A breathing pattern the index does not sample. A grey cast to the skin. A mother who has gone very quiet. The nurse cannot always name the variable. She says the patient is about to turn. Often she is right, and the chart later confirms it. It would be easy to stop here and declare the craft vindicated, the calculation exposed. Easy, and I want to. But the override is not a verdict against the model. It is a fact the model has to explain, or fail to.

Because the override is not always right, and unaided judgment has catastrophic failure modes of its own — the missed sepsis, the anchoring on a familiar face. Nor is the fix simply to fold the nurse's cues into the next version as new inputs, as if her breathing-pattern hunch were a variable awaiting discovery. The model asks: given these patients, who is sickest? The nurse, in the override, is asking something the model never poses — what counts as sick enough to reorder a whole ward right now. Those are not two answers to one question. They are two questions wearing the same word.

Which leaves me doubting the tools I have been using to adjudicate. "Accuracy," "override," "failure" — each presumes a fixed object being triaged, and it is precisely that object that goes unstable at the moment of the override. The smooth-running system hides what it is sorting; the friction is where the thing being sorted briefly shows its face, and I no longer trust my instruments to say whose face it is.
