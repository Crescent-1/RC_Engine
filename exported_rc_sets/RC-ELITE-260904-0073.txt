A loss recorded is not the same as a loss suffered, and the two rarely balance. The steward's account of the night of the fire at the Rocchetta Mattei survives in correspondence and in a working ledger, and it reads like what it is: the paperwork of a building site. Fire in the unfinished domed hall, after dark. Workmen roused. Water carried from the cistern. The flames confined to the timber scaffolding and the panelling on the eastern arc, and the masonry, being masonry, unharmed. The Count arrived at first light and walked the room. He gave instructions. Labour was shifted to the west wing, replacement timber was ordered, and the campaign resumed within the week. Read the documents in sequence and the fire behaves like a bad debt in an otherwise solvent enterprise — an entry that must be carried forward, paid down over several seasons, and eventually retired without altering the character of the business. A building of that size runs on such entries. Roofs fail. Suppliers default. Money is owed to a mason in Riola and settled late. The archive that records a fire is the same archive that records a delayed shipment of lime, and it records both in the same hand, at the same width of margin, with the same absence of adjectives. That flatness is precisely what makes the account persuasive: it looks like the version that was not composed for anyone.

Not that the rival account is folklore and can be set aside on that ground; rather that it comes to us by a different route and asks to be weighed differently. Later visitors to the Rocchetta, and the local memory they drew on, transmit a Count who stood before the smoking hall in open distress, who said the fire was a judgment on what the room had been intended for, and who sent for his closest medical confidants before the ashes were cold. That version now bears more weight than it once did, because the Rocchetta is visited, restored, and interpreted, and every guide who describes the mosque-hall is choosing a creditor for the night's loss.

Against the general picture of unsentimental continuity, one entry presses hard: the steward's precise tally of scorched cedar panelling ordered for replacement, quantities exact, delivery dated. Composure, here, means brevity in a ledger. It is administrative, not emotional. The record shows a man giving orders promptly; it cannot show what he felt while giving them.

The oral tradition presses its own detail: that the rebuilt dome carried a new astrological motif. Mattei's medicine borrowed its vocabulary from alchemy, which had borrowed from astrology, which had once been surveying the sky for kings; the word travels down through the centuries and arrives on a ceiling in the Apennines, and the tradition holds that the fire is what put it there.

Both accounts agree on one thing. The dome as built departs from the surviving drawings. The ledger reads that departure as a revision in materials. The tradition reads it as a change of mind. The stone cannot say which, and the temptation to make it say is the error — a fact both parties claim is not evidence for either, only the point where their claims touch: the eight-pointed figure over the west arc.
