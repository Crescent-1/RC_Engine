[PASSAGE]

The file is thin: eleven pages, a carbon of a checklist, a hand-drawn plan folded twice, and a signature dated March 1998. What was that signature supposed to certify? Not that the rooming house conformed to a drawing on record, and not that its owner had made certain promises in writing. A fire-safety inspection of an occupied lodging house is meant to certify the building as lived in: this hallway, on this morning, with these doors, these detectors, these people asleep behind them. The unit of certification is the room, not the structure. That distinction turns out to carry most of the weight.

Suppose the inspection consisted of matching the filed plan against the checklist, line by line, as a clerk reconciles two columns. The plan on file showed six units on two floors, with a single stair and a rear egress; the building by 1998 held nine, the extra three having been partitioned out of what the drawing called a parlor and a storage room. Every checklist item could have been marked satisfied against the six-unit drawing and every mark would have been true of a building that no longer existed. Whether those partitions had anything to do with what happened eleven months later is a separate matter, and the fire marshal's report addresses it. A floor plan is not a building.

His deposition, taken the following year, describes the visit in the flat register such documents encourage. He entered by the front door. He walked the second-floor corridor. He pushed on doors to test whether they latched, and he recalls that one stuck. All of this is consistent, and it is also consistent with the six-unit configuration and with the nine-unit configuration alike; a man can walk a corridor and see partition walls without registering them as units, because units are a legal category and walls are only walls. The corridor was walked. What was found and what was assumed produce identical marks on the carbon.

Then there is the affidavit, signed by the owner two weeks earlier, listing smoke detectors installed in each sleeping room and tested within the preceding ninety days. Departmental practice at the time, as the deposition concedes without embarrassment, was to accept such attestations for detector items and to reserve independent testing for cases where something looked wrong. Read quickly, this is one more document in a file of documents, each contributing its portion of confidence. Read slowly, it changes the grammar of the signature: what the inspector certified was not that detectors were present but that a man had said so, which is a claim about the man.

So the definition that survives is the narrow one. An inspection is the inspector's own sensory contact with the current state of each unit — not the drawing, not the corridor walked, not the sworn word of an interested party. Applied honestly to those eleven pages, the definition yields a hard result: the 1998 signature attests to nothing that can be shown to have happened in the nine rooms. The price of keeping it is that the same test, run backward through the department's files, empties most of a decade's passed inspections into the same category. That accounting belongs to auditors, not to historians. The file cannot pay it.
