A quarto notebook catalogued in a county record office as "Commonplace book of Thomas Sowerby, clerk, Manchester, 1849–1871," and the catalogue entry decides more than it appears to. A commonplace book, as the schoolmasters of the seventeenth century meant it and as the Victorians inherited it, is a store: extracts copied under headings, wisdom banked against future need, the compiler's own voice deliberately absent because his voice is not the asset. Much of Sowerby's notebook obliges. Fourteen leaves of hymn verses, transcribed accurately from Watts and Cowper. Proverbs, some in Latin. Two pages of shipping tonnages copied from a newspaper. The genre fits, and the cataloguer, working through eleven boxes of an unaccessioned bequest, paid for the fit with a word that settles the object's worth before anyone has read it.

Set against that: sixty-odd entries carry dates in Sowerby's own hand, the weather is noted on nearly every one of them from 1856 onward — "close, thundery, no rain by six" — and there are first-person sentences that no theory of copied extracts can absorb, of which the plainest is "I could not sleep for the noise of the engines," written twice in the same fortnight and copied from nobody.

The case for the store, though, survives closer reading and is strengthened by it. The extracts are not accumulated; they are arranged. Between March and September of 1861, the months in which Sowerby's wife Ann was ill, the transcribed verses turn almost uniformly to mortality — Cowper on the grave, four separate psalms of affliction, a proverb about the borrowed hour repeated on three occasions with slight variation in spelling. Nothing in the notebook says she was ill; a parish register says it. What the arrangement shows is selection under private pressure, and selection is precisely the operation the genre disallows. A commonplace book is meant to be indifferent to its keeper's month. This one keeps time with his.

The diary side has its own complication. Nineteen of the dated entries have been struck through and written again beneath, and four of those exist in three or four successive versions, each shorter, each with an adjective removed. A man recording his day does not draft it. Something is being composed here, and composed toward a standard the notebook never states.

Three claims, then, each with its pages. The store accounts for perhaps a third of the leaves and cannot explain the weather, the dates, or the engines. The diary accounts for another third and cannot explain fourteen leaves of Watts or the drafting. And the drafting itself supports a third reading that the first two have no room for: that the crossings-out are the ordinary machinery of devotional self-examination, in which a man revises his account of his own week until it is honest, a discipline his chapel would have expected of him weekly. That reading, too, takes a third, and is falsified by the shipping tonnages. This is not a case where the evidence is thin and patience would settle it; the evidence is abundant and settles nothing, because each category is doing real work on real pages and lying about the rest. The cost of saying so is that Sowerby's notebook stays where the cataloguer's word put it, shelved under a heading two-thirds of it refuses.
