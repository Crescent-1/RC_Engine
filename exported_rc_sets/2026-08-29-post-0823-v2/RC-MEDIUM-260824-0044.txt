Let me fix what "Global South" will mean here, since the phrase invites lazy mapping onto latitudes and per-capita income tables. It has never been a compass reading; Australia sits well south of Abu Dhabi and nobody proposes it for the club. It is a bloc — a negotiating habit inherited from the non-aligned years, held together by a shared grievance about who set the rules. On that reckoning the United Arab Emirates is not an absurdity. It was a British protectorate until 1971. Its security still rests on other people's fleets. Its coastline and outdoor workforce sit in the path of heat that will make parts of the Gulf unlivable. When such a state took the presidency of COP28, the awkwardness stopped being a matter of vocabulary and became a matter of who chairs the room.

The objection arrives promptly and it deserves its best day. Sovereign funds measured in the trillions. A hydrocarbon export book that funds the state twice over. Solar capacity built without a cent of concessional finance. Whatever the UAE is short of, it is not capital. Set it beside Malawi in a loss-and-damage discussion and the shared category looks like a costume. Nor does the softer label rescue it: to call the Emirates "developing" implies it is merely at an earlier station on a track the wealthy have already ridden, which is precisely backwards — its problem is not arriving late at prosperity but arriving there by selling the very commodity under negotiation. A petrostate borrowing the moral standing of the exposed, while keeping the gavel, is a fair description of what many delegations saw.

And yet each rival label fails in its own way, which is what I find instructive. "Developing" misdescribes a country with more desalination capacity than it can pay for in carbon terms. "Petrostate" flattens a foreign policy that runs from Emirati-funded transmission lines in East Africa to mediation in Yemen to money in Caribbean adaptation funds. "Donor" flatters, since donors do not spend the conference arguing that historical emissions are somebody else's ledger to settle. Kuwait, Norway, Guyana, Indonesia — each strains the taxonomy differently. What is under strain is not our knowledge of the Emirates; it is the expectation that a category name can decide who is owed what. Legitimacy questions are being smuggled into classification questions because classification is cheaper to argue about, and the categories cannot carry the freight.

So the Global South survives as a working vocabulary. It should. It keeps the table open to states that a poverty-to-riches ladder cannot rank, and it preserves the one claim that unites them, which concerns the origin of the rules rather than the size of the reserves. The bill is legible. Sharp inequalities inside the coalition go quiet. A country with money can speak in the cadence of a country without any, and membership will be mistaken for a mandate to speak for the smallest members — which it never was. That confusion is affordable only while nothing turns on it. It will not stay affordable: the eligibility criteria for the loss-and-damage fund, and the seat allocations that follow, will require somebody to write the boundary down, and once it is written the courtesy that made it usable will be gone.
