Scribes in the second millennium BCE began compiling the birth-anomaly omens that later scholars gathered under the incipit Šumma izbu — "if a malformed newborn." A copied excerpt sits in front of me now, in the flat conditional format the series uses throughout. If the anomaly is thus, then such-and-such follows. Observation, then consequence. The entries are ruled off from one another, one per line, like a tally. The format will look familiar to anyone who has read a Babylonian medical text, where a physician's tablet runs the same way: if the patient's temple throbs and his hand shakes, then he suffers the hand of a particular god. Same grammar. Same clean two-part hinge.

Work both readings out and they seem to run along one track. The physician, having palpated the abdomen or watched the fever's timing, moves from the visible particular in front of him inward, to the hidden condition that produced it, using a correspondence he learned by heart and did not himself devise; the omen-scribe, having inspected the malformed lamb the shepherd brought in, moves from that visible particular outward to a verdict about something entirely elsewhere — the king's campaign, the harvest, the standing of the palace — using a correspondence he also learned by heart and also did not devise. Both begin in the seen. Both end in the unseen. Both trust the list.

The swelling and the fever are two faces of one process. Whatever is wrong inside the patient produces the sign, so the sign carries information about the cause because it descends from the cause: read the symptom backward and you arrive where it came from. The lamb's extra limb has no such descent. It does not cause the enemy's defeat and is not caused by it; nothing links the ewe's uterus and the battlefield in the way the disease links throat to pulse. The parallel format hides a difference in kind. Two clauses joined by a conditional look the same whether or not anything travels between them.

An objection: the scribes plainly believed the gods wrote the verdict into the flesh, so a causal chain does exist, merely with an invisible middle term, and the parallel survives. But the tablet never requires that chain, never displays it, never argues from it. Its format is arranged so that the sign, once matched to its entry, is sufficient to certify the verdict — full stop, no further showing. A diagnostic symptom can never claim that. Fever certifies nothing on its own; the physician must hold that this fever belongs to this disease and not another, which is a claim about necessity, and which is why medical entries multiply qualifications while omen entries do not. Sufficiency has quietly taken necessity's seat.

That swap explains the shape of the series itself. It grows. It does not consolidate. A medical tradition, pressed hard enough, compiles its cases into a nosology, because symptoms that share a cause can be grouped under it — but no omen explains another omen, so each anomaly must be certified separately, line by ruled line, forever. Calling the two practices variants of one inferential habit splits a difference that is not there to split; they are not close cousins, nor wholly alien procedures, and the temptation to say something balanced about them mostly survives because the tablets, ruled and rowed alike, look as though they belong on the same shelf.
