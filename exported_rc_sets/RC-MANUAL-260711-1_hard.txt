[PASSAGE]
A gift must not be a payment, and everyone knows what this requires. The price is peeled off before wrapping. The recipient says it is too much, the giver that it is nothing. A decent interval must pass before anything comes back — too quickly and you insult someone, too slowly and you forget them. Return something of wildly different value and you have said something you did not mean. The anthropologists who counted found what anyone could predict: debts, terms, a rough equivalence maintained over years by people who would be offended to hear it named.

The conclusion nearly draws itself. This is barter that has learned manners. The disavowal — the sticker peeled off, the protest of unworthiness — is the lie that keeps the machinery quiet, caught by the anthropologist in the act.

But consider what sort of lie this would have to be. The recipient knows the sticker was there. The giver knows he knows. Children are taught the interval, taught to protest, corrected when wrong. Nothing here is hidden; every party to it can describe the concealment on request. In a bazaar the opening price is not the price, and both know it, neither deceived; the figure is not a falsehood but a form's first move, and a man who took it literally would not be more honest, only incompetent. The gift's disavowal has that shape. It is not a lie that fails to fool anybody. It is a rule.

Which would settle the matter, except that the rule has a condition it cannot inspect. What it prescribes is that one not reckon; what it can require is only that one not appear to. A person may peel the sticker, protest, wait the correct nine weeks, compute all the way through — and have followed every rule. The gift was supposed to differ from the payment precisely at the reckoning, and the reckoning is the one place the rule cannot go. Nor can anyone else. The observer sees conduct, and conduct is what a perfect performance also produces. The giver's testimony is worth no more, since what she finds when she looks inward was put there by the training: introspection here is not a window onto the rule's success but the rule again, speaking in the first person.

So the question stays open, for a reason worth stating plainly. This is not a shortage of evidence, to be repaired by better fieldwork. There are two vantages from which a gift could be seen, and each is disqualified for this question — the observer by what conduct cannot show him, the participant by having been taught what to feel. My aunt works the sticker off with her thumbnail before wrapping, and if you ask her why she says only that one does not mention it; she could not tell you whether she means she has forgotten what it cost or that the cost is not a thing one says. There is nowhere further to look.
