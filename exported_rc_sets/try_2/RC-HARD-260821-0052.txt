[PASSAGE]

A job advertisement from a Boston analytics firm, posted in 2023, sought a "Prompt Engineer / Content Strategist." The slash is the whole story. Whoever wrote it could not decide which floor of the building the hire belonged on. The role: coax useful output from a language model by writing careful instructions. Engineering claims it because the work is iterative and testable. Marketing claims it because the work is words. Neither claims it at review time. The title itself keeps sliding — engineer one quarter, specialist the next, then simply "AI lead," which names a direction rather than a craft. Job titles usually lag the work by a year or two. This one is not lagging. It is oscillating, and oscillation of that kind is a symptom worth taking seriously before it is explained away.

Programming is the obvious first candidate, and it holds up further than skeptics allow: the prompter writes an input, observes an output, adjusts, versions the result, keeps a file of what worked and what broke, and speaks fluently of edge cases, regressions, and test suites — the vocabulary transfers almost without friction. Call it soft code, then, and the taxonomy is nearly settled. But look at what a compiler does. It rejects. It refuses the malformed line and tells you where. There is no such gate here; every prompt is accepted, and the failures arrive as plausible prose rather than as errors. Syntax is not fixed; determinism is not promised; the same instruction on Tuesday and Thursday can differ. What decides the outcome resembles persuasion more than specification.

So try writing. The prompter addresses something. Sets a tone, establishes constraints, anticipates misreadings. This is rhetoric — audience, occasion, decorum — and one could imagine a canon of technique assembling itself around the practice, the way manuals of style once did. Translation fits too: intent in one idiom, instruction in another. But rhetoric assumes an audience with habits. This audience has none it keeps. It is retrained in April and reads differently in May. A translator between French and Japanese works a pair that stays put for a lifetime; here one side of the pair is replaced quarterly, without notice. Skill accumulated against version four is not skill against version five. It is sediment. Whatever a canon needs, it needs a reader who holds still.

Each trial failed the same way. Programming required a compiler; rhetoric required an audience; translation required a stable pair of languages. Notice what those three exemplars have in common: none is a skill. Each is a fixed thing the skill was measured against. The compiler does not vary; French does not; the classical audience was a fiction precisely because it held still. Our categories for craft are not descriptions of what practitioners do. They are descriptions of what practitioners do it *to*. Inventing a fifth name for prompting would repeat the error, since the new name would need its own fixed correlate and there is none to supply. The useful question is not what to call the prompter. It is which of the objects our older crafts were built around are still sitting where we left them — and how the crafts anchored to them are faring now that some are not.
