In a folder at the Brooklyn Historical Society sits a manila envelope labeled "Blessing Man — Flatbush Ave., 1968–1981," and inside it are forty-one newspaper clippings, most from the same three columnists. He is not alone in the literature. Across a dozen studies from the 1970s onward, folklorists compiling what they call charisma indices — scored tallies drawn from press coverage, photographic frequency, and oral-history mentions — keep finding the same clustering. The costumed subway fixtures, the self-appointed blessing figures, the sidewalk preachers with a signature hat: they score high, and they score high in study after study, across coders trained separately. The convergence is real. The obvious reading is that these people had something, that the instrument found it, and that the scores mean the same thing wherever they appear.

Marguerite Okonjo, who spent nineteen years processing community-collection accessions before she wrote a word about them, put it flatly in a 1994 conference paper: "The index does not detect magnetism. It detects the clipping habit — ours and theirs." Her charge is that the apparatus manufactures its own quarry. I think she overstates it, and I think she is close to right. Not that charisma is fictional; rather that the property the indices measure is inseparable from the accumulation that made measurement possible. What work does the distinction do? Considerable work, once you ask which cases the index can be trusted on.

Consider the pair Okonjo built her paper around. Two Chicago performers, 1958 to 1972, near-identical routines — chalk portraits, a fixed corner, a recited blessing. One kept a shoebox of clippings and staged an annual photo with the alderman. The other kept nothing. On indices compiled in 1983, the shoebox keeper scores nearly four times higher. One mechanism, cleanly shown. Not the only one.

The trouble deepens when you notice that the shoebox was itself a performance. What the Blessing Man chose to save, the origin story he told reporters, which photograph he pressed into a visitor's hand — these were curatorial decisions, made by him, in advance of any folklorist. Fifty years later a graduate student codes that same pile for "self-mythologizing narrative" and "iconic visual repetition," and scores it high. Object and method turn out to be one heap of paper accumulating in two directions. This does not condemn the indices. It tells you exactly what they can be trusted to register, which is not nothing, and exactly which cases — the shoeboxless ones — they silently discount.

So the property cannot be recovered. Charisma, as a trait residing in a person, does not survive extraction from the archive that made it visible. But something durable does survive, and it is not the performer. A collection of forty-one clippings from three columnists is evidence of three columnists, their editors, and the readers who wrote in. A photograph circulated for thirty years is evidence of the people who kept circulating it. I have been reading, this whole time, from a finding aid — the Brooklyn envelope's own two-page inventory, typed on a manual machine, corrections struck over in the same ink. It documents a public that decided, repeatedly, to keep her. That decision has structure, and the structure is measurable. The better question is who mailed in the forty-first clipping, and why, in 1979.
