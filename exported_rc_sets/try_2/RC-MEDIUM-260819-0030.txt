[PASSAGE]

Ask where authenticity comes from and two guilds stake rival claims. One traces it to the Romantic poet, alone with his feeling; the other to the anthropologist in the field, notebook open, racing extinction. Each files for priority. Neither concedes. The dispute has outlasted every generation that inherited it.

I once found the Romantic case irresistible, and I can still feel why. The argument runs inward: what is real in a person lies beneath the crust of manners, and the work of art is the moment that crust cracks. Wordsworth walking the fells, Keats writing that he was certain of nothing but the heart's affections—these were not decorations on the idea but its engine. Convention lied; feeling did not. To be authentic was to refuse the borrowed script and speak from the one place society had not yet colonized. For a long stretch of my apprenticeship I took that place to be self-evidently the source, and the poets its first cartographers.

Then the other guild made its case, and for a while it seemed the stronger one. The anthropologists relocated authenticity outward—not in the solitary breast but in the shared life of a people: the ritual, the kinship term, the harvest song passed down before the trade ships came. Boas assembling his enormous Kwakwaka'wakw texts, Malinowski among the Trobrianders, treated a culture as something that could be lost the way a manuscript burns. Here authenticity wore the dignity of the endangered. It was not a mood to be expressed but a record to be defended against the leveling machinery of empire, and defense, plainly, is a graver duty than self-discovery. The stakes felt higher. The clock was running. Against that urgency the Romantic seemed almost indulgent—a man admiring his own weather.

I no longer think either guild wins, because I no longer think they are fighting over what they say they are. Both assume authenticity is an essence lying in wait—to be uttered, or to be catalogued—and it was the sameness of that assumption, not the difference in their objects, that finally stopped me. The poet's introspection did not report a prior self; it manufactured one, in the very act of writing it down. The ethnographer's field notes did not preserve an untouched culture; the notes helped decide, retroactively, which strand would count as the real one, purified of whatever contact had already reshaped it. In both rooms the thing was being made in the moment of its supposed recovery. So the priority quarrel—who found authenticity first—rests on a premise that dissolves the moment you look hard at the record. The better question is not who owns the essence but why so many practices, poetic and scientific alike, are organized to produce the effect of an essence: what work does that effect do, for whom, and at whose expense? That is not a matter literary history can settle, nor ethnography either; it asks how communities transact in the currency of the real. Perhaps it belongs to a philosophy of value—if any discipline is competent to ask why we needed the essence to have been there all along.
