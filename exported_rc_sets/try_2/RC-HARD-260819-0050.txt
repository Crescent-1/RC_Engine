[PASSAGE]

In 1948, working out of Bell Labs, Claude Shannon set down what a signal is. Not a metaphor. A quantity. A signal was whatever passed through a channel and could be measured against the static that also passed through it. Noise had a number. The signal-to-noise ratio was that number made into a fraction. Everything was bounded: a sender, a receiver, a channel with finite capacity. Nothing floated free. You could compute the thing or you could not, and if you could not, you were not yet talking about signals in any sense Shannon would have recognized.

The word did not stay in the channel. Cybernetics carried it into the study of feedback and control; from there it drifted into economics, where a diploma became a signal of unobserved ability, and into sociology, where almost any gesture could be read as one. By the time it reached the columnists and the dating manuals, the mathematics had fallen away entirely. People spoke of sending a signal, of separating signal from noise in a news cycle, without any channel in view and no ratio anyone could name. Granted, the popular sense keeps none of Shannon's arithmetic — and yet it holds onto the one discipline that mattered underneath the arithmetic, the habit of asking what, in a given wash of input, actually carries information, and what is merely the clutter that arrives alongside it. That question survives the loss of its equations.

Consider the harder cases. A man reads his partner's curt reply and announces that it is a signal — of what, he is certain, though he could not say which channel he means or how curtness on a bad day differs from curtness that means something. Or the analyst who watches a stock tick upward and calls it a strong signal, a strong buy, with no denominator anywhere, no noise estimated, no baseline against which strength could be judged. The vocabulary is technical. The procedure is a hunch. What looks like measurement is closer to an intuition wearing a borrowed coat.

It would be easy to stop there, with the coat and the hunch, and call the whole thing empty borrowing. I don't think that is quite right. Even the analyst with no denominator has been made to do one thing by the word he chose: he has committed himself to the claim that the tick is a pattern and not a coincidence. That is a distinction, and holding it costs something. To call an event a signal is to promise, however loosely, that it would recur, that it points beyond itself. Most talk asks for no such promise. So the popular usage does perform labor — thinner than Shannon's, unmeasured, often self-flattering, but not nothing. To close the account now, before that survival is weighed, would be to mistake the loss of the mathematics for the loss of the work. Who is to blame for the drift hardly matters; the word passed through too many hands to pin. What matters is that something small and real crossed over intact, worn down but still asking the one question it was built to ask, if we are patient enough to hear it doing so.
