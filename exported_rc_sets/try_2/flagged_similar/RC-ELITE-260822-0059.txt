Public provision resembles a road: what matters first is whether needed goods arrive. Its surface may be rough and its milestones officious, yet a child fed at journey’s end is not an abstraction. G. F. McCleary’s defence of infants’ milk depots amounts to a precise objection: attention to paperwork mistakes the apparatus for its purpose, since safe milk, not administrative innocence, was what sick infants required. In the twentieth century’s first decade, after recurring summer reports of infant diarrhoea, a municipal health office opened small depots in crowded districts. Measured milk waited in boiled, stoppered bottles. Numbered tickets governed collection; fixed morning hours governed families. A nurse slid an iron weight along the scale and explained feeding. Mothers brought address cards and returned empties. The road had rules because bottles spoiled, stocks ran short, and doses differed by age. The department called the milk “free.”

Queues formed early. Names became familiar. Absence acquired detail. Depot keepers knew who missed a collection, changed lodgings, or sent an older child. “Free,” incidentally, meant that no coins changed hands, not no time, documents, or explanation; feeding an infant also clarified a household to the office. Reminder slips travelled through schoolteachers.

Annual reports listed rising distributions and falling deaths in wards nearest the depots. McCleary was right: these were not clerical trifles. Milk arrived. Infants drank. Some survived the summer. Mothers petitioned for longer hours and more stations, so the service mattered beyond each queue: households planned around it, and the municipality had to describe whom it served. It repeatedly called the provision “public.” The word passed unnoticed. Yet it meant municipally maintained, not equally accessible. Unmarried mothers were routed to a separate desk; recent arrivals had to establish residence; families beyond the catchment were redirected or turned away. The city had enlarged care—or, no: more precisely, it had enlarged one managed channel of care. This was not inspection disguised as feeding; rather, it was material assistance whose eligibility rules determined which forms of care could be received without a family entering an official record.

During wartime shortage, neighbours exchanged bottles, ration cards were borrowed, and nurses began recording condensed milk, barley water, and other substitutes for cow’s milk. The logs grew fuller at the same moment that the actual paths from depot shelf to children’s mouths became harder to follow.

As commercial pasteurisation spread and child-welfare clinics expanded their weighing, advice, and distribution, the depots closed one by one. Their ledgers went to an archive, retaining quantities issued, weights taken, and named absences; headings for “attendance” stood beside distribution totals, fixing the programme’s reach in orderly columns. McCleary’s defence holds where bottles can be followed: paperwork helped keep food safe and regular. Yet the files contain few accounts of refused applicants, tickets exchanged between neighbours, or unrecorded meals. We might console ourselves that such omissions merely fringe a successful service, or that the surviving names prove its administrative appetite. Neither comfort is recorded. Set beside those missing routes, the preserved figures establish provision without establishing its reach, and inspection without establishing its chief effect. Given those mismatched remains, the surviving record cannot decide whether the depots chiefly nourished families or made them administratively legible.
