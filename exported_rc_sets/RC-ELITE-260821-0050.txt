[PASSAGE]

The story always has the same shape. A grandmother saw him across a church hall in 1961 and told her sister that night. Three weeks to the engagement, fifty-four years to the funeral. Every family keeps one of these. The aunt who married a man she'd known nine days; the cousin who broke off a sensible engagement to a dentist because something in her chest said no, and who now has four children with the man she met the following spring. Against this: the friend who has been on the apps for six years, filtering by height, politics, smoking status, desired number of children, and who has learned to conduct a first date like a hiring panel. She is exhausted. She is also single. The folklore draws the obvious conclusion. Chemistry does not lie. The body knows something the questionnaire cannot ask about, some fast subterranean reading of gait and smell and timing, and the checklist people are simply deaf to it. They are trying to hear a chord by reading the score. And the evidence, such as it is, arrives ready-made in anecdote: the ones who just knew are still married, and the ones who audited are still auditing.

Two claims are being sold here as one. The first is that choosing on feeling tends to go well. The second is that it goes well because feeling is a scanner — an instrument tuned by evolution to detect a particular person's suitability. Only the first has anything going for it. The advice can be sound while every reason anyone gives for it is false, which is a more common arrangement than the people giving reasons would like.

And the first claim does hold up tolerably well. People who report choosing on conviction rather than criteria are not, in the aggregate, worse off ten years on; on several measures they do slightly better. Whatever the mystical machinery underneath, the outcome column is not embarrassing. That much survives the audit, and it is worth keeping.

Who pays for the survival, though, and what account is it drawn on? Three candidates present themselves, none flattering to instinct. Commitment: the woman who felt certain has staked more on being right and works harder to make it so — the outcome is bought, not detected. Narrative: satisfied spouses reconstruct their courtship, and the certainty they remember at the church hall in 1961 may be a satisfaction from 1994 filed under an earlier date. Filtering: the ones who felt certain and were wrong left quietly and are not in the sample. Any of the three would produce the correlation without instinct doing any perceptual work at all. The trouble is that the evidence does not choose among them, and cannot yet — the studies that would separate commitment effects from detection effects would need decades and a control group nobody will volunteer for. So the belief stands and its mechanism does not, and there is no honest way to say how much confidence should transfer. Grandmothers have always said you'll know it when you feel it, and the sentence is still worth repeating, provided one hears knowing as a debt to be paid rather than a fact received.
