[PASSAGE]

A woman is wheeled through the double doors of an operating suite at seven in the morning, already unconscious, and somewhere near her sternum a hand will hold a scalpel. Whose? She has a plain claim: the steadiest, most practiced hand in the building, the one that has done this hundreds of times. Call this the patient's demand, and notice how naturally it translates into a definition. To a first approximation, informed consent means telling her who will cut, and how many times that person has cut before. Disclose the operator. Name the hand. On this reading, the whole ethical problem reduces to honesty about a fact already known upstairs.

But that most practiced hand was, once, the least practiced in some other room. Every attending surgeon began as a resident whose first incision on a living person left a faint tremor she had to overrule. Skill is not issued. It is grown, slowly, on bodies. Take away the training ground of actual patients and the profession stops replenishing itself. Future patients then inherit a hospital staffed by hands that never learned. The demand here is not the resident's convenience. It is competence itself, which must be manufactured somewhere, on someone, or it ceases to exist.

So the hospital owes two things it cannot both pay. Maximal skill now and the making of skill for later pull against each other in the same room. Simulation labs, graduated supervision, the attending's hovering glove — these narrow the gap; none closes it. The bind is built into the walls, not into anyone's failure to speak.

It would be comfortable to stop there, calling the thing tragic and structural and therefore no one's verdict to render. But return to that first, generous definition of consent — disclosure of who holds the scalpel — and look at what it quietly settled. To define the patient's protection as a fact about the operator is to treat her as the only party with a stake in the outcome. The room contains at least two futures being financed: hers, and the next patient's, whose surgeon is being built tonight. A definition that counts only one of them has not stayed neutral. It has already decided, before any argument, who is permitted to be the sole injured party.

Follow the money, then, though it is not money. Patients at teaching hospitals absorb a risk that is statistically real and individually invisible — a slightly worse odds no single person can point to as the cause of her own scar. Residents absorb something else: the shame and dry-mouthed fear of learning in public, on a body that consented to a surgery but never to being practice. The institution absorbs neither. It banks the good outcomes as care delivered and the trained surgeons as teaching accomplished, and hands the two costs to the two people least able to see the other's. The old truism holds — someone has to learn on someone — but it no longer sounds like resignation. It names, exactly, the two who pay so a third can take the credit for both.
