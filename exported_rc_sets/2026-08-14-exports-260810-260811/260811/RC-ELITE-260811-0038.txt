[PASSAGE]

In the parish of Nether Wick, through the wet autumn of 1918, a sexton named Coombes climbed the tower each morning and pulled the great tenor bell once for every villager the influenza had taken in the night, so that the whole valley — the men in the flint-quarry, the women scalding milk pails, the schoolmaster keeping his register — could stand still and count. Three tolls meant three fresh graves. Nine meant a very bad night indeed. The parish had no telephone and one overworked doctor, and the bell did the work a bulletin board would later do: it told you, before you had washed, exactly how much the sickness had cost while you slept, and whom you might, by the arithmetic, expect to have lost.

Then the arithmetic broke. Deaths ran to a dozen a week, then two dozen. The counting went ragged. A woman would lose the thread at eleven and simply decide it had been ten. Some counted low on purpose. Others, half asleep, heard the wind knock the bell and added a phantom to the sum. By November nobody in Nether Wick could have told you the week's true figure from the tower alone. The instrument that had measured the disaster now blurred it. As information, the bell had failed. If you wanted a number you went to the sexton's slate, not to your own ear at the window.

So why keep ringing? A bell that no longer counts, that only announces horror without delivering fact, looks like cruelty dressed as duty — noise for its own sake, a wound reopened each dawn. When the district officer, fearing for what he called morale, ordered Coombes to stop, the objection seemed unanswerable. Yet the village would not have it. They petitioned, crowded the vestry, threatened to ring it themselves. Read quickly, that looks like a community clutching a comfort it could not bear to release, denial with a rope in its hands. It was the opposite. What they were defending had never depended on the count. The bell told them they were still a place that marked its dead together, and that assurance did not need to be accurate to be true.

Here is what the vestry records make plain, and what the officer never grasped. Weeks before his order, Coombes had quietly stopped tolling per death. He rang once, each evening, for the whole day — one stroke whether the day had taken one soul or eleven. Nobody had announced the change. Nobody had noticed. The village had already converted its bell from a counter into a nightly full stop, a shared drawing of breath, and precisely because it no longer measured the catastrophe it could not be crushed by the catastrophe's size. That is why it survived the worst weeks unbroken. Whether every community under long siege performs this quiet substitution — trading the instrument that informs for the one that endures — I cannot say from a single tower; but Nether Wick shows that when a ritual outlives its facts, it is often because it was doing the harder job all along.
