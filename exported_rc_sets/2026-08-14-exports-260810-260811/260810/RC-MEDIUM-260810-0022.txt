[PASSAGE]

A cardiologist writes a statin prescription for a man of fifty-five whose arteries have so far done nothing wrong. An insurer files it under prevention; a health ministry counts it toward a target; a pharmacist hands over ninety tablets. The word does an enormous amount of work — it moves money, sorts budgets, justifies decades of daily swallowing — and nobody stops to ask what it claims. It claims a counterfactual. It says something *would have* happened. The efficacy of the pill is not in doubt; the trials are large and the arithmetic is honest. What is in doubt is the tense.

Where the French hygienists of the 1840s spoke of *prophylaxie* and the German sanitarians of *Vorbeugung*, the shared image was the same: a chain already rattling, and a hand thrust into it. Cholera moved along a pump handle. Snow removed the handle. Quarantine did not reduce the probability of plague reaching a port; it interrupted a journey that was underway. When the tuberculosis dispensaries and the polio campaigns inherited the vocabulary, the grammar held — an organism was present, or on its way, and the intervention stood between it and a body. This is not a story of lost virtue. Those campaigns were coercive, often crude, and frequently wrong about mechanism. What they possessed was not moral clarity but a particular kind of temporal object: a future that could plausibly be described in the indicative. Prevention meant stopping something. The word was accurate to its era and travelled well within it, the way a trade term stays legible along one route and loses its referent on another.

The route has changed. Where a nineteenth-century inspector could point to a specific cistern and say what would issue from it, contemporary preventive medicine works on distributions — elevated risk, hazard ratios, the shape of a curve across ten thousand people — such that when a clinician tells a hypertensive patient that untreated pressure will cause a stroke, the sentence is true of the cohort and false, four times out of five, of the person hearing it. That gap is not a translation error awaiting a better word. Substituting *risk-reducing* fixes the label and leaves everything else standing.

Because the trouble was never that patients are told a certainty where only a probability exists; clinicians hedge constantly, and consent forms are thick with percentages. The epistemology is more or less on display. What the inherited word governs is something else — how long the future stays open. Where the Ayurvedic tradition organizes *rasayana* around the maintenance of a body over decades, and where the sanitarian organized his effort around a fortnight's interruption, modern pharmacological prevention borrows the sanitarian's verb for the Ayurvedic timescale. Thirty years of daily dosing is not an interruption of anything; it is a mode of living, and it is being described with a word designed for an intercepted arrival. The side effect ceases to be a cost weighed against an event and becomes a permanent condition weighed against a permanently deferred one. Whether that trade is worth making is not settled by noticing the mismatch — and the mismatch, once noticed, is not a thing the vocabulary can be adjusted to repair, because what the vocabulary got wrong is not the odds but the duration it silently assigns to them.
