[PASSAGE]

The word *pain* arrives in English through Old French *peine*, and beneath that lies Latin *poena*: penalty, the price paid to a court or a god. Only later, by the fourteenth century, does the word drift toward mere bodily sensation, shedding its juridical layer the way a coin loses its inscription in circulation. That older stratum matters, because the way medicine now speaks of chronic pain has restored something of the earlier sense while pretending not to: a levy exacted on populations across the developed world, rising through the 1990s and 2000s in every registry that bothered to count, described in the literature as an epidemic of nervous systems turning on their owners, a betrayal from within the tissue, an emergency answerable — so the framing went — chiefly by chemistry.

The counts are not in dispute. Roughly a fifth of adults in wealthy countries report persistent pain; prescription volumes for opioids rose severalfold before the retrenchment; disability claims citing back and neck pain outpaced nearly every other category. Something has genuinely increased. That much is settled ground.

What is not settled is what increased. Consider the possibility that fewer bodies broke and more bodies learned to say so — and, further, that saying so was correct. A nervous system is a threat-appraisal instrument, and appraisal is trained. Shift schedules, the fragmentation of attention into ninety-second intervals, the demand that a worker be continuously improvable: these do not merely stress tissue, they instruct the interpreting apparatus in what counts as danger. Pain then becomes less a malfunction than an accurate reading of unsustainable conditions. This is not an argument that pain is good, or that suffering ennobles; a fire alarm is not a virtue. It is an argument that the alarm may be well-calibrated, and that silencing a well-calibrated alarm is a peculiar sort of medicine.

The distribution supports it. Chronic pain concentrates among warehouse pickers, agency nurses, migrant labourers on rolling contracts — populations whose imaging findings are frequently unremarkable and whose degenerative markers are no worse than the comfortable. Pain tracks contractual uncertainty more tightly than it tracks cartilage. Which does not make the cartilage, or the nociceptors, irrelevant.

So both halves stand, and joining them is the work. Pain is real, physiologically instantiated, mediated by receptors and central sensitisation that deserve every hour of laboratory attention they receive; and pain is a statement, in the older *poena* register, about what is being exacted. Medicine's error was never that it took pain seriously. It was the assumption that a sensation with a somatic address must have a somatic cause, which allowed the question of sustainability to be answered by prescription. The verdict, then, reverses the lament: the proliferation of diagnoses is not the pathology. The refusal to hear what the diagnoses are reporting is. Each side still owes something — the neurobiologists a mechanism that explains why identical tissue hurts differently under different contracts; the social epidemiologists a reason why some people under identical contracts do not hurt at all; and the institutions that commission both a plainer account of what they are asking bodies to absorb, though I suspect that last debt will go on being deferred, as such debts generally are.
