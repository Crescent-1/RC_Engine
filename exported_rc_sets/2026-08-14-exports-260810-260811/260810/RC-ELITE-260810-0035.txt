[PASSAGE]

An elephant matriarch in Amboseli turns aside from a straight path to the water and stands over the weathered jaw of a female who died two seasons before, touching it with the underside of her trunk; a chimpanzee mother in the Bossou hills carries her infant's mummified body across her shoulders for two months, grooming what the flies have left; crows gather in the branches above a dead crow and produce a noise that field notes from the 1970s recorded, with admirable restraint, as "assembly." Such cases are offered, at conferences and in the popular press alike, as though their evidential burden were self-evident, and the question they raise appears correspondingly simple: is this grief, properly so called, or merely the machinery of attachment running on after its object has been withdrawn?

The observations, one should note, are not in dispute. What is remarkable is how completely two bodies of researchers can share a data set and part company at its interpretation. The first group treats the corpse-carrying, the vigils, the refusal of food as a family of behaviours whose most economical explanation is felt loss, and charges its opponents with a squeamishness dressed up as rigour. The second declines to infer any interior at all, describing instead the disruption of a habituated routine, and accuses the first of importing a Victorian sentimentality into the savannah. Each side's papers are careful, well-controlled, admirably footnoted. Neither has noticed that the word under contest is not one word.

For one camp, grief names something felt from the inside — an experienced absence, a subject to whom the loss occurs — and such a thing is, by construction, not visible to a spotting scope. For the other, grief is what is defined by a protocol: measurable behavioural change following the death of a bonded conspecific, ideally with cortisol figures appended. The first definition renders the claim unverifiable; the second renders it nearly automatic, since almost any social animal alters its routine when a companion stops appearing. The two camps have been answering different questions with a shared vocabulary, and the sensible response would appear to be a shrug: a quarrel about usage, tidied by stipulation.

That response would be wrong, and not by a narrow margin. The definitions are not interchangeable labels but the visible edges of two rival accounts of what an emotion is for the purposes of science — one holding that emotions are inner states which behaviour imperfectly indicates, the other that they are functional patterns of which the inner state is an optional ornament. Stipulating a definition does not adjudicate between these; it conceals that adjudication has been declined. Nor is this a dispute about elephants, exactly. What the field has been arguing about, under cover of the animals, is the reach of comparative psychology itself: whether its instruments were ever built to license claims about experience, or only about function wearing experience's name. So the question worth putting is not whether animals grieve but what evidence could in principle discriminate between an inner-state and a functional reading of any bereaved creature's conduct — and here the awkwardness is that no such criterion has been fixed for the human case either, where the widow's altered routine and the widow's felt absence have never been prised apart by anything but her testimony, which the elephant does not offer and the crow declines to give.
