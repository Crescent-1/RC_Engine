What does a single stroke of ink through a written bequest actually record? I have in front of me a photocopy of one page from a probate archive: a draft will, the clause typed, a house on a named street left to a named nephew, and running across it one diagonal line in a darker ink, with two initials in the right margin. The draft carries a date stamp. The stroke does not. No codicil explains it. No witness signed beside it. And so the mark says, with equal plausibility, two flatly opposed things: that the house was never given, the clause abandoned before the will was signed; or that the house was given, formally, and then taken back. Probate practice tends to treat these as the same outcome — the nephew gets nothing either way — but they are not the same event, and they are certainly not the same evidence. One is a draft correction. The other is a legal act.

The distinction is well worn in the reported cases. A strike made before execution is inert; the signed instrument simply never contained the clause. A strike made after execution, in the right form, with the right intention, is revocation, and courts have long been willing to look for that intention. Timing has been reconstructed from ink tone, from the memory of a solicitor's clerk, from the order of pages in a file. The techniques are real, and they work often enough that jurisdictions proceed as though the document itself, properly read, settles the matter.

This page is where that assumption gives out. A deletion mark carries no timestamp relative to execution — its silence is not an absence in the record but the thing producing the ambiguity — and the problem is spreading, since estates now pass through drafts corrected in software and drafts corrected in ink, sometimes both, with strike-throughs surviving into printed copies that were never meant to be signed. Suppose two testators, each with an identical typed clause. One draws the line the night before the signing; the other, the night after, alone, in the same ink, initialling the margin exactly as her solicitor once showed her. The pages are indistinguishable. The legal consequences are not.

The reason lies in what such instruments are for. A will is built to record a final state — who takes what, when the testator is gone — and not the sequence of decisions that produced it. It is a photograph, not a logbook. Ask it to prove that a change occurred and it answers readily: here is the stroke, here are the initials. Ask it to place that change before or after the moment of execution and it has no vocabulary at all. The mark is evidence of an act. It is not evidence of the act's position in time, and nothing in the form was ever designed to supply that.

So the burden moves. It should not rest on whoever disputes the strike. It rests on the process that accepts it. A mark that cannot be dated is being asked to carry a meaning it cannot certify, and until someone can say when the pen touched the page, the confident reading of that stroke is a decision the archive has made rather than one the document supports.
