[PASSAGE]

Consider the word "resilience." In ecology, where the term acquired its first precise usage, it named something narrow and measurable: the rate at which a perturbed system returns to a prior equilibrium. C.S. Holling, writing in 1973, drew the distinction sharply. There was the speed of return — engineering resilience, he called it — and there was a second property, the amount of disturbance a system could absorb before it flipped into a wholly different configuration. Both were quantifiable, at least in principle. Both required a specified reference state and a specified perturbation. Strip away either and the number dissolves. Resilience, on this account, is not a virtue a system possesses. It is a relation between a system, a shock of stated magnitude, and a defined outcome. No shock, no measure. No baseline, no meaning.

What survives migration is rarely the definition. It is the aura — and the aura, in the studied cases, travels precisely because it has been cut loose from the constraints that once gave it teeth.

Trace the term's departure from the discipline. By the 1990s it appears in psychology, where the reference state is a developmental norm and the perturbation is trauma; the measurement holds, if loosely. By the 2000s it is in urban planning, in corporate strategy, in the vocabulary of central banks. At each step something is left behind. First the specified perturbation goes — resilience becomes a capacity held against shocks unnamed. Then the baseline goes — for what state, exactly, is the resilient city supposed to recover? Then, in the most attenuated usage, the notion of recovery itself dissolves into mere endurance. A resilient person, we are now told, simply keeps going. The relation has collapsed into a trait.

That collapsed version is, by the standards of its origin, a corruption — though whether corruption is the right instrument for reading it depends on what we take the concept to be doing once it circulates, which is not obviously the work it was built to do.

Here the audit turns on itself. If the migrated term no longer measures anything, we might expect it to do no work; the evidence suggests otherwise. In the studied deployments — grant applications, therapeutic self-descriptions, municipal white papers — the degraded "resilience" functions less as a measurement than as a license, a way of naming a stance toward the unspecified future without committing to any baseline one might be held to. That is not nothing. It may even be the point. The uncomfortable possibility is that the technical version and the folk version were never rivals for the same job: one calibrates, the other consoles. Whether the consoling version thereby earns its keep, or merely borrows the credibility of the calibrated one, is a question the epistemics of concept-drift cannot settle alone — and here the historians of scientific language, who have watched such borrowings before, are the better-instrumented witnesses.
