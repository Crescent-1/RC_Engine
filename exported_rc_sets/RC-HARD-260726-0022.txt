[PASSAGE]

A chair-maker in Windsor once told a visiting journalist that he had never in forty years explained how to sharpen a spokeshave. He showed. The boy watched. The boy got it wrong, and got it wrong again, and then one afternoon did not get it wrong. This story, or one of its cousins, appears in nearly every book on traditional craft published in the last thirty years. The praise is always the same. Words flatten what the hands know. A description of the angle is not the angle. Craft knowledge lives in the wrist, in the eye's judgment of grain, in ten thousand corrections too fine to name. Instruction manuals teach you to read manuals. Only the shop teaches the work. It is an appealing claim, and most of us have half-believed it.

But the shops in question were noisy places, and what filled the noise was talk — not instruction exactly, not lecture, but the running verbal weather of eight or ten people working within earshot of one another: the older journeyman muttering that a joint had been forced, the nickname attached to a particular kind of tearout, the argument about whether a customer's chair had been badly made or badly used, the tools called by names that encoded what they were for and how they went wrong. None of this was teaching in the sense the manuals mean. All of it was commentary. It sorted the master's continuous motion into distinctions a beginner could actually see, and it came from the peers, not the master.

So the two lines meet here. Imitation cannot work unless the learner already knows which features of the demonstration matter, and that knowledge came from the surrounding chorus, which means tacit transmission was never an alternative to language but a division of labour with it. Now consider what has thinned. Not the master's skill; there are excellent makers. Not the availability of demonstration; there is more footage of hands than any apprentice could watch. What has gone is the room with eight people in it. A maker working alone, or a student in a two-week intensive, or an apprentice paired with a single teacher, may see everything and still have no one to tell them what they are seeing. Video changes nothing here. A workshop of forty solitary viewers is still forty solitary viewers.

The craft schools now reviving apprenticeship have understood the visible half. They restage the demonstration carefully, insist on silence, ration explanation, treat the manual as a fallen thing. Some of them are very good. But the arrangement they have recovered was one half of a pair, and the other half was never designed, never written down, and therefore never noticed as something requiring recovery. Whether it can be rebuilt deliberately is genuinely open; peer commentary of that density arose from economic conditions — many hands, long tenures, shared piecework — that no school controls. Until such groupings exist again somewhere, and they may, what the revival transmits is the gesture of apprenticeship rather than its mechanism, and we should not comfort ourselves that the gesture will suffice.
