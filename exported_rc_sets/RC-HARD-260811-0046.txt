[PASSAGE]

The steel box that Malcom McLean loaded onto the tanker Ideal X in 1956 held thirty-three tons of cargo and required almost none of the men who had once carried it piece by piece. Within a decade the arithmetic was settled: a gang of longshoremen that had worked a ship for days could be replaced by a crane and a handful of operators. The union hall on the waterfront, which had rationed access to the hook and controlled the pace of every hold, found the work itself dissolving beneath it. The technology arrived, the labor vanished, and the unions that had lived on that labor followed it down.

The archive complicates the sequence at nearly every point. The International Longshoremen's and Warehousemen's Union signed its Mechanization and Modernization Agreement in 1960, trading work rules for a fund that paid men to leave — a bargain a union already losing leverage makes, not one dictating terms. On the Atlantic coast the ILA had watched its guaranteed gang sizes shrink through the late 1950s, before any single box dimension existed to blame. And there was no single box. Through the 1960s McLean's twenty-four-foot Sea-Land units competed with Matson's rival lengths and with dimensions favored by Grace Line, each shipping firm defending its own castings and cranes. Standardization was not a technical starting gun. It was an outcome still years away while the union's grip on the hold was already loosening pier by pier, contract by contract, some ports faster than others.

So set the two changes side by side in the same years. The ISO dimensions were fixed only after a long committee war, running from roughly 1958 to 1970, in which each carrier fought for its own module. A union strong enough to tie particular box sizes to particular manning rules would have kept several modules alive; varied work rules require varied cargo. Weakened, it could enforce no such coupling. The single dimension won because resistance had thinned. And the thinning of resistance was itself the erasure of the work that varied sizes had once justified. Each advance cleared ground for the other in the same span of months. The box did not precede the union's decline, nor did the decline simply summon one box. The two are one process, read from opposite ends. Neither arrow points; both are the same line seen twice.

This holds for a narrow object only. It cannot be traced pier by pier with the records that survive, and it does not license a general theory in which every industrial standard is really a labor settlement wearing technical clothes. For container-size convergence and dockworker bargaining power between 1956 and 1970, on the coasts where the contracts can be read, the sequence collapses into simultaneity and the causal question loses its footing. Elsewhere the textbook order may stand untouched. The suspension is not a refusal to decide; it is what the evidence in this one case, held to its own edges, will support and no more, provided the records are read as closely as the cranes once were counted.
