[PASSAGE]

A catalogue category is a claim about what an item is, not merely an address at which it has been filed. Its rule is exclusive assignment: each item receives a medium and a cultural function. The Golden Record, assembled under Carl Sagan’s committee and carried aboard both Voyagers in 1977, divides terrestrial life into images, greetings, music and sounds. Photographs show; greetings address; music performs; sounds present audible activity. No continuous narration connects them. Yet within Sounds of Earth lies a Morse rendering of “Per aspera ad astra,” the Latin sentence conventionally translated as “through hardships to the stars.” Greetings would seem its natural destination. But its pulses remain technological sound while conveying language through a signal code. Its unsettled status constitutes the catalogue’s hidden addressee.

The division now extends beyond the sequence cut into the gold-plated disc. Across NASA webpages, streaming releases, museum displays and classroom editions, its branches become tabs, track labels and routes of discovery. A heading determines where a search arrives. What could operate aboard the spacecraft as a convenience of sequence acquires authority over contemporary listening: the same pulses become available as an earthly noise before their verbal content is encountered.

NASA’s published contents list places “Morse Code” under “The Sounds of Earth.” That placement, joined to the sentence’s linguistic content, proves the exception to exclusive assignment. Sagan’s description of the mission as launching a “bottle” into the cosmic “ocean” supplies an explanation, not that proof. A compact Earth has been prepared for an indefinitely distant reception; tidy compartments make the offering seem complete. The compression is severe. Whale song enters beside environmental noise. Human brainwaves, converted into sound, occupy a route between bodily activity and audible representation. A Navajo night chant is filed as music. Blind Willie Johnson’s wordless “Dark Was the Night, Cold Was the Ground” carries vocal expression without a spoken greeting. These crossings recur inside the selection; the Morse sentence is not an isolated filing accident.

The arrangement resembles a customs declaration, with Earth presented as luggage and each article entered in one box. During inspection, a sound becomes a sentence; a bodily trace becomes a performance. The declaration secures passage by holding those identities still. Its hidden addressee is the human auditor requiring a clean inventory before imagining extraterrestrial reception. The boxes describe an itinerary of attention, rather than stable properties of their contents.

Exclusive classification thus imposes boundaries that the material repeatedly crosses, but the Record’s physical encoding and its editorial divisions do not have equivalent tasks. On the pictorial decoding cover, binary instructions must discriminate reliably enough for playback; an uncertain numerical value can prevent the recovery of an image. Curatorial labels have no corresponding requirement to make chant, speech, signal and music parallel, mutually exclusive kinds. Their overlap does not corrupt the recording. For this Voyager catalogue, overlapping descriptions and the original sequence are necessary evidence: one preserves the sentence’s several operations, the other its placement among earthly sounds. That correction carries its price in less elegant menus, duplicated entries and the loss of the lucid four-part Earth through which this particular selection was prepared for outward transmission.
