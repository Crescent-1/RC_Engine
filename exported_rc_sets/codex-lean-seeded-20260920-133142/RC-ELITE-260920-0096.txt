Whether a defendant’s self-interested autobiography can be her biographer’s best document depends initially on a rule that seems to exclude it: testimony nearest the accused is ordinarily least trustworthy where her interests are engaged. Mary Carleton’s The Case of Madam Mary Carleton, published in London in 1663 amid her bigamy trial, puts unusual pressure on that rule. Her claimed noble origins, travels and persecution arrive with the assurance missing from much hostile reporting. Even an unverifiable incident has its appointed place. Foreignness, displayed through cultivated speech and courtship, acquires a consistency that invites belief, though consistency alone cannot establish where she was born or whom she married.

Her husband John’s rebuttal offers accusations against her assertions. Both publications seek a verdict beyond the courtroom. Both have an interest in discrediting the other. Yet setting charge beside counterclaim misses what her ordered life accomplishes. Claimed birth establishes rank; travel accounts for displacement; letters sustain distant connections; jewels make wealth portable and visible. Speech and restrained conduct then let the woman before her hosts appear continuous with the absent noble family. She need not produce that family. Granted that none of this proves noble descent or innocence, her pamphlet nevertheless deserves treatment as the most exact surviving biography of her achieved credibility: its repetitions, selected particulars and silences preserve how the German Princess became recognizable. Ordinary domestic history would introduce other tests. Dates and accessible witnesses could interrupt the succession of mutually supporting impressions. Their absence belongs to the evidence for this reading; London’s appetite for printed scandal explains the occasion for producing it, not the grounds for accepting it.

Printers and booksellers certainly helped shape the public object; a hired writer may also have shaped its sentences. We cannot recover an unassisted voice by removing the title page. Even the composure attributed to Carleton might owe something to someone who knew how a dignified defendant should sound. Much of the pamphlet therefore belongs, in its manufacture, to the publishing trade. But manufacture became an event in her life. Once available for purchase, this account supplied a version of her that witnesses could encounter, purchasers could repeat and later narrators could inherit. Influence on any particular witness would require separate evidence; the publication itself establishes that her self-presentation had acquired a reach beyond her presence. Her credibility was now partly entrusted to sentences she did not have to speak. Before transportation, return and execution hardened the competing image of the habitual criminal, the printed princess was already available to organize recollection, including recollection by people who had never met her.

As a paper passport, the pamphlet gives noble birth as its place of issue, lets jewels and overseas letters serve as stamps, and offers composed prose as the bearer’s likeness, while its blank spaces cover journeys no accessible authority certifies. Such a passport could explain its bearer’s reception without establishing her entitlement to travel under that name. The useful finding is a smaller inventory of what counted as credentials in this particular presentation. We can begin with the prominently displayed letters, whose contents the pamphlet never supplies: their asserted existence supports her connections, although the reader is not given enough to test what those connections were.
