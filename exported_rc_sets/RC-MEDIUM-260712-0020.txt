[PASSAGE]

In a warehouse outside Turin, sometime in 1956, a man with a clipboard measured the elbow height of four hundred assembly workers and wrote down a single number. That number became a bench. The bench became a standard. This was the working method of mid-century industrial ergonomics: gather the population, find the fiftieth percentile of every joint and reach, and build the workstation around that composite phantom, the average man. Deviation was noise to be minimized, not a signal to be honored. It sounds, to a modern ear, like a machine for grinding down the particular—a cold arithmetic that erased the tall, the left-handed, the arthritic. The engineers who did it were not sentimental. They believed the body was a measurable thing, and that measurement, aggregated, yielded a truth no single body could supply.

They were not wrong to believe it. Averaging gave them systems. Reproducible systems. Teachable ones. A safety protocol built on the mean could be printed, shipped, obeyed in Osaka and Milan alike. Most workers really did cluster near that mean. The move worth noticing was quieter than efficiency: they treated the body as a system prior to treating it as a person. That priority—not scale, not economy—was the intellectual bet.

When the agenda swung toward inclusive design, toward the adjustable chair and the accommodated wrist, we gained something plainly good and lost something quietly. We stopped studying how labour distributes across a whole population. The institutional memory of coordinating three hundred bodies in one room—what fails, what queues, where the load piles up—thinned out. Customization answers for one worker at a time. It has no grammar for the crowd. We traded a general knowledge of work for a fine skill at individual adjustment, and mistook the trade for a pure advance.

The doctrine was still false. The average man never sat at any bench. Accommodation was real progress, and I would not undo it. But the bill came due elsewhere: we no longer ask what organizing principle should govern a shared workspace if not the average. The automated line is arriving that must fit no fixed body at all—only a shifting mix of them. It will ask the old question again. We have forgotten the old answer.
