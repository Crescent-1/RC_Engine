[PASSAGE]

A museum label presents itself as a narrow conduit between an object and its visitor: fifty words on a raked card, set at knee height beside a bronze or a beaded collar, with nothing supposedly added in transit. Inspection reveals the curator's choices. Every label selects a date over a use, a maker over a market, a material over a wound. The selection is not concealed so much as unremarked. Whether that unremarked selection makes the description untrustworthy, or merely situated in a place and a purpose, the card itself gives no way to tell.

Eilean Hooper-Greenhill's account of the post-museum is useful here, and its usefulness lies in what it declines to demand. She does not ask that curatorial authority be dissolved; she asks that its acts of selection become visible, so that a claim can be weighed rather than absorbed. On that view a label earns credit by accountable framing, not by a performance of neutrality — a performance that, as evidence goes, tends to be weakest exactly where the object's history is most disputed.

The examples differ in what they disclose. A Benin plaque carrying only "brass, sixteenth century" makes a claim about relevance while appearing to make none; the 1897 expedition is not denied, it is simply outside the frame. A donated family photograph whose sitters were identified through parish and shipping records carries inference openly, and a viewer can check the reasoning. A recent installation described as being "about displacement," full stop, presents a contested reading in the grammar of settled fact. Interpretation is unavoidable in all three. It is not equally answerable in all three.

A practical objection remains: Visitors do not come to adjudicate provenance disputes, and a card that recites every curatorial hesitation becomes institutional autobiography, the museum studying itself in a mirror while the plaque waits. Readability is a real constraint, not an alibi. But conceding that disclosure must be brief concedes nothing about neutrality; a short label is still a chosen one, and brevity restricts how much can be marked, not whether marking is owed.

The apparent dispute concerns how much a label should say, though the deeper issue is the kind of statement a label makes. The word *label* comes into museums from commerce and taxonomy — the tag on a bale, the binomial pinned beneath a beetle — where the thing named was already agreed upon and the tag merely fixed it. Objects with contested histories were absorbed into a form built for uncontested ones. The dispute therefore concerns grammar rather than length. A label can name observation and inference in different registers within the same twenty words. Nothing forbids "carried to Britain in 1897" beside "brass, sixteenth century." The demand for accessibility, which looks like a reason to say less, is what makes the marking necessary: a visitor with one pass through the gallery has no other means of knowing which parts of the sentence are load-bearing. So the question is not whether a label may interpret, since it cannot avoid it, but whose interpretation the visitor is being asked, in those seconds, to take on trust.
