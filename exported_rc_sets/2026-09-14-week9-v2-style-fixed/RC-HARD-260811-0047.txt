[PASSAGE]

Across the three volumes, the effect accumulates like a tide. Old Reinis lays his first keel in 1874 with wet rope burning his palms; his son bends steel plate under gaslight thirty years on; the granddaughter, Anda, sails out of the ruined harbor in 1945 and comes back in 1968 to a yard that has forgotten her name. The trilogy's evenness holds the reader. Nobody in these books is a specimen. The author gives the drunken foreman the same close weather of attention as the shipping magnate, the same slow inventory of doubts. A decade's fear arrives as it was felt, not as it would be labeled. When Reinis frets over a Hamburg loan, the fret is his, undated, unresolved. You are inside the year, not above it. I have read few novels this patient, this unwilling to reduce a person to the era that produced him. The prose refuses the easy verdict at every turn, and refuses it so consistently that the refusal starts to feel like a moral position — humaneness as method. At this point, the design seems complete.

Its structure is clear: yard, hull, exile, return, three generations answering one another across roughly a century of Baltic weather. When the trilogy first appeared, a reviewer in a Riga weekly called it "a novel that always already knows the tide," and everyone has quoted it since — as praise. The phrase sits well. It names the book's unhurried confidence, its refusal to be startled by anything its characters do. What the phrase also names, more quietly, is a position. To know the tide is to stand where the water has already gone out.

A second look changes the meaning of that humaneness. Every hopeful miscalculation in these books is framed so the reader, and only the reader, sees the wave coming. The son mortgages the yard for steel just before the war that will make steel worthless to him; we are placed to feel, from outside his hour, precisely how the loan will close on him. Anda's return in 1968 is staged the same way: the yard's forgetting is announced two chapters before she walks back through its gate. This is not neutrality. A narrator seated after 1945 cannot grant his 1874 patriarch a genuinely open morning; he can only reconstruct one and let us watch the light fail on schedule. The much-praised lived contingency is retrospection in costume — fate parceled out as texture, so finely that it passes for freedom. The book does not refuse to judge. It has judged, in advance, and hidden the sentence inside the weather.

None of which makes the trilogy a lesser thing; it makes it a different thing than the reviews claim. What it delivers is not suspense but elegy served early, grief for people the narrator has already buried and now walks slowly back through. That is rarer, and harder, than open-endedness — and it costs exactly what it is praised for possessing: the reader's freedom to be surprised alongside Reinis is the one experience these three volumes, knowing the tide, can never give.
