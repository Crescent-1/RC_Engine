[PASSAGE]

Contamination is treated according to what it is thought to be, and resistance genes in wastewater are not reliably any one thing. Chlorination is the obvious remedy, and a good one on its own terms. It kills. Free chlorine oxidizes membranes, proteins, whatever it reaches, and the count of viable resistant organisms in a treated effluent falls by orders of magnitude. The objection raised against it by wastewater microbiologists is precise rather than sweeping: a dead cell is not an empty one. Extracellular DNA persists in water for hours to days, sometimes longer when adsorbed to sediment, and a resistance cassette in that fragment can still be taken up by a competent bacterium downstream. Filtration has the mirror problem. Membranes are sized for particles, and a plasmid a few hundred nanometres across in solution is not a particle in the sense the filter was designed around.

The causes are unglamorous and additive: antibiotic use in clinics and livestock selects for resistant strains; hospitals and feedlots concentrate those strains at high density; bacteria trade resistance genes across species on mobile elements; and a treatment plant, receiving all of this, mostly redistributes what arrives rather than manufacturing it.

Engineers did not arrive at these methods by mistaking DNA for a germ. A plant must report something. Regulators require an endpoint that can be measured on a Tuesday by a technician with a defined protocol, and "log reduction of viable organisms" is such an endpoint in a way that "diminished probability of horizontal transfer somewhere downstream" is not. The WHO's guidance on antimicrobial resistance in the environment is explicit that surveillance methods remain unsettled, which is an admission about instruments, not about intentions. The material simply changes category as it moves. In the sewer it behaves as a pollutant load. In the aeration basin it is a pathogen carrier. In the receiving river, released from its dead host, it is transferable information, and the categories that make treatment legible were built before that last state was measurable.

Conventional treatment does substantially reduce viable resistant bacteria, and abandoning it would be absurd. But a reduction in carriers is not the elimination of the trait. The case resembles neither a pathogen that can be killed nor a chemical that can be diluted; its hazard is realized only on uptake, inside a host that may be encountered years later.

What follows from that is a layered procedure, and it works. Reduce antibiotic discharge where it is concentrated, at hospital wards and animal operations. Segregate those high-strength streams rather than blending them into the municipal flow. Then treat in sequence: biological degradation to lower the organic burden, membrane capture to hold back cells and the larger free fragments, and a validated DNA-damaging step — UV at adequate fluence, ozone at demonstrated dose — aimed at the nucleic acid itself. Monitor both compartments, culturable carriers and extracellular genes by quantitative assay. No single stage certifies that every sequence is destroyed, which is precisely the argument for the sequence rather than against it. The approach is sound because it tracks the material through each form it takes, and it will remain the correct one for as long as resistance genes can still travel through shared water.
