What are sixteen bars worth? In the archive of a provincial conservatoire, in a room with a broken window sash and a card index that no one has migrated, sits the engraver's proof of a symphony's slow movement, and across bars 94 to 109 the composer has drawn two diagonal strokes in brown ink. The strokes are not in dispute. What they mean is. One party — call them the strict editionists — holds that the work is what the composer authorized, and that authorization is an act with a date; the deletion is the last word and the last word governs. The other party, the restorationists, points to two concert notices from the year before publication describing a passage no printed score contains, and argues that the deleted bars were heard, and that what was heard cannot simply be unheard by a pen.

I expected the quarrel to be small. It is not. Two national societies have issued incompatible critical editions. A funding body in Vienna declined to underwrite a recording on textual grounds. A journal ran a special issue; the editor's introduction runs to nine thousand words and cites forty-one items. Conductors are asked, in interviews, which text they use, and their answers are reported as positions. All of this is conducted in the register of rigor: better sources, cleaner stemma, more scrupulous handling of the concert notices. An informant, a librarian of thirty years, describes the annual conference session as a ritual — the same two men, the same lectern, the same slides of the brown ink, applause of equal length for both.

The escalation is disproportionate, and disproportion is diagnostic. Nobody spends a career on ink. Strip the footnotes and the two camps are not disagreeing about the proof sheet at all. The restorationists believe, without ever writing it down, that a piece of music belongs to those who have received it, that meaning silts up over generations, and that a composer is one contributor among many to something held in common. The strict editionists believe the opposite: that the artist is sovereign over the artifact, and everyone after is a custodian. Neither belief is stated. Both are unarguable in the open. So they are fought where they can be fought — over dates, hands, watermarks, the reliability of a Leipzig reviewer who may have been drunk.

Say it more carefully: the proxy is not merely a convenient venue. It is the only venue. A direct argument about whether art means what its maker meant has no shared standard of evidence — the restorationist cannot lose, the editionist cannot lose, and the exchange becomes two cosmologies bowing to each other. The textual dispute supplies what the deep question lacks: sub-questions that look falsifiable. When was the ink applied? Whose hand? The word "authentic" performed this migration once already, from theology to law to instrument-making before it reached the concert hall, and each move bought an unarguable commitment a set of arguable proxies. So the strict editionists are right about the bars, because the definition they inherited already decided the case before the watermarks were examined — and they will remain right until someone can say, in plain terms and to a standard both camps accept, what a work is when its author has stopped speaking.
