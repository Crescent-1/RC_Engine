[PASSAGE]

In 1363, an English parliament decided that a carter, ploughman, or oxherd should wear nothing but blanket and russet cloth costing no more than twelve pence the yard, and belt himself with linen — no silver on the buckle. Was there ever a law so easy to laugh at? Venice appointed magistrates specifically to inspect sleeves. Florence had officials who counted the buttons on a woman's gown in the street and levied fines by the button; one chronicler records a lady arguing that her buttons were not buttons, having no buttonholes, and winning. The Japanese shogunate banned certain reds. Nuremberg told merchants' wives how far a train might trail behind them. Everywhere the same pattern: the powerful decided that the merely rich must not look like them, and dressed the decision up as thrift and piety, with a garnish of hostility toward whatever foreign silk was arriving that decade. The whole apparatus reads now as caste enforcement conducted at the level of the hemline, petty in method and vicious in aim, and it collapsed, as it deserved to, once liberal societies decided a person's coat was that person's affair. I have never met anyone who wished the button-counters back. Nor, for what it is worth, do I.

But watch what the button-counters were actually doing. A dinner party where one guest arrives in cloth-of-gold obliges every other guest to spend more next time, and the guest after that more again — the sort of game where nobody can stop while anyone continues, and where the winner has merely arrived at the starting line of the following round. Sumptuary law was a clumsy referee blowing a whistle on that game. Its stated reasons were nonsense and its enforcement was snobbery. Its effect, in a world without advertising, consumer credit, or the mail-order catalogue, was to put a ceiling on how far a family could be dragged upward by the spending of its neighbours. Crude, yes. But something was holding the ceiling up.

Nothing holds it now. When the statutes went, no institution inherited the job; the function was not transferred but abolished, on the assumption that it had never been a function at all, only a prejudice wearing the costume of policy. Fred Hirsch, in 1976, named the difficulty precisely: positional goods are those whose value consists in others not having them, and no amount of general enrichment can distribute them more widely. He proposed no magistrate. Neither do I. What I am pointing at is an absence — the absence of anything whatever, custom or law or shame, standing between a household and the escalation its neighbours set in motion. Markets are not referees. They are the game.

So let me say plainly what I am not saying. The old laws cannot be salvaged, patched, or quietly reintroduced under a better name; they were built to keep a wool merchant's daughter in her place, and that was not incidental to them but the point. The loss is real, and it is uncompensated, and it is also permanent. We were right to burn the instrument. We simply had nothing else that did the work, and we still have nothing.
