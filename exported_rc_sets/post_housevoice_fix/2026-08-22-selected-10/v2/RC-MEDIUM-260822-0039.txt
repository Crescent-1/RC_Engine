[PASSAGE]

In 1953, Keep America Beautiful began assembling cheerful civic instruction from advertisements, school materials, cleanup days and the familiar figure of the careless motorist. The instruction was nearly everywhere and it was easy to follow: filmstrips for classrooms, tidy verges on billboards, a Saturday morning of sacks and gloves. Roadsides still acquired cups, cartons, cigarette packs and bottles at a rate no poster could approach. I once took this as a simple failure of public education: an exhortation met a durable habit and lost. The campaigns remained visible long after their promises had thinned. Plastic did not. The highway shoulder, with its flattened coffee lids, seemed to offer a blunt verdict on persuasion.

The verdict was incomplete. Keep America Beautiful was not an accidental association of tidying-minded citizens; packaging, beverage and food interests supported an organisation operating while bottle-deposit proposals and restrictions on disposable containers gained force. Granted, the campaigns did not make America litter-free, but they made the consumer appear to be the decisive agent in a problem whose materials had been designed, manufactured and distributed elsewhere. “Litter” had once named matter out of place: straw in a stable, scraps on a floor, debris after use. It became a civic offense, then a personal blemish. Producer and passer-by could now seem to occupy symmetrical positions beside the same paper cup. They did not. One selected its material, volume and route to market; the other encountered it after use. Clean up. Be neat. Care. The small commands transferred attention from the commodity’s career to the hand that failed to dispose of it properly.

I abandoned my earlier reading in 2014, while looking at a roadside-cleanup photograph in which a volunteer held a black sack open with one shoe against its rim. The lesson had not been ignored. — No: more precisely, it had been absorbed at the scale where it was easiest to perform. Picking up a wrapper became a daily instrument for measuring character. It could also stand in for inquiry into why the wrapper had been made to travel briefly from shop to car to ditch. The campaign’s visible defeat therefore concealed a success of classification: waste was experienced as conduct before it was considered as an output.

Its minor character, the conscientious passer-by who retrieves another person’s discarded object, clarifies the arrangement because the figure appears at the point where a public landscape has been made into a field of intimate repair. There is genuine care in the gesture; a roadside is cleaner when the sack is filled. Yet the action resembles an observatory’s technician adjusting a lens while the instrument’s makers continue to send more light into it: the correction is necessary, exact and local, while the conditions requiring it remain outside the technician’s reach. This figure matters less as proof that citizenship has succeeded than as evidence that citizenship has been assigned the aftercare of disposable production. In that roadside scene, responsibility has been relocated to the person bending over the cup, and the more consequential inquiry concerns the arrangements that made that bending seem sufficient.
