Public access has never meant that every use of a shared room travels with every visitor who enters it. The branch librarian traces the trouble through successive meanings of “public”: a door kept open without inquiry, then observation and description as liberties exercised through that door, then the translation of both into potential circulation. A lifestyle streamer crossed the reading room with her phone upright, broadcasting live. Nobody challenged her at first. Faces appeared. Voices carried. Objections began only when patrons learned that the scene was already online, moving beyond the room faster than consent could be requested or refusal made useful. At the board meeting, the librarian proposed a cream notice, NO CASUAL FILMING, to be taped beside the clock. One faction called it a restoration of library quiet, treating the camera as an amplified conversation; another called it a border guard, turning an institution funded by strangers into a club for those whom staff deemed unobtrusive. The quarrel was not caused simply by new equipment; in her account, portable transmission made an old distinction operationally urgent. Not secrecy imposed on civic life, she said, but a separation between being observable in place and being distributable elsewhere. Yet the notice’s blunt adjective offered staff control without explaining whose purposes counted as casual.

The streamer answers that she entered neither a confidential service desk nor a private home. Her viewers wanted the neglected building: cracked terrazzo, dim stacks, a local room surviving between a pharmacy and a vacant shop. Where patrons treated the camera as taking possession of their images, she treated it as returning a publicly maintained place to public attention. She did not interview anyone. She named no one. She lingered nowhere. She concedes that a live audience removes the interval in which accidental exposure can be edited away, but says the complaint converts ordinary co-presence into proprietary authority over a room belonging, in some measure, to everyone.

The man beside the computers gives the narrower account. Recently dismissed, he was completing a job application when the camera passed: screen glare, hunched shoulders, an unfinished form, the cheerfully narrated bad day. A sidewalk permits glances, he allows, but the broadcast preserved what nearby strangers would forget. For him, that difference was enough.

A deaf student reports another preservation: staff now preserve vigilance around any upright phone. Her video calls carry classmates’ faces; captions carry their words. Under disability practice the device is access, while under the notice it resembles evidence of breach. Twice asked what she was recording, she became conspicuous because the rule promised inconspicuousness to others.

The security guard prefers the sign. Before it, each dispute made him classify a phone within seconds: notebook, captioning tool, parent’s camera, or instrument of humiliation. People shouted. He guessed. Someone complained. The notice gives him words before judgment, although its exceptions return judgment through a side door; administrative tradition values that pause even when equal treatment cannot survive classification.

The board secretary records six incompatible accounts and no unanimously acceptable wording, leaving permission, assistance and enforcement unsettled. The surviving proposition is not that the library is public or private, but that entry need not entail equal availability to everyone else; because neither openness nor uniform prohibition can secure that distinction, this rule can only keep it under revision.
