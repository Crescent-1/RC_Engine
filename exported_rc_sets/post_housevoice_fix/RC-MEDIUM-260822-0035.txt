[PASSAGE]

Begin with the file. Tioga County, Pennsylvania, 2019. A mother of two works two part-time jobs, one at a diner, one driving for a rideshare platform. Her childcare subsidy lapses in March. The stated reason: missed work-verification deadline. She says she mailed the forms. The state says it has no record. I confess my first instinct was the old one, the one I was raised on — keep your papers, meet your dates, and the world mostly holds. Somebody dropped something. Probably her.

Not her. The intake queue at the county assistance office had a six-week backlog and four caseworkers for a workload sized to nine. Her packet was date-stamped on receipt and processed after the cutoff. Worse: the cutoff had been revised downward in February and applied to submissions already in transit. So this is not the story of a woman who cannot follow instructions. Nor — and here I have to correct my second instinct, which was to blame the four exhausted caseworkers — is it the story of an understaffed office. Understaffing is the symptom. The design is the thing.

Suppose she had made every date. The verification form still required an employer's countersignature attesting that the second job was real, and her second employer was a platform that classifies its drivers as independent contractors and, as a matter of standing legal posture, declines to countersign anything absent a subpoena — a refusal that some readers will take as proof that gig work is not real employment and that the mandate is right to test it, when what the refusal actually tests is whether a woman earning eleven dollars an hour can obtain a court order during the same twenty hours per week she must document as job search or lose the subsidy she is documenting in order to keep.

Follow the threshold itself. It moves. Pennsylvania recalculated eligibility bands against revised federal poverty guidelines on a rolling monthly basis in that period, so the marginal effect of an extra shift was unknowable in advance: the same eight hours could clear a work requirement and breach an income ceiling. Her caseworker could not tell her which. I have looked for the sentence in the policy manual that resolves this and cannot find it. The mandate did not measure her employability. It measured the distance between two of the state's own numbers.

Four passes, and nothing from the earlier ones withdrawn: the deadline was real, the office was starved, the platform did refuse, the thresholds did drift. What accumulates is not a list of fixable errors. The mandate presumes stable employment categories and a bureaucracy that answers on time, and by 2019 neither existed; the test therefore stopped testing her and began testing the apparatus, which failed. Here the two camps — the design school, which says tighten enforcement, and the capacity school, which says fund the offices — both take the resulting failure rate as data about recipients. It is not. And I refuse the tidy middle verdict that it is somewhat both. The burden now sits with whoever claims a mandate can detect a pre-existing barrier: show one it did not help build. Start with the countersignature line on Form PA 1663, still blank, still required.
