[PASSAGE]

Cinema has taught its faithful to look for the breach in performance: the moment when a real cry escapes the part and leaves feeling, briefly, on the face. I used to hunt such moments with the zeal of a convert, replaying a close-up in which an actress sits beside a chipped blue enamel basin and her lower lid shines. The School of Accident called that tear grief’s involuntary witness; the School of Craft called it glycerine, breath control, and trade. The eye fills. The camera stays. We believe. Between induced sorrow and induced moisture, this tear appeared to await a verdict.

That close shot frustrates the division. Glycerine before a take may mingle with tears brought on by private bereavement; a person flattened by grief may sit beside that blue basin dry-eyed; a trained actor may alter breath, memory and facial muscles until the lacrimal glands answer, although no loss in her life corresponds to the role’s. The liquid supplies no pedigree. This gives the Craft school much of what it wants: cinema can manufacture persuasive moisture. Yet manufacture does not cancel feeling that may attend it — no, it prevents the fluid from certifying feeling. The close-up’s tear is a poor witness.

The inward test has an older, tenderer appeal: ask the actress at the basin. I once treated an interview after a premiere as a priest’s deposition. Yet her account of the close-up could report sorrow and omit the calculation that held the take together; at the instant, sorrow can coexist with fatigue, vanity, concentration, even pleasure in hitting a mark. Stanislavski’s Tortsov tells a pupil in An Actor Prepares that every moment onstage he must be “living the part.” Performance has travelled from theatre into managerial ratings and machine benchmarks, where achievement is assessed outside the doer. Sincerity does no cleaner work than the moisture.

When the close-up holds on the actress’s cheek beside the basin—whether moisture arrived through a nozzle, a remembered death, or the disciplined tightening of a throat—the filmed cry ceases to look like evidence dispatched from an interior and appears as an event arranged among body, role, camera attention, and a spectator’s wish to recognise pain, each giving the droplet a use the others cannot supply. Camera and spectator are not symmetrical: the former selects duration and scale; the latter lends belief, suspicion, or appetite. “Real” names ethical and aesthetic weight within this arrangement, not a substance in the tear.

Tempting as it is, ‘partly real and partly fake’ will not do for the close-up. It imagines two ingredients whose line can be located before the scene begins. It cannot. Authenticity is not a hidden content in the tear, waiting for a measure; rather, it is a contested mode of receiving the filmed event. Viewers may grant it gravity, enjoy its virtuosity, or dismiss it as manipulation, and the performer’s testimony joins that traffic without mastering it. This is not a denial: bodies hurt and actors work; it refuses to make the droplet final proof. I had wanted the wet eye to save me from the uncertainty of other people. That faith costs something when it fails. No bodily display can verify an interior on screen. Attention may settle instead on what a film asks a body to enact, and on the claims an audience makes in believing, enjoying, or refusing that enactment. Those claims stay open.
