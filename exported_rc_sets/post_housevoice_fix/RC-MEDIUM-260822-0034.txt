[PASSAGE]

The style manual on the sub-editor's desk at the paper where I once corrected proofs — a 1963 edition, rebacked in tape — devotes four pages to the distinction between "which" and "that," and does so with the serene confidence of an institution that has never been contradicted to its face. This is prescriptivism at its most attractive: a single coherent grammar, teachable, examinable, capable of sorting any utterance into the permitted and the barred, and enforced through the mild ceremonies of schooling, the red pen, the returned application, the raised eyebrow at a job interview. One admires the architecture. One admires, in particular, how completely it accounts for everything except the people who speak.

The sub-editor also spoke. So did the compositors. So did everyone in the building after six o'clock, when the manual stayed shut. "Ain't" persisted three floors down and had persisted for two centuries. Double negatives did their work in that print room with perfect precision, understood by all parties, misunderstood by none. Nobody was confused. Sociolinguists have spent decades documenting exactly this: variation by region, by generation, by room, by hour, stable and rule-governed in its own right, entirely indifferent to the four pages on "which." The manual prescribed. The building spoke. The building won, and it is tempting to file the matter there.

Tempting, but the ledger doesn't balance that way — no: more precisely, the two parties were never keeping the same accounts. The manual was answering a question about what ought to appear in a newspaper of record; the linguists studying that same print room were answering a question about what human beings demonstrably do with their mouths. Neither answer bears on the other. To find the manual empirically refuted by the compositors is like finding a wedding invitation refuted by a census: the invitation was not a claim about attendance. And the symmetry usually drawn between the two positions fails at exactly this joint, because only one of them carries institutional force. Descriptivism can tell you that "ain't" is systematic; it cannot tell the applicant whether to use it on the form. The manual could, and did, and the applicant knew it. This is not incommensurability, and it is certainly not an invitation to declare both sides equally right and retire. It is a difference in what the question was for.

What the print room actually knew, and neither literature quite says, is that a compositor choosing his register at the counter was not choosing between correct and incorrect but between two goods that do not reconcile: being understood by strangers, and being recognised by his own. The manual encoded the first and pretended it was the whole of the matter. The linguists mapped the second with great care and declined, on principle, to say what it cost anyone. Grant the descriptivists their evidence entirely — the variation is real, the systems are real — and the compositor's difficulty survives intact, because it was never a difficulty about rules. It is a standing negotiation, conducted daily, by people who cannot be told from outside which loss to accept, and who may be right to distrust anyone confident enough to tell them.
