[PASSAGE]

A surveyor sets a plane table over a brass pin, sights a distant church spire, and pencils a line across the sheet — a ray, not a point. That is all a bearing ever gives: a direction, extending indefinitely, containing the target somewhere along its length. The remedy is old and elegant. Take a second station whose distance and direction from the first are already known, sight the same object, draw a second ray, and the two lines cross once. The crossing is the answer. Distance, which no single sighting can supply, emerges from geometry rather than measurement. Navigators fixed positions this way from two lighthouses; astronomers built the parallax method on the same logic, using the Earth's orbit as their baseline and the six-month interval as their second station. The appeal is that it demands so little. No rangefinder, no travel to the object, no instrument that measures anything but angle. Two competent observers and one honest baseline convert direction into location. The arithmetic is untroubled by weather or distance or the observer's opinion of what he is looking at.

Wildfire lookouts run this logic as a daily protocol. A watchman aligns the Osborne Firefinder's sighting apertures on a smoke, reads an azimuth off the circular map beneath it, and radios the number to a dispatcher, who pairs it with a bearing called in from another tower. The instrument resolves fractions of a degree — though its accuracy turns less on that resolution than on what each watchman has actually put in his sights.

Consider two towers in a mountain district, one on a granite dome above a river canyon, the other eleven miles northeast on a timbered ridge. On an August afternoon both call in bearings on the same column of smoke, four minutes apart. The dispatcher plots them. The lines cross. The intersection falls in a drainage neither tower can see into, and a crew is dispatched to it.

There is nothing wrong with the arithmetic. The ridge between the two towers hides the fire's base from both, and the column, rising into a westerly, bends downwind before either watchman picks it up. The granite dome sees the lower bend of the plume; the timbered ridge, from its angle, catches a higher torn section a quarter-mile further along. Two rays, two different objects, one crossing. The crossing is real, computable, and empty. Triangulation cannot detect this, because the method assumes agreement about the target and has no means of testing that assumption — it verifies the lines, never the thing at their far end. So the failure is not error in the ordinary sense. Both readings are correct. Both watchmen are competent. The consolation that better instruments would help is worth naming and setting down; a firefinder accurate to a tenth of a degree pins the empty point more tightly. What crews rely on instead is smaller and duller: before trusting an intersection, the dispatcher asks a tower to shoot a bearing on a known landmark — a lookout peak, a lake outlet — and checks it against the recorded value. The instrument is not the doubtful part. What wants asking is whether the two towers are looking at the same smoke, and the answer comes from a bearing on Bald Mountain.
