[PASSAGE]

For roughly four decades, occupational psychology treated a particular proposition as settled: that a working adult possesses an underlying personality type, that a trained administrator can identify it with a paper instrument in under an hour, and that once identified, it can be matched to a job structure suited to it. I want to be exact about how strong that consensus was. Corporations bought the instruments in bulk. Universities certified administrators. Placement decisions — who moved into supervision, who stayed on analysis, who was told they were miscast — were made downstream of a four-letter code. This was not folklore that leaked into human resources from the culture. It was professional practice, taught as such, with a literature behind it.

Several weaker claims travel under the same name, and none of them is what I am examining. Not the claim that temperament colours how someone works — trivially true, and no basis for an industry. Not the claim that a shared vocabulary helps a team argue less bitterly about style, which is a claim about conversation and would never have justified a hiring decision. Not the claim that people find the categories illuminating about themselves; they do, and so what. The proposition doing the load-bearing work was harder and fully falsifiable: that measured type predicts job performance well enough that placing people by it beats placing them without it.

That claim rests on one condition. Type must hold still. If a person's measured type drifts, there is nothing durable for the placement to be matched against — you cannot fit a component to a dimension that changes between measurements. Stability was the load path. And for decades it looked secure, for reasons that were not silly. The instruments were internally consistent; items within a scale moved together, as they should. Administrators were trained and re-trained. Respondents recognised their own descriptions and said so. Norming samples ran to thousands. There were certification programs, licensing exams, continuing-education requirements for people whose living depended on scoring the thing correctly. Every part of that apparatus was constructed as though stability had been established. None of it established stability. Internal consistency tells you a scale coheres at one sitting. It says nothing about what happens at the second sitting. The direct test — administer, wait, administer again, count how many people land in the same box — is cheap, obvious, and was not, for a long while, run at scale.

When it was run at scale, and the retest data accumulated across the ordinary channels of the field's own journals, the condition failed outright rather than narrowly: substantial fractions of respondents were classified differently on retake within weeks, with no change in the job and none in the person, and meta-analytic estimates of predictive validity against actual performance outcomes sat close to what you would get from nothing. So the field was right to retire type-matching as a selection tool, and I would say so with no hedge. What it has not yet paid for is the aftermath: coaching practices, team workshops, and the private self-descriptions of millions of employees still run on that vocabulary, and nobody has explained why a diagnostic language survives so comfortably the death of its diagnosis.
