[PASSAGE]

A couple has spent eleven months and most of a savings account renovating a kitchen. The contractor's estimate for finishing has doubled. Selling now, half-done, would cost them less than completing it. They complete it. Asked why, they mention the eleven months.

A decision scientist I heard address a room of hospital administrators was crisp about this: the sunk cost fallacy is a wiring error, nothing more exotic, and the correction is a rule you can teach in an afternoon — past expenditures are past, so ignore them and price the future. She was right that the rule is teachable. Her own audience could recite it within the hour. What she did not mention, and what the follow-up work in her field keeps turning up, is that the teaching does not hold where it would count. People who state the rule cleanly about a stranger's kitchen abandon it about their own marriage, their own dissertation, their own department.

Call it a bias, then, and the prediction follows: it should erode under feedback, the way a systematic error in a clock is filed away once the watchmaker sees it gaining. It mostly doesn't. Call it a heuristic and the prediction is better — refusing to walk away from expenditure is a legible signal to others that you cannot be bled by strategic delay, which is a real advantage in a real bargaining world, and there are cases where the reluctance pays exactly this way. But the paradigm case is not one of them. Nobody is watching the couple's kitchen. Call it a norm, finally — a moral inheritance about not squandering what has been spent — and the cultural variation is genuine; how much waste offends a person plainly depends on how a person was raised. The variation is around a constant. The pull is present in populations whose stated thrift ethics diverge sharply, and it is present in individuals who explicitly disavow the ethic.

Return to the kitchen. Nothing about the couple has changed; three vocabularies have been tried on them and each has left the same sediment behind. The scientist's confident claim survives in modified form. She said the persistence was the error. The persistence is instead the finding: this behavior does not sit inside one level of explanation, and its refusal to sit is the most reliable thing about it. Notice that the three categories were never parallel to begin with — bias names a malfunction, heuristic names a function, norm names an obligation, and a discipline that offers all three as a menu is offering three different kinds of answer to a question it has not fixed. So the live matter is not which box the couple belongs in. It is why decision science requires a box at all, and what loosens if the requirement is dropped: economics stops needing an irrational agent as a residual category, therapy stops treating the reluctance as a symptom to be argued away, the whole picture of the deviant chooser goes slack. The classification will keep failing to hold until the disciplines stop demanding that their objects sit at exactly one altitude of explanation. Meanwhile the countertop goes in, quartz, the pattern they picked in March.
