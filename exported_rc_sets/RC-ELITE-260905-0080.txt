[PASSAGE]

A stroller stops before a Berlin shop window and lets the passing crowd go ahead. Such a pause belongs readily to the world of Franz Hessel’s Walking in Berlin, published in 1929. It also supplies an accusation. Unemployment, police violence and nationalist mobilisation were transforming the city; Hessel lingered over courtyards, faded façades and unhurried acquaintances. We can distinguish three uses of urban description. Reportage connects a street to an event. Social observation connects its ordinary uses to the people who command them. Reverie releases it into private association. Each has a recognisable operation. A reporter names the confrontation, locates its participants and establishes what happened. A social observer follows someone through an entrance, notices who passes unchallenged and discovers how apparently common space is apportioned. A reverist allows a doorway to summon childhood. The brass plate beside that doorway need not interest all three equally. These distinctions give the charge against Hessel considerable force. His composure, on the evidence of his preferred scenes, looks cultivated rather than compulsory. He has time. The practice of strolling had travelled, through nineteenth-century Parisian writing, from leisurely streetgoing into an art of reading metropolitan life; transplanted to Berlin, its cultivated detachment could look like an inherited exemption from urgency. Call the opposing interpreters the accusers and the defenders: these are positions, not organised schools. The accusers find protected leisure where the defenders find a city becoming less shareable. Yet a remembered courtyard can accommodate reverie and social observation simultaneously: remembering former encounters may reveal that their setting no longer admits them. Nor does every account of an entrance establish who was refused. The three compartments cease to hold their contents apart. Agreement about Hessel’s leisurely manner would therefore settle little; the contested object becomes the relation between his walker’s freedom and the freedoms available around him.

The accusers want the lens turned towards Blutmai, joblessness, the republic’s approaching collapse. The defenders inspect its field of vision: who can loiter, enter, remember, feel at home. One demands an identifiable subject; the other notices the limits of the frame. Granted, the latter makes political attention a matter of altered access rather than topical declaration, without making the missing subjects disappear. A wider field is not necessarily a sufficiently exposed picture.

“Political” consequently names either avowed comment on collective events or sensory and social arrangements that disclose unequal possession of a city. This difference generates much of the disagreement about the same mild sentences. Definitions can make the quarrel look soluble by stipulation. Hessel’s adequacy, however, depends—by the stronger test—on the social range his walker permits, not merely on our ability to give his quietness a political name. Some exclusions may remain comfortably unseen.

Walking in Berlin matters most as an education of urban readers, teaching perception of fragile continuities while perhaps leaving their destruction recognisable too late; as Hessel’s scattered newspaper pieces become more fully available and catalogued, their immediate occasions will require comparison with the book’s slower invitations. The consequential object is how these public forms distribute attention under pressure, since an education in noticing may disclose its limits only when circumstances change.
