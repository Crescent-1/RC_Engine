Restoration was long understood as the reversal of material deterioration until the work originally authorized by its maker became available again. The procedure gave that proposition considerable substance. Archivists brought surviving prints together, compared their lengths and shot sequences, and inspected the joins where a projectionist might have removed damaged film; an early print supplied evidence of arrangement, while a later, cleaner copy could supply the image. Neither age nor condition decided every choice. Missing frames were borrowed from another element, with adjacent movement checked to prevent a repair from creating a jump that no audience had previously seen. Projection speed was established from surviving instructions and the movement within shots; tinting, toning and faded colour were reconstructed through comparison with less altered material. Each replacement had a source. Once these operations had been completed, a new master gathered the strongest evidence into a continuous film, and the expense of searching, copying and laboratory work purchased something more exact than an attractive reissue: an approximation to the complete state from which the surviving copies were presumed to have descended.

Borrowing restoration’s promise from painting conservation, twentieth-century film archives applied it to an art whose surviving material was already dispersed among copies, and, from the late 1980s through the early 2000s, increasingly encountered differences that cleaning or replacement could not settle. As photochemical reconstruction acquired digital tools, borrowed images could meet almost without visible seams; as repertory screenings and home video, followed later by streaming, carried the resulting master to millions, the archive’s selection became the film available to audiences who could not inspect its sources. Distributors gained a reusable master; alternative releases lost circulation. Comparison nevertheless disclosed a difficulty untouched by technical improvement: different language editions, censor-approved cuts and revisions authorized for separate markets could all belong to a film’s initial circulation, leaving no single complete ancestor for their differences to recover.

Digital repair can recover lost information. It need not invent a version. A torn frame can be recomposed from surviving pieces; faded colour channels can be realigned using an unfaded source. Such repairs have an ascertainable object. The FIAF Code of Ethics directs archives “only to complete what is incomplete and to remove the accretions of time, wear and misinformation.” That restraint is necessary. It does not identify which historically attested release supplies the completeness against which a missing passage becomes a loss.

Restoration consequently multiplies the films offered under one title: Buenos Aires footage enlarged Metropolis in 2010, recovering missing scenes; Welles’s memorandum supplied Touch of Evil with an intended revision, not an uncontested original; competing scores, speeds and cuts of Gance’s Napoléon brought exhibition’s own variations into restored circulation. Following Paolo Cherchi Usai’s insistence on cinema’s material history, archivists collate witnesses, declare which release or intention governs shot selection, repair damage within that choice, and retain unused variants with an account of interventions; seamlessness cannot perform those operations.

Archives were right to retain the ordinary word “restore,” with a smaller promise: returning a damaged film to view, not cancelling its other histories. Fulfilling that promise requires keeping the chosen route and the versions excluded from it recoverable alongside the restoration.
