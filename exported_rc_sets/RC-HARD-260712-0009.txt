[PASSAGE]

Open a biostatistics textbook printed after 1990. Somewhere in the first chapter you will find Ronald Fisher's potato plots at Rothamsted, the 1920s field trials where he scattered treatments across parcels of English soil by the throw of dice. Open a manual of clinical epidemiology instead. There you meet the 1948 streptomycin trial for tuberculosis, the British study that assigned patients to treatment by sealed envelope and is routinely called the first modern randomized controlled trial. Two disciplines. Two founding scenes. One method.

The dispute is not a footnote. It is live, and it has institutional weight. Prize citations phrase it one way; departmental histories phrase it another. Statisticians say medicine borrowed the tool and forgot the lender. Epidemiologists say the tool was inert until they picked it up. Each field teaches its own version to its own students, and each version quietly casts the other discipline as a latecomer. The question sounds settleable. It is not, though the reader could be forgiven for expecting a scoreboard.

The statisticians hold the stronger analytic hand, and it is worth stating without discount. Fisher did not merely happen to randomize; he demonstrated, with full mathematical rigor, that randomization is the only device that licenses a valid estimate of error in the presence of confounders one cannot name or measure. The logic was complete by the early 1930s. It was airtight. Nothing medicine later did improved the proof one line.

Grant all of that, and the epidemiologists still have a reply. A proof that solves an inference problem on paper is not the same accomplishment as a procedure that changes how a profession decides whether a drug may enter a sick body. Fisher's argument was true before anyone acted on it; the trial became an idea only when it began to carry consequences.

Which is where the contest turns strange. Both camps argue as though ideas have birthplaces — single rooms, datable mornings, rightful owners. Neither examines the premise. Yet the statistician locates origin in the formal proof, the epidemiologist in the consequential act, and neither can win because they are answering different questions while pretending to answer one. The dispute survives because each field's origin myth depends on it; concede the shared assumption and both lose an ancestor.

So far the archives record a quarrel over parentage. What they less often record is that the method had no single moment of becoming — that it accreted, like sediment, across two decades and two problems that only later looked like one. The useful question was never which discipline owns the randomized trial. It is what we should mean by the origin of a method at all, given that a method, unlike a discovery, does not exist until it is done and done again, so that its birthday, if it has one, falls somewhere no ledger thought to mark.
