[PASSAGE]

In a converted stockroom off the third floor, a firm I once advised kept its biometric kiosk—a chest-high cabinet where employees pressed a thumb to a plate each Monday and watched their cortisol proxy scroll across a screen. From such small stations a great edifice now rises. Corporations have assembled screening protocols, wearable monitors, salaried counselors, and quarterly interventions of a thoroughness the old shop foreman could not have dreamed; and the reported rates of occupational illness, measured against that apparatus, have genuinely fallen. I distrust novelty, and I confess a nostalgia for the era when a worker's health was his own affair—but honesty compels me to record that this machinery detects and treats real harm the old privacy merely hid.

Still, a suspicion nags. The instrument that measures may also legislate. When a firm defines "occupational stress" precisely enough to screen for it, it does not simply find the condition waiting there; it draws the boundary that decides which fatigue counts, which ache is reportable, which Monday reading crosses the line into diagnosis.

Grant the suspicion its due—it earns it. The categories do shape the counting. Stress, once screened, becomes codifiable stress; fatigue becomes the quantifiable kind, the kind a kiosk can register at eight in the morning. I would like to say this settles the matter against the apparatus. It does not. The suffering these categories capture was not conjured from nothing; the machinist with the ruined shoulder hurt before anyone had a form for it. What the infrastructure invents is not the pain but its address—the coordinates at which pain becomes a thing an institution can be asked to answer for. And a suffering that can be addressed is, for the first time, a suffering that can be reduced.

So the original claim stands, and I will not pretend otherwise. Wellness programs do lower illness rates. The mechanism that lowers them is double—part genuine repair, part the very act of naming that makes repair possible—but a mixed cause is still a cause, and the reduction is not an artifact. The thing works. That is the finding, and it holds.

Now the bill. To make suffering addressable, the firm made it visible—permanently, cheaply, on schedule. The worker's body is legible now in a way it never was at the lathe. Every Monday it reports. The private fact of feeling unwell, which a man used to carry home unrecorded, has become an auditable asset on someone else's books; his relief and his exposure arrive in the same instrument. The illness figures fall, truly. They fall because the corporation now owns a standing map of the bodies it employs. And this is not peculiar to health. Wherever a measurement system arrives before the phenomenon it claims only to observe—in schooling, in policing, in the quiet metrics by which we are all increasingly known—the same purchase is made, and the price of being helped is being seen at that scale.
