**PASSAGE**

When geologists coined the term "Anthropocene" to mark a proposed epoch defined by human influence on Earth's systems, they assumed the label would clarify a boundary: on one side, processes driven by tectonics, climate, and deep time; on the other, processes driven by us. Yet the closer one examines the materials now accumulating on the planet's surface—industrial slag fused into cliff-like masses, plastics welded into "plastiglomerate" by beach fires, concrete outweighing all living biomass—the less useful that boundary becomes. These substances are not intrusions into geology; they are geology, produced by the same forces of burial, heating, and cementation that make sandstone, only accelerated from aeons into decades. The trouble is not that humans have added new objects to the rock record. It is that the categories with which we sort the natural from the artificial were never as stable as they seemed.

One response is to treat this collapse as merely terminological. On this view, whether a slag conglomerate "counts" as a rock is a matter of definitional convenience, and geology can proceed by simply widening its lexicon to admit "anthropogenic geomaterials." The classification changes; the science does not. This is tidy, but it underestimates what is at stake. Geology has always been a historical discipline: it reads present strata as evidence of absent processes, inferring vanished climates and extinct organisms from the traces they left. A category is not a mere label here—it is an instruction about what a trace can be made to testify to. To call something a rock is to license certain inferences about the depth of time it encodes.

That is where the difficulty sharpens. A future geologist encountering the Workington slag would face materials that mimic the signatures of slow deep-time processes while having formed in a human lifetime. The very cues that make strata legible—hardness, layering, the appearance of great age—would now be unreliable guides to duration. What such a geologist would recover is not primarily a record of climate or chemistry, but a record of *us*: our metallurgy, our waste, our habits of extraction, fossilised as surely as any mollusc shell. The rock's testimony has, in effect, changed subject.

None of this warrants the romantic conclusion that the natural and the artificial were always illusions. Tectonic uplift proceeds whether or not humans exist; slag does not. The point is narrower and, arguably, more unsettling: the distinction between natural and human processes remains real at the level of *causes*, yet dissolves at the level of *products*. Two substances can be causally distinct and materially indistinguishable. Geology can keep its lexicon or revise it, but either choice leaves untouched the deeper adjustment—that the discipline must now read its own strata as, in part, a document about the reader.
