[PASSAGE]

Moral titles appear to describe conduct, but they also establish its rank. To call someone a hero is to distinguish that person from others whose decency, though acknowledged, receives less ceremonial treatment. Heroic exceptionalists would reserve this distinction for extraordinary achievement: Omaha Beach on D-Day, June 6, 1944, supplies the military standard, while Carlyle’s figures supply its world-shaping ambition. Danger alone is insufficient. What matters is danger borne in accomplishing something whose consequence exceeds the familiar purposes of an occupation. Sacrificial egalitarians, by contrast, find ample warrant in the COVID-19 clinician’s acceptance of grave exposure for strangers; employment does not abolish courage, and remuneration is not ordinarily understood to purchase unlimited willingness to die. Both positions possess a certain dignity. The exceptionalist protects praise from becoming a complimentary synonym for attendance; the egalitarian protects it from an admiration so selective that ordinary people can qualify only by entering history. Apparently, the remaining task is to compare the dangers of the beach and the ward, allowing suitably for their consequences. Yet these standards already serve different purposes. One elevates an actor within history; the other expresses gratitude for a burden accepted. More exact comparisons of danger cannot make historical elevation and moral gratitude identical.

At hospital entrances, this divergence might seem merely a disagreement over the wording of a welcome. “Welcome healthcare heroes!” reads one sign described in “Are Doctors Heroes?” The exceptionalist hears an extravagant claim about stature. The egalitarian hears overdue recognition. Each finds the other deficient in taste. But the welcome travels: Mattel’s #ThankYouHeroes toys and the public clanging of pots extend it beyond any private exchange of thanks. Institutions acquire a vocabulary for addressing exposed workers. Its generosity is quite compatible with leaving their conditions unchanged. Calling assigned danger heroic can honour the person enduring it while making shortages and inadequate protection appear as opportunities for nobility. The title does not simply identify a virtue; its public conferment helps establish what endurance may be expected.

The nurse passing the entrance screening before returning to contracted, hazardous work resists both accounts in their stricter forms. Her shift need not alter history; nor does her employment permit the comfortable description of her exposure as wholly voluntary sacrifice. She still has patients to attend. Withholding admiration because neither definition fits would display an impressive fidelity to terminology at the expense of attention. Carlyle’s heroes, in On Heroes, Hero-worship and the Heroic in History, are “modellers, patterns, and in a wide sense creators” of collective attainment. Such authority seems to settle her exclusion, until the criterion’s purpose is recalled: it singles out makers of events, not exemplary bearers of burdens. Choosing between them changes both who receives notice and whether that notice acknowledges an outstanding debt.

Neither definition, then, supplies a neutral instrument for placing soldiers and clinicians at their appropriate heights. Both have treated a public honour too readily as a discoverable property. The nurse’s reception concerns something more consequential than her eligibility: how praise frames her duty, acknowledges her exposure, or excuses its continuation. These are commitments made partly by those welcoming her, and their force persists when she returns to work under that description.
