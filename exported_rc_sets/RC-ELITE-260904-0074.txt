The attribution dates from an 1893 museum catalogue, which took a single line in a surviving Cremonese account book — a payment recorded in 1721 to the workshop associated with Carlo Bergonzi — and converted it into a sentence about authorship. The instrument sits behind glass: a small viol, worn at the ribs, its neck joined to the body by a graft anyone can see from a metre away. The label calls it rebuilt. Two claims are stacked there as if they were one. That a payment was made in 1721 is documented; that the graft in front of the visitor is what the payment bought is not, and the catalogue never distinguished between them.

Cremonese workshop books of the period logged commissions, not craftsmen. An entry names the person paying and the sum owed, sometimes the instrument, rarely the hand that did the work. The vocabulary is thinner still. *Racconciatura* — refitting — covers a run of jobs whose only common feature is that a customer wanted an instrument playable again: new gut, a replacement peg, a bridge cut down, a soundpost reset, and, at the far end, a neck opened and re-seated. Reading such a book is like auditing a merchant's accounts where every disbursement is entered as "expenses." The total is honest. The apportionment is missing, and cannot be recovered from the ledger by any amount of squinting at it. What one can recover is the sum. The 1721 payment is small — comparable, in the same book, to entries for stringing and adjustment, and well under what the same workshop charged elsewhere for structural work. A drama is available in the word *refitting*. It is not funded by the number beside it.

Around 1720 pitch standards across northern Italy were unsettled; Venetian and Roman practice diverged enough that an instrument travelling between cities needed restringing and often a bridge and nut recut. The Cremona viol shows exactly that kind of adjustment. Wood is the awkward part. Dendrochronological dating of the neck's spruce places its felling decades before the ledger entry — evidence read one way by conservators who date structural interventions from material, and another by archival historians who prefer the written record and treat wood dates as terminus post quem and nothing more. The disagreement is not decorative. Provenance now sets insurance value, loan conditions, and which instruments get played. A graft attributed to Bergonzi's shop carries a price; a graft of unknown date carries a caution.

The label is unchanged. So is the viol. What the 1721 line documents is a bill. One conservator, examining the joint in 2011, described the graft's tool marks as inconsistent with the workshop's late practice and recorded the observation without further claim — a properly small statement about a properly small thing. The catalogue made a larger one for it. The invoice was for adjusting the instrument to a pitch it had to meet; the graft it was thought to prove predates the invoice. What survives is a record of maintenance, and the more interesting question is not who rebuilt the viol but why a museum needed it rebuilt at all — since a document that records only what was paid will, if pressed hard enough, appear to say who made the thing.
