[PASSAGE]

Ask a chess grandmaster why she sacrificed the knight, and she will tell you a story. She saw the weakness on the long diagonal; she judged the initiative worth a piece; she trusted her opponent to flinch. It is a lovely story, and I have no wish to spoil the dinner by doubting it. The trouble is that the story explains too much and predicts too little. Give me a hundred grandmasters and a hundred such positions and I will show you the sacrifice appearing at roughly the same rate whether or not any particular player consulted the long diagonal at all. The reasons people offer are sincere, various, and beautifully articulated, and they float above the regularity like the smell of good cooking above a kitchen that would produce the same meal without it. I am not saying the players are lying. I am saying their reasons are a running commentary on a game whose moves were already constrained by the board. We reach, understandably, for the agent — the deliberating, choosing, reason-giving self — because that is who we can interview. The interview is charming. It is also, I suspect, the least reliable witness in the building, precisely because it is the one that always has something to say.

So perhaps the thing to explain is not the chooser but the distribution of choices. Drop the interview. Watch the pattern instead.

Consider markets. Consider queues. Consider birds. Prices settle. Lines find their length. Flocks bank as one. No bird decides the turn. Each obeys three dull local rules, and the elegance is the sum. The equilibrium needs no author. It needs only agents too simple to have a story.

And here the neat picture buckles, which is the interesting part. For the equilibrium account, having banished the deliberating chooser, quietly smuggles back a chooser of its own — the system — and grants it exactly the coherence it denied the individual. The flock does not deliberate, true. But when I say the market "finds" a price or the queue "seeks" its length, I have not eliminated purpose; I have relocated it, from the visible agent I could interrogate to an invisible one I cannot. That is a strange trade for a skeptic to make. The local rule was innocent. The global claim is not, because the local rules were never asked to add up to a thing that wants. What the agency-talk loses is real: it treats the running commentary as the cause, and mistakes fluency for control. What the systems-talk cannot see is also real: at the level where the pattern lives, the deliberating self has vanished entirely, and with it any account of the one moment that will not average out — the move no distribution predicted, made by a player who, this once, meant it. Neither picture holds that moment. Both explain around it. I may be missing something, but I do not think either of them is looking where it fell.
