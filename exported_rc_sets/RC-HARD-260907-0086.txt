[PASSAGE]

The practice of range-rating a bus dates to the diesel era, when a coach's advertised mileage was a fuel-tank arithmetic problem and nobody expected weather to enter into it. We inherited that habit and the confidence with it. So when a northern agency I have worked with put its first battery coaches into winter service — rated near 240 kilometres, and returning under 140 on the coldest mornings — the shortfall was read as a heater problem, since heaters are what make coaches cold or warm and cost energy either way. That reading was wrong, or rather it was too small. The gap held steady across drivers with very different pedal habits, across a downtown circulator and a long suburban run, and across the days when the cabin was barely heated at all.

Cold does reduce range. Every operator knows it and every schedule already pays for it: reaction rates inside the cell slow, the cabin draws power that would otherwise go to the wheels, and winter timetables absorb a penalty of perhaps a fifth. Think of the pack as a house in January. The rooms are colder, so the furnace runs; the pipes carry water more sluggishly; you close off the back bedroom and live in less of the house than you paid for. That is derating, and it is gradual, and it is budgeted. What made the northern numbers interesting is that the house appeared to have lost a floor.

An engineer at the manufacturer put loggers on six buses. Cell-level temperature. Cell-level voltage. GPS, door counts, load. Then the question, asked flatly in her notes and left standing there: is this ordinary winter loss, or is it something else wearing winter's coat? The instruments could see the shortfall arrive. They could not yet say why.

What the logs showed was that the determining variable was not anything that happened on the road. It was pack temperature at the moment charging began. Buses parked overnight in unheated depots started the morning with cells in the low single digits, and the battery management system — the term migrated into buses from consumer laptops, where it once meant little more than a low-charge warning, and before that from aerospace power conditioning — imposes at that point not a gradual penalty but a hard restriction: charging current cut, available discharge power capped, by threshold rather than by degrees. On a twelve-minute circulator with the heat off, throttling still occurred. The buses were not spending their charge. They were never receiving it, and never permitted to spend what they had.

Below roughly five degrees, lithium ions move through the electrolyte and across the electrode interface far more reluctantly, so pushing current in risks plating metallic lithium on the anode and pulling current out risks voltage collapse; the management system, doing exactly what it was designed to do, protects the cells by refusing both. Nothing is missing from the pack. The energy is in there, in the house, behind a door that cold has made too stiff to open at speed. Which is why the fix that works is not a bigger battery or a milder timetable but preconditioning — warming the pack from grid power in the last hour before departure, the side consideration that turns out to be the whole business.
