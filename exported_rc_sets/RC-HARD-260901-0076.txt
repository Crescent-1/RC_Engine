The template dates to October 17, 2015, mid-Synod, on the New York Times op-ed page: roughly nine hundred words under the headline "The Plot to Change Catholicism." No footnotes. No links to the synodal *relatio*. A column, not a brief.

It is tempting to read the column's shape as pure conviction — the writer thought the Pope wrong and said so with heat. That is half right and therefore misleading. The op-ed form asks for an antagonist within a fixed count, and a synod is a slow, procedural, multilingual argument about the discipline of the sacraments. Something had to be cut. What survived the cutting was a verb: *plot*.

Watch the ladder as it is climbed. Rung one: a disclaimer of lay standing, a deference so carefully phrased that it purchases license for everything after. Rung two: a summary of synodal maneuvering, accurate in outline, selective in emphasis, sourced to no quoted paragraph. Rung three: schism. Each rung is narrower than the one below and rests on less. It would be an error, though, to take that internal ascent as the history it describes; the sequence belongs to the argument, not to the Synod, whose actual sessions produced revisions and votes that fit no such curve. Within four years the shape was everywhere — the credential, the compressed maneuver, the warning — reproduced on Catholic commentary sites, in Anglican blog polemics, in Orthodox-convert Substacks, in at least one Southern Baptist quarterly that borrowed the ladder wholesale for its own convention fights.

The column is still cited, unrevised, as evidence in disputes about Francis's legacy, and its lack of primary quotation now reads as a genre marker rather than a gap — the sound of somebody who knows. Against which: surely engaged criticism need not cite curial documents to be legitimate. Newman quoted little; the Fathers argued from memory. Granting that, the two cases are not parallel, and the disanalogy is what matters. Newman's authority came from having demonstrably read the sources and expected his readers to check. Here the omission does the opposite work: it manufactures interiority. The column is credited with access precisely because it shows none, and that trick, unlike an argument, can be copied by anyone with a keyboard and no access at all.

So the dispute has been miscast from the start. Readers have spent a decade asking whether Douthat was right about Francis — whether the annulment reforms and *Amoris Laetitia* vindicated him — when the column's rhetorical construction makes that question unanswerable from the text. A document engineered for circulation cannot be graded for adjudication; it did not attempt the second task. Take the schism warning specifically: nothing in those nine hundred words specifies what would count as schism, which canonical threshold, which body. Later events may have unsettled some readers, and a reader may privately feel confirmed. The column cannot be credited with having argued them there, because it argued nothing that could be checked. That is the verdict, and it holds. It leaves standing a question the column itself provoked and never faced — whether nine hundred syndicated words about a Church council can carry documentary weight at all, or whether the form is built to be quoted rather than examined.
