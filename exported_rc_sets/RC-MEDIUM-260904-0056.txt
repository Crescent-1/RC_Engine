[PASSAGE]

A record of danger is not the same object as a safe child. Northbridge County's 2018 rule made the first easy to produce. Consider one sequence. A school counsellor takes a disclosure on a Tuesday morning and rings the central desk; the desk logs it within four minutes, assigns a case number, and routes it to triage. Triage sorts it. A welfare visit follows nineteen days later. Every hand-off is timestamped, every officer named, and the file is exemplary. Nothing in it is a lapse of the sort the rule was written to prevent — no suppression, no informal decision, no allegation quietly parked with a headteacher. And yet a reader who follows only the timestamps might mistake a well-documented delay for an accident, or for the rule's typical product.

The belief the rule embodies is old and not foolish. Local discretion had failed in familiar ways: schools shading allegations downward to protect reputations, thresholds varying by borough and temperament, a child's safety resting on whether one adult in the building had the nerve to make a call. Centralised mandatory reporting was to fix all four. Every disclosure enters the same channel. The threshold is written down. Responsibility becomes locatable because the channel remembers who touched what and when. Specialists, not sympathetic strangers, assess the risk. Stated that way the policy sounds like a complete net; the claim it actually makes is narrower, being a claim about intake.

The new study is best read as a study of that narrowness. Researchers reconstructed 312 referrals from the rule's first eighteen months, drawing on school logs, central-desk timestamps, police occurrence books, social-work case files, and interviews with parents, counsellors, and caseworkers. Each referral was cut into intervals: disclosure to referral, referral to triage decision, triage to first contact, first contact to protective action. Sources were then checked against one another, so that a step recorded once but absent from the parallel file, or entered retroactively after a review, could be flagged. This is careful clockwork. It measures the movement of paperwork through stages, and infers from those stages when a child was reached — which is not the same as measuring whether the child was safer afterwards.

The findings split along that seam. Unlogged and rejected referrals fell sharply; where a referral stalled, the study could name the desk it stalled at, which under the old arrangement was frequently impossible. Median time from disclosure to a child's first protective contact barely moved. Triage queues and staffing shortfalls had not been removed by centralisation; they had been shifted downstream, past the point the rule was designed to govern. The two improvements are therefore not parallel achievements: one belongs to the intake stage the rule reformed, the other to stages it left alone. Limits matter here. One county, eighteen months, and outcome data thin enough that recurrence rates could not be estimated at all. The verdict follows from that. Northbridge's rule improved the record of danger, and on the present evidence it did that and no more; whether it improved protection depends on whether the later stages catch up, and asking that of the rule now is asking a question the study was not built to settle.
