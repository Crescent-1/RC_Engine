[PASSAGE]

Ask a good doctor whether early screening saves lives, and the answer arrives fast: yes, obviously, catch the tumor small and you catch it in time. Every year I hear some version of this, and it has the ring of settled sense. The individual case is legible. A patient walks in, a scan finds a shadow, the shadow comes out, the patient walks home. What could be plainer? This is the humane view, the bedside view, and I will grant it its full force before I quarrel with anything. At the scale of one frightened person on one examining table, screening earlier is simply better medicine. That claim does real work, and I do not intend to insult it.

The trouble begins the moment you count. Screening a healthy population turns up findings that would never have troubled anyone—slow lesions, indolent growths, the biological equivalent of a rattle in an engine that will outlast the car. The rival framing, which the epidemiologists press hard, calls this overdiagnosis, and it sounds like the responsible position: treat fewer, harm fewer, spare the anxious biopsies. Notice that framing carefully, because it is not mine, though I will let it speak. Here is my claim, stated plainly: a benefit that is real for each patient can be unreal for the patients taken together. Screening does something for the person that it does not do for the population. The bedside verdict and the ledger verdict are both honest, and they point opposite ways.

Sharpen it. The benefit is not merely diluted at scale; it can reverse sign. A test that saves this man's life can, run across a million men, add more deaths from the treatments than it subtracts from the disease. Same intervention. Same logic. The arithmetic simply changes hands.

Now, the fair objection. Surely, someone says, this is just a technical problem—better tests, sharper thresholds, and the two scales snap back into agreement. Refine the tool and the individual good becomes the aggregate good, as it always should have been. I want to concede as much of this as I honestly can, because the impulse is right and much of medicine has improved exactly this way. Yes: some scale gaps are fixable, and pretending otherwise is lazy. But concede too much and you miss what the concession quietly surrenders. The technical fix assumes the two verdicts want to converge. My whole point is that they need not. The gap is not always noise waiting to be cleaned up. Sometimes it is the shape of the thing.

So sharpen once more, and surrender the comfort. The individual claim is true. The aggregate claim is true. They do not meet. There is no altitude from which both resolve, only a choice of which one you will act on today. I have spent a career signing off on protocols written for populations and then watching them applied, one body at a time, to people who are not populations. That is the vantage this whole argument was written from—the desk where the ledger lands and the patient waits. From there you learn the ugly thing early. Choosing the scale is not a preliminary to the decision. It is the decision. And nobody hands you the scale.
