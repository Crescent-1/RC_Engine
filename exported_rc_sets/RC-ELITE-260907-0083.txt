Was a man's failure to see at night his own fault, and how much of the sea's disorder was it made to explain? For the better part of a century the answer was settled with admirable economy: night blindness ran with diet and constitution, a defect the seaman carried aboard with his kit. The Board of Trade circulars phrased it with the courtesy of the period — a want of fresh provisions, a certain class of man — and the reach of the explanation was magnificent. Lookouts who reported nothing until the bow wave was under them. Collisions in convoy that nobody had seen coming. Men lost off the rigging on moonless nights, their footing gone. Against all of it stood the trimmer on a coastal collier, well fed ashore a fortnight earlier, who could not find the ladder. Is such blindness a fixed property of the man, or of the night he happens to be standing in?

The 1943 dark-adaptation work of the Admiralty put the matter, at last, on an instrument. Survivors of torpedoed ships were taken from hospital wards and seated before an adaptometer, their thresholds recorded in minutes and foot-lamberts rather than inferred from a symptom sheet or from an officer's estimate of a man's character. That much was progress, and it answered the earlier question by removing it from opinion. It answered rather less than the writing-up implied. What the instrument caught was a man three days off a raft, burned, dehydrated, sleepless — and to ask whether his diet had ruined his rods is to have mistaken the argument for one about the seaman's body when it is really an argument about the conditions under which anybody's rods were ever measured at sea.

The investigators saw the confound and worked at it, which is more than their predecessors managed: thresholds retaken over successive days as the men recovered, a comparison group drawn from crews on the same convoy routes who had not been sunk, adjustment for age and for months of prior night-lookout duty. This mattered to more parties than the Admiralty. Shipowners, insurers, the men's own unions and the schools that certified them had all leaned, for decades, on the proposition that vision was a fitness of the individual. Then the corrected figures came in. Watch rotation dominated. Recent light exposure dominated. Diet fell away. A man who had spent forty minutes in a lit alleyway before relieving the lookout adapted worse than a man half his age advantage who had come up in darkness — which leaves the older doctrine tracking something, but nobody could yet say what.

Read the thing as a customs declaration rather than a proof: the tables list what passed the counter, not what the ship carried. Nearly all the men came off one class of vessel; nearly all reached the adaptometer because a hospital had admitted them, and wartime admission was itself a filter nobody designed. So the finding stands and does not close. The Admiralty owes an account of whether diet contributes at all or merely contributes invisibly under blackout conditions that swamp it; the older doctrine owes an account of why its predictions worked as often as they did, if the thing it named was never the thing it measured; and until one of those debts is discharged, the trimmer on the collier stays where he was, unable to find the ladder for reasons neither party has yet earned the right to name.
