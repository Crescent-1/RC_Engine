# Reading Comprehension Passage (Medium Difficulty)

For over a century, the question of who the ancient Indians were remained trapped in a stalemate between colonial-era migration theories and nationalist reassertions of pure indigeneity. The nineteenth-century notion that Indo-Aryan-speaking pastoralists arrived in the subcontinent from Central Asia around 1500 BCE was, initially, uncontroversial—even early Indian historians folded it into accounts of a composite civilization. But the mid-twentieth-century discovery of the Indus Valley Civilization, an urban society predating the Vedas, destabilized this consensus. A politically charged indigenist movement countered with the "Out of India" theory, insisting that Vedic culture and its languages had always been native to the subcontinent, radiating outward rather than arriving from elsewhere.

Into this deadlock has stepped ancient DNA analysis, and its arrival has been treated, not unreasonably, as a kind of epistemological rescue. Genome sequencing from skeletal remains across Eurasia has traced a layered ancestry for South Asians: a deep foundational population descended from the original out-of-Africa migrants, a subsequent admixture with Iranian-related agriculturalists that underpinned the Indus cities, and finally an infusion of Steppe pastoralist ancestry arriving around 2000–1500 BCE, correlating with the spread of Indo-European languages. Where philology and archaeology had produced competing, ideologically inflected narratives, genetics appeared to offer something closer to verifiable fact—hard numbers extracted from bone rather than contested inscriptions.

Yet this optimism deserves scrutiny. Population genetics necessarily reduces individuals to genomes and genomes to statistical signals, a method well suited to tracking biological movement but structurally blind to the human dimensions of history—power, ideology, language dominance, and the choices that shaped how populations actually mixed. A DNA sample can confirm that two groups interbred; it cannot say whether that mixing was conquest, trade, or coexistence, nor explain why caste endogamy later hardened genetic boundaries that had once been fluid. Treating genomic data as the final word risks a subtler danger than the old ideological distortions: the pseudo-scientific "biologizing" of identity, where social and cultural categories are mistaken for biological ones simply because they now come wrapped in the authority of a laboratory.

The resolution, then, is not to discard genetics but to discipline its claims. Ancient DNA has genuinely narrowed the range of plausible migration scenarios and forced ideologically motivated theories to reckon with hard evidence. But its findings are most trustworthy when read alongside archaeology, linguistics, and historiography—disciplines attentive to the meanings, hierarchies, and choices that a genome, however precisely sequenced, was never built to record.

## Questions

**Q1. Which of the following best captures the central argument of the passage?**

C) The discovery of the Indus Valley Civilization was the primary reason indigenist "Out of India" theories gained political traction in the twentieth century.

C) Because Steppe ancestry arrived after the Indus Valley collapse, the pastoralists must have been responsible for destroying Harappan cities.

## Answer Key

**Q1: B**
Correct — The passage's structure moves from presenting genetics as an "epistemological rescue" (claim) to a critique of its reductionism (pivot) to a final synthesis urging genetics be "disciplined" by other disciplines. This option captures both halves of that arc.
- A is a half-truth: it captures the opening claim but ignores the pivot's critique entirely.
- C is a true detail from the passage but describes a historical trigger, not the passage's overall argument.
- D overcorrects, mirroring only the pivot while ignoring the final paragraph's endorsement of genetics' genuine value.

**Q2: B**
Correct — The passage explicitly states Steppe pastoralist ancestry arrived "correlating with the spread of Indo-European languages."
- A describes the Iranian-agriculturalist admixture layer, a different phase.
- C is discussed elsewhere as something genetics can track but doesn't causally tie to Steppe arrival timing.
- D refers to the earliest, unrelated ancestral layer.

**Q3: A**
Correct — This requires two steps: (1) DNA confirms mixing occurred but not its social nature (conquest/trade/coexistence); (2) therefore, explanations of resulting hierarchies need non-genetic corroboration. This is directly supported by the passage's reasoning.
- B is an unsupported leap about sampling difficulty, not discussed in the passage.
- C invents a causal claim (pastoralists destroying cities) never made or implied.
- D contradicts the passage, which explicitly says genetics is "blind" to explaining why endogamy developed.

**Q4: B**
Correct — The critique functions as the pivot: it tempers the initial enthusiasm before the concluding paragraph offers a qualified, balanced position combining both views.
- A is too strong; the author never claims genetics is "more" biased than older theories, only that it carries a different, subtler risk.
- C is wrong direction; the critique is not marshalled in support of the indigenist theory.
- D is an extreme distractor contradicted by the final paragraph's explicit endorsement of continued genetic research.

**Q5: A**
Correct — The passage uses "biologizing" to describe mistaking social/cultural categories for biological ones under the guise of scientific authority.
- B, C, and D are literal or unrelated misreadings not supported by the surrounding sentence, which is about identity categories, not literal lab or political processes.

**Q6: C**
Correct — The author calls DNA analysis "not unreasonably" celebrated, praises its narrowing of debate, yet devotes a full paragraph to its limits — the hallmark of cautious, qualified appreciation rather than pure endorsement or rejection.
- A and B represent the two extremes the passage explicitly avoids.
- D is inaccurate since the author clearly evaluates and takes a position, rather than remaining neutral.
