Tuning, in bell-founding, does not mean what it means for a string. A string is tuned after it exists; a bell is tuned before it does, in clay, by a man with a board. The founder came to the town with a mule, a bag of tallow, and a pattern of profiles, dug a pit beside the church deep enough to swallow a man standing, and began the core: brick laid dry in a beehive, packed and smeared with loam, then shaved down as it dried by a wooden sweep-board pivoting on an iron spindle set in the pit's floor. Theophilus, in the twelfth-century treatise on the various arts, describes the spindle and the board plainly, as though nothing about them required argument.

Over the finished core came the false bell. Wax and tallow in warm layers, built up by hand and then cut back by a second sweep-board whose edge carried the outer profile, so that what lay between the two boards was not a bell but the future thickness of one. Letters were pressed into the wax while it stayed workable — the founder's name, a saint's, the year — along with relief bands at the shoulder and waist. These read as ornament, and they were ornament. They were also metal that would be there, at that thickness, at that height, and thickness at height is what a bell's tone is. Nothing about the pitch was decided after this stage. It was decided here, in grease.

Then the cope: loam troweled over the wax in coats, dried between each, bound with iron bands. Fire under the whole assembly. The wax ran out through channels cut for it and was caught in pans. What remained was a cavity. Not a mould waiting to be adjusted — the adjustment had already happened, above ground, in daylight, with a board. The cavity was a decision already made, now buried.

The pour took a quarter of an hour. The furnace was tapped, and bronze ran down clay gutters into the pit in one motion, and the founder judged when to open it by the colour of the metal and the way it moved — too cold and it would freeze short of the crown, too hot and it would eat the loam. Nineteenth-century metallurgists writing about bell alloys treated this as guesswork to be replaced by the pyrometer; the surviving founding families treated it as the trade itself, and refused to write it down. Both camps agreed on the word: the founder was said to *know his metal*, a phrase that travelled out of the pits into the glass-houses and then into iron, where it kept the same meaning and lost the mule.

Consider what had to be true for a bell to ring in tune. Every dimension governing its pitch was fixed underground, in wax and loam, before any sound existed to check against. Strike-note, hum, the tierce — all of it settled by hand and eye in a pit. One might want to say the risk was shared evenly between the shaping and the pour. It was not. A bad pour wasted metal; a bad profile wasted metal, wax, loam, iron, weeks, and the pit. And this stayed true until a bell could be cast thick and then cut thinner on a lathe — after which the tuner worked in the light, shaving bronze off the inside of a finished bell, listening.
