# READING COMPREHENSION

**Passage:**

The promise of ancient DNA was that it would settle arguments historians had circled for a century. Where texts could be read tendentiously and pottery shards assembled into whatever story the excavator preferred, a genome seemed to offer something recalcitrant — a fact that would not bend to nationalist appetite. Populations either mixed or they did not; the alleles do not care who is offended. For a certain kind of scholar, exhausted by the interminable quarrels over origins, this was liberation.

It was also, on inspection, a category confusion dressed as a discovery.

Consider what a genome actually records. It tracks the movement of bodies, the timing of admixture, the eventual hardening of endogamy into what would later be called caste. These are real signals, and dismissing them because they are politically inconvenient is its own kind of bad faith. The indigenist who denies that Steppe pastoralists ever arrived is not defending method; he is refusing evidence. So far the geneticists are on firm ground. The trouble begins when the signal is asked to carry meaning it never contained. That two populations mixed tells us nothing about whether the mixing was conquest, commerce, marriage, or slow demographic seepage over centuries nobody experienced as an event. The DNA is mute precisely where history becomes interesting — at the level of power, coercion, prestige, and the choices, however constrained, of the people doing the mixing.

Here one expects the humanist's rejoinder: that culture escapes biology, that meaning cannot be sequenced. True enough, but the rejoinder conceals a second error, its mirror image. For if genetics overreaches by biologizing the social, the older text-based historiography routinely committed the reverse sin — treating the categories of the Rig Veda, or the self-descriptions of literate elites, as though they transparently reported the demographic realities of a subcontinent most of whose inhabitants left no words at all. The texts were not neutral either. They were the propaganda of the winners, and reading history off them was never the innocent alternative to reading it off the genome. Both methods purchase clarity by amputation. One discards the body; the other discards everyone who could not write.

So the corrective needs correcting.

What the genetic turn genuinely alters is not the answer to the old question but the standing of the question itself. To ask for the *origin* of a people — a single stream, a founding stock, a moment before which they were not and after which they were — is to import a grammar the evidence increasingly refuses. There is no bottom. Push the First Indians back sixty-five thousand years and you find them already arriving from somewhere; push further and the very unit whose origin you sought dissolves into a process that was never anywhere at rest. Origin-talk assumes a stillness that the data denies at every depth you probe.

And yet — this is the part the debunkers miss — the stillness cannot simply be discarded, because the political present demands it. A modern nation cannot organize loyalty around a crucible that never settled. It needs an origin the way a court needs a plaintiff. The scholarly recognition that peoples are processes and the civic requirement that they be things are not going to be reconciled by better data, because they are not, at bottom, disagreements about data. The genome answered a question. It could not tell us why we insisted on asking it, or why, having been answered, we go on asking it still.

# Why this RC is difficult

**Hidden Structural Shifts:**
The passage runs a four-stage movement that keeps overturning the reader's provisional resolution. (1) Genetics as liberation from tendentious history. (2) Immediate reversal — genetics is "category confusion," mute where history matters. A reader who freezes here concludes the passage is anti-genetics. (3) The humanist rejoinder is raised *and then itself undercut* — texts are equally amputating. Readers who anchor on "both methods amputate" freeze into a false-symmetry reading. (4) The real pivot ("So the corrective needs correcting"): the target is not either method but *origin-talk itself*. Then a final twist — origin-talk cannot merely be discarded because the political present *requires* it. The ending relocates the tension from epistemology to the irreconcilability of scholarly and civic functions. Readers freeze most often at stage 2 (thinking the essay debunks DNA) or stage 3 (thinking the essay preaches methodological balance).

**Core Distractor Traps:**
- Q1(A)/(D) anchor on early or late fragments — (A) mistakes stage-1 framing plus a check; (D) adopts the "abandon origins" reading the passage explicitly complicates in its final paragraph.
- Q1(C) is the seductive false-synthesis trap: the passage denies reconciliation "by better data."
- Q2(C)/(D) misassign the pivot as endorsing one method.
- Q3(A)/(C)/(D) all read the "propaganda" line locally as a factual charge against texts, missing its structural role of destroying the *neutrality* of the textual alternative.
- Q4(A)/(D) are real passage claims but address the wrong mechanism; only (C) targets the inference from admixture to peacefulness.
- Q5(A)/(D) import external political theories; (C) reverses the passage.
- Q6(A)/(B)/(D) collapse dialectical suspension into partisanship, adjudication, or despair.

**Tonal Ambiguity:**
The author appears anti-genetics, then anti-humanist, then anti-origin, then defends the persistence of origin-talk as politically inescapable. The stance is never endorsement — each position is granted validity only to the depth needed to expose its boundary. The closing sentences withhold resolution deliberately: the genome "could not tell us why we insisted on asking."
