The term "genetic counselling" was coined in 1947 by Sheldon Reed at the Dight Institute in Minneapolis, and for the first two decades it named a plain activity. A counsellor sits with a couple. Their first child has phenylketonuria. He works the arithmetic — autosomal recessive, one in four — and then he tells them what he thinks they should do. Not coldly. He has read the file, he knows the mother is thirty-four, he knows what a second affected child would cost this household. The pedigree chart is drawn by hand on graph paper, and he turns it toward them so they can see. Nobody in the room thinks anything improper is happening. This was competent medicine, and by the standards of the day it was generous.

Reopen the same file twenty years later and a second pattern is legible. The couple came back in 1954, and again in 1957, with the identical question. Each time the arithmetic was performed correctly. Each time the recommendation was the same, and each time it was delivered. What no page records is anyone asking the wife what she wanted, or whether the number meant to her what it meant to him.

Through the 1960s the ground shifted underneath the practice without anyone quite moving: state sterilization statutes were repealed or quietly stopped being enforced, newborn PKU screening spread from Massachusetts outward, chromosomal diagnosis of Down syndrome became routine, and parents began to write things down. Some of them were the grown children of the earlier era's patients. Their letters and testimony describe being directed out of parenthood by men who had shown them a fraction and never taught them how to hold it; one in four, and no account of what the other three would have been. Here the old camps hardened. The Minneapolis school held that a trained man who withholds his judgment is withholding the only thing he has. The critics answered that a recommendation offered with a pedigree chart is not advice but instruction.

By the early 1970s the ethics discussions inside the American Society of Human Genetics were using "genetic counselling" in a second sense, and the two senses could not be reconciled. Counselling now meant: give the information, decline the recommendation. So the phrase had been carrying two opposed jobs all along. Not a practice that reformed itself — rather a word that changed employers.

Which is the vantage from which the whole sequence has to be read. The mid-1970s consensus statements made non-directiveness a professional norm, and I grant them their best day: the norm is honest about what the counsellor cannot know. But consider a dinner host who insists guests serve themselves, having decided what is on the table, in what order the dishes arrive, and which of them sits within reach. The counsellor must still choose which risk to raise first, how long to dwell on it, whether to mention the one-in-four before or after the description of what phenylketonuria does to an untreated child. Those choices steer. They are simply no longer called steering. I may be missing something, but the tidy verdict — old advice bad, new silence good — will not survive the graph paper, still in the folder, still turned toward the couple.
