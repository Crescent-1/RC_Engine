Arnoud Visser, in a study published in July 2026, investigates the stock figure of the pedant and his association with individual superiority, social distinction, and sexual transgression — and beginning from Prudenzio, the vain schoolmaster of Francesco Belo's El pedante, he states the claim flatly: these men are not simply failed scholars held up for laughter, but devices for showing how displayed learning turns into power over other people. The claim is put to the test.

The test proceeds in a sequence those of us trained on this material can follow without difficulty. First, each display of learning is identified — a tag of Latin, a citation, a lecture nobody asked for. Then the listeners are recorded: who is obliged to sit still for it, who defers, who is silenced. Then the speech is followed forward into what it produces, a decision taken, a misunderstanding sown, a household rearranged. Last comes the reckoning of outcome: humiliation, exclusion, a sexual complication. Erudition is tracked out of language and into conduct.

Run across the erudite comedies, the pattern holds. Calandra, first performed in 1513, already has a tutor whose intellectual pomposity is unmistakable; Belo's play of the 1520s gives the pedant the centre of the stage; the later Italian comedies inherited the house and added rooms. In each, the Latin is conspicuous and the jargon thick, which makes it tempting to say the language is the joke. But the damage arrives at a particular moment — when the learning is used to rank a rival, to blame a servant, to control a marriage. The speech is the instrument, not the injury.

Suppose a pedant mounts a lectern and expounds some remote point of Greek prosody. Nobody understands him; nobody is deceived, punished, or displaced. The method would record a display and then find nothing downstream. Obscurity alone, on this showing, does not generate the satire.

Why the pattern recurs is not hard to see. The pedant's learning asserts superiority while remaining wholly dependent on the household, the patron, or the schoolroom that pays for it — and such dependents can always demonstrate, in an afternoon, that the man cannot manage a debt or a dinner. This is why failed scholarship, and not authority in some abstract sense, remains the visible material of the stage. The comedies mock learning; what they mock it for is its pretension to rule.

Prudenzio repays a second look on these terms. He is scruffy. He talks in a mangled mix of Latin and vernacular. He presents himself as a scholarly genius and behaves like a blundering buffoon — and the polarities Visser draws out, learning against ignorance, grand speech against banal conduct, are not two jokes but one joke at two moments. His erudition licenses him to instruct, to judge, to interfere in matters of courtship and money. Then it fails him at every practical turn. The completion of the joke lies in that distance.

The old belief survives, then, as an account of the surface target: intellectual pretension is what audiences saw being mocked. The engine beneath it is the passage from erudition to imposed superiority and from there to practical collapse, so that Prudenzio's grand Latin, once heard as the whole of his absurdity, opens instead onto his conduct toward the household he presumes to govern — though a wider corpus than these Italian scripts would be needed before the reading could be called general.
