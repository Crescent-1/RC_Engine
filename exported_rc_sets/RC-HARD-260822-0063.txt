At a small desk beside the stage, usually lit by a hooded lamp, the surtitler works from a prepared translation of the libretto. By surtitles, here, we mean the brief lines projected above or beside the action, not the printed translation in a programme. The translator first renders the text; the surtitler then divides that rendering into screens, marks them against the score, and advances them during performance. A cue may be a chord, an entrance, a raised hand. The machinery is modest. The labour appears settled. The words seem to have been translated before the curtain rose.

Rehearsal disturbs that account. A literal sentence may require three screens, while the singer gives its crucial preposition twelve seconds of musical air; two characters may overlap so that their separately intelligible remarks become, in English, an illegible column; a joke may arrive only after the orchestra has already delivered its sting. Cut the names. Join the replies. Show the consequence. Some of these alterations are plainly forced by the dimensions of the screen and by the finite speed at which most spectators can read. It would be unserious to deny that constraint. Yet the desk resembles a camera operator’s viewfinder more than a copying machine: it selects a field, holds it long enough to register, then moves before the object has ceased existing. In one rehearsal, an attendant’s repeated complaint about a missing key became “He has shut us out,” not because those words were sung, but because the locked door had become the scene’s audible fact.

The annotated score makes the criterion less permissive than that example can suggest. Its margins contain bar numbers, pencil slashes, crossings-out, and cues such as “after laugh” or “wait for look”; they record not a freedom to paraphrase but a sequence of tests applied under performance conditions. Music cannot be reduced to background. Gesture cannot be treated as redundant. Plot cannot be assumed to survive a reader’s delayed comprehension. The operator is calibrating several signals whose alignment is known only from the auditorium, not from a page. A printed comparison can therefore establish that a phrase has vanished, but it cannot by itself establish what vanished with it. Some decisions that look like mistranslation in a bilingual column preserve the timing by which an accusation, a recognition, or a silence becomes available. The score’s own economy offers a stern warning: “Surtitles are not a translation,” writes the surtitler Sonya Friedman in her practical account of the craft; they are “a guide to the action.” That guide, however, remains answerable to the words it cannot carry.

An audience may still be entitled to ask for more than guidance, and perhaps for verbal fidelity that this apparatus cannot supply. But complete fidelity has a cost that its advocates must specify rather than merely deplore: viewers would read through held notes, faces, and the small exchanges by which music alters speech. It is not enough to point to every omission on paper. One must say what a restored phrase would preserve in the theatre, at that instant. Would “I have misplaced the key” clarify more than “He has shut us out” while the singer stands at the bolted door?
