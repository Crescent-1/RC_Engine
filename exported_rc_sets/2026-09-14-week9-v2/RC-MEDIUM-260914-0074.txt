[PASSAGE]

Omri Boehm's Radical Universalism, a lucid and vivid tract arguing that philosophy is prior to politics, uses the binding of Isaac as evidence. Most criticism of the book has followed its lead. Genesis 22 unfolds in a fixed order: the command, the three-day walk, the wood laid on the son's back, the binding, the knife raised, the angel's call, the ram caught in a thicket. Boehm and his critics treat that order as an illustration of a doctrine already secured elsewhere, mostly from Kant's "What is Enlightenment?" The frame will not hold, and not because the doctrine is empty. It fails because the interruption changes what counts as obedience. As readers, we first fix on one moment in the sequence as the moment that authorizes the doctrine: for some the command, for Boehm the refusal to be determined by what is given. Every prior moment is then read as preparation for it. Every later moment is read as confirmation of it, subordinate, explanatory, not evidential in its own right. The ram becomes decoration for a decision already made about the knife. What looks like an argument drawn from the narrative is a distribution of weight across the narrative, and the certainty attaches to that distribution rather than to anything the text does on its own.

The obvious objection points to the order of events: Abraham obeys before he is stopped, so the sequence seems to confirm absolute duty. Up to the angel's speech, that is true, and obedience looks complete and self-explanatory, needing no gloss. After the speech it is no longer true in the same way. The narrative has now made the interruption part of what the command meant, so that obedience includes being prevented. This does not leave the episode indeterminate, and it is not a request for more historical context. It shows that the received frame cannot take the passage as a straightforward proof.

The episode's apparent clarity comes from a compression that the received reading never registers. Command, act and verdict are collapsed into a single moral instant, and once collapsed they seem to speak with one voice. Whether the story can ground a universal duty remains an open question, properly posed. Its answer, whatever it proves to be, cannot be read off obedience alone.

A universalist reader will charge that this reading dissolves a binding episode into mere literary ambiguity. It does not deny obligation; it locates obligation in how the interruption is handled, which is a different matter from denying that there is anything to handle — though I grant that the doctrine's authority then rests on judgment rather than on a command standing uncontested in the text.

Seen this way, Genesis 22 functions in Boehm's argument as the machinery by which a fractured sequence is converted into moral certainty, and the conversion is the book's real achievement rather than its illustration. That achievement has a price, and a precise one. A doctrine that finds its warrant in that conversion cannot also claim that its absolute duty reaches us unmediated.
