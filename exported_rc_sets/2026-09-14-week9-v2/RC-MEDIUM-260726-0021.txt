[PASSAGE]

When a hospital decides that a benefactor's name should no longer hang above one of its wards, the decision is usually defended as though the benefactor could still hear it. A Sheffield hospital took down such a brass plate in 2019, more than a century after a local firm had engraved it. In the interval, the man it honoured had acquired no new opinions, made no fresh investments and written no letters. Yet the removal was argued in the same grammar we use for a colleague who has said something indefensible at a meeting: he must answer for this, the record must be corrected, the standard applies to him as it applies to everyone. That last demand deserves its due. Standards that bend around the identity of the accused are not standards. Whatever else moral judgment is, it seems to require indifference to who is being judged, and to when. On this view the dead can claim no exemption for being dead.

Death, however, changes something that indifference cannot reach, because blame, unlike a measurement, is aimed. It is addressed to an agent who might yet do otherwise, and its whole point lies in that possibility of acting differently. Directed at someone who has stopped acting, the address fails. What is left over is not a lie but a monument, a different sort of object that cannot correct anyone.

Once this is said aloud, a series of concessions seems to follow. If the living are granted endless reinterpretation (the second chance, the changed man, the recognition that a person at fifty is not the person who acted at twenty-eight), consistency appears to demand either the same generosity for the dead or a dignified silence about them. The dead can neither recant nor be rehabilitated, so continuing to arraign them looks like beating on a locked door. It becomes easy to conclude that the decent course is amnesty, or amnesia, or the historian's careful refusal to score. That conclusion moves too fast. It treats blame as a relation between judge and judged that dissolves the moment one of them is absent.

Blame has never been that kind of relation, since it has always had an audience; the dead man is its occasion rather than its recipient.

The plate comes down, then, and it comes down rightly, though not because a Victorian ironmaster is being punished; he is beyond the reach of any sanction the ward committee can devise. It comes down because a hospital is a going concern with staff, patients and a future, and what it hangs on its walls instructs them. On this reading the demand for universal standards in the first paragraph was never wrong so much as misdescribed: the standard is universal and the sanction is not, because sanction is an instrument and instruments require something to work on. Symmetry between past and future was a grammatical illusion, produced by a verb that takes the same form in both directions. No verdict on the dead can therefore be final in the way verdicts on the living are. Arguments about such plates will keep restarting, not from indecision but because the people they are actually addressed to keep being replaced.
