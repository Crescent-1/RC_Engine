In 1783 a parish priest named Jozef Ignác Bajza carried a Slovak-language manuscript from his rectory desk into a process with several offices in it. Josephine press regulation had recently moved review out of purely ecclesiastical hands and into a state apparatus that still used clerics as readers. The manuscript passed, a Pressburg shop set the type, and Bajza paid for it himself. A second part followed in 1784-85. Publication in this period meant the whole administrative act of manuscript, imprimatur, print run and distribution, not a title page. On that measure the word has to stretch: a project of three parts, of which two were printed, is published in one sense and unpublished in another. Historians of the Slovak literary language have usually read every later stage backward through that stretched word.

The surviving record would fit on a single sheet: a Foreword lamenting the neglect of the language, a rival's pamphlet attacking Bajza's spelling, a diocesan mention of the book's reception, and a bibliographer's later record that part three never appeared. Each item is accurate, yet together they do not say why the third part stopped, and their individual cleanness is what tempts a reader to link them into a chain. What they document is an interrupted venture, a smaller and odder thing than the birth of a national epic.

Context supplies a cause for the stall, though a layered one. Slovak Catholic clergy in these decades were competing, in print and in correspondence, over whose orthography would count as the written language, so that a book was also a position in a quarrel; ecclesiastical reviewers trained in the Catholic Enlightenment's dogmatic caution read satire on clerical life with particular attentiveness; and a parish income could not underwrite a three-volume print run. Each pressure narrowed what came next. The manuscript was reviewed and printed, then contested philologically by a man who argued that its spelling alone disqualified it. Once contested, it did not sell, leaving the last volume unfunded until it was abandoned. No single office closed the door; the door had fewer and fewer hinges left.

A considerable weight has since settled onto that foreword, that hostile pamphlet and that missing manuscript. National literary historiography has claimed a founding text; communist-era readers found an anticlerical satirist inside a priest; minor-language and postcolonial scholarship has treated the case as exemplary of peripheral print; and translators have prepared editions of a work whose third part has never been read. For all this interpretive traffic, the documentary base has not grown by a page.

What left the Pressburg shop resembles a facade with two wings behind it and, where the third should stand, foundation stones and an intention. The facade is genuine masonry, not a false front. A building left in that condition, however, is described by what it announces rather than by what it encloses, and the phrase "the first Slovak novel" has always described the announcement. Bajza's contemporaries argued about the dressing of the stone, not the plan, while later readers walked past the gap without seeing it. Until the Trnava and Pressburg holdings yield something they have so far withheld, the recoverable object is not the vanished third part but the announcement itself: what a stalled building was understood, in 1783 and after, to be claiming.
