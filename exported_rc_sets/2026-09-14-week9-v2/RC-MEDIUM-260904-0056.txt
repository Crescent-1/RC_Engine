[PASSAGE]

A record of danger is not the same thing as a safe child, although reforms are often judged as if it were. Northbridge County's 2018 rule made the first easy to produce. Under the rule, a school counsellor took a disclosure on a Tuesday morning and rang the central desk. The desk logged it within four minutes, assigned a case number and sent it to triage. A welfare visit followed nineteen days later. Every hand-off was timestamped and every officer named. By the rule's own standards the file is exemplary: there was no suppression, no informal decision, no allegation quietly parked with a headteacher. A reader who follows only the timestamps could still mistake a well-documented delay for an accident, or for the rule's typical product.

The belief behind the rule is old and not foolish. Local discretion had failed in familiar ways. Schools shaded allegations downward to protect their reputations, thresholds varied by borough and temperament, and a child's safety could rest on whether one adult in the building had the nerve to make a call. Centralised mandatory reporting was meant to end those failures. Every disclosure would enter one channel under a written threshold. Responsibility would become locatable, because the channel remembers who touched what and when, and specialists rather than sympathetic strangers would assess the risk. Put that way, the policy sounds like a complete net. Its actual claim is narrower: it is a claim about intake.

The new study is best read as a study of that narrowness. Researchers reconstructed 312 referrals from the rule's first eighteen months. They drew on school logs, central-desk timestamps, police occurrence books, social-work case files, and interviews with parents, counsellors and caseworkers. Each referral was cut into four intervals: disclosure to referral, referral to triage decision, triage to first contact, and first contact to protective action. The sources were then checked against one another, so that a step recorded once but missing from the parallel file, or entered retroactively after a review, could be flagged. The method is meticulous, but what it measures is the movement of paperwork through stages, from which it infers when a child was reached, and that is not the same as measuring whether the child was safer afterwards.

The findings divide along that line. Unlogged and rejected referrals fell sharply. Where a referral stalled, the cross-checked records let the study name the desk it stalled at, which the old arrangement rarely allowed. Yet median time from disclosure to a child's first protective contact barely moved. Triage queues and staffing shortfalls had not been removed by centralisation; they had been shifted downstream, past the point the rule was designed to govern. So the two results are not parallel achievements. One belongs to the intake stage the rule reformed, and the other to stages it left alone. The evidence is also limited to one county and eighteen months, with outcome data so thin that recurrence rates could not be estimated at all. What the study supports is that Northbridge's rule improved the record of danger and did no more. Whether it improved protection depends on whether the later stages catch up, and asking that of the rule now is asking a question the study was not built to settle.
