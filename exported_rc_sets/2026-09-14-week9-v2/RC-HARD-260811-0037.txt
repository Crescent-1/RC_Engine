[PASSAGE]

Whistleblowers increasingly reach the public through documentaries, and the documentaries have settled on a form. In one, an engineer in a gray suit sits in a hotel conference room in Dayton, describing the afternoon he photocopied test data showing that the brake assemblies had never been run to failure. The soundtrack swells; a later TED talk earned him a standing ovation. Neither production dwelt on the settlement, which appeared only in a caption held for perhaps two seconds: a sum in the low eight figures under the relevant bounty statute, plus a non-prosecution agreement that closed a separate inquiry into the same man's expense filings. None of this is disputed, but it raises the question of what the ovation was for. On the available evidence, conscience arrived with counsel present and terms negotiated in advance, which describes a transaction, and sentiment applied to a transaction remains sentiment.

The retaliation data then cut in an unexpected direction. Surveys of employees who witnessed serious misconduct find that a majority say nothing, and that among those who do speak internally, something near half report demotion, exclusion or termination within two years. Where no statutory shield and no bounty exist, disclosure rates collapse toward zero regardless of how the respondents score on any instrument measuring moral conviction. This is awkward for the prosecution. The pure conscience celebrated by the mythology looks less like a scarce resource than an inoperative one. Whistleblowing does not fail for want of virtue; it functions when it is engineered, and where it is not engineered it does not function at all, which moves the question from character to design.

That finding does not, however, dispose of the moral question, and the tidy inference that the payout explains the whole act should be resisted. Some accountants and clinicians reported and then collected nothing; others collected after nine years, or collected sums exceeded within eighteen months by legal fees, lost pension vesting and the cost of moving a family out of a town where the employer was the employer. The bounty explains why such people entered the process. It does not explain why they stayed in it after the first deposition, when the calculation had visibly turned against them. What the record shows is not the restored saint of the documentaries but something less legible: a person whose participation was purchased and whose persistence was not, the two operating in the same body at the same time.

The conclusion for policy is that the architecture deserves defending, and enlarging: the shields, the anti-retaliation provisions, the percentage awards that make a lawyer take the call. That verdict stands. The bill for it is presented separately, because an institution that learns integrity has a market price will price it. Compliance departments already model expected bounty exposure the way they model litigation reserves, and internal counsel are trained to identify likely claimants before any claim exists. Juries will learn the same lesson in time, and the standard cross-examination will ask not "Did you lie?" but "What were you paid?" Three of the largest awards in the program's history are already under appeal on precisely that ground. When those rulings come down the ovation will be harder to arrange, and the mechanism will still be the only thing that works.
