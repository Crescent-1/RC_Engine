[PASSAGE]

In 1957 a journalist counted the motivation researchers on retainer to American brands and published the number. The critique landed. Depth interviews, symbol testing, the flattery of the subconscious — all named, all quoted back to the industry in its own memoranda. The industry did not fight long. Within fifteen years it had truth-in-advertising codes, disclosure requirements, an internal vocabulary of ethics. By the 1980s it had something better: the wink. Agencies hired copywriters who mocked copywriting. The persuaders stopped hiding. They took a bow.

Three slices, then, taken at intervals like plates from the same photographic survey. 1957: a soap commercial engineered around a mother's guilt, its mechanism concealed, its power dependent on concealment. 1994: a beer commercial that opens by announcing beer commercials use attractive people to sell beer, then uses attractive people, and sells beer. The mechanism is the joke; the joke is the mechanism. Then 2019, and the instrument stops registering the same class of object. A feed contains no argument. Nothing in it claims that a product will improve a life. What it does is set the interval between rewards, weight the queue, and decide which of nine thousand available items appears while a thumb is already moving. There is no message to hold up to the light. The unit that mid-century critics learned to dissect — the discrete persuasive artifact, with an author and an intention and a running time — has become a rounding error in the system's actual output.

Two positions have hardened. One holds the critique spent: nothing is hidden, the persuader has filed his methods, and unmasking a face that is already bare is a ritual, not an analysis. The other holds it urgent, merely misaimed — confession being the most durable disguise, irony a way of pre-absorbing objection. Both arguments are well made. Both, examined closely, keep reaching for the 1994 beer commercial as their exhibit, the self-aware ad that admits what it is doing. Each side is fighting over a specimen collected twenty-five years ago, and neither seems to notice that the light from it is old.

Which suggests that the obsolescence question, as posed, cannot be settled — not because both answers are true, but because it was asked about the wrong stratum. Read the second paragraph again and the sequence is not confession following concealment; it is the artifact thinning out until it is gone, taking the whole apparatus of unmasking with it. A critique does not expire when its object owns up. It expires, or relocates, when its object stops being a statement anyone could own up to. What erodes across those three plates is the artifact-level charge — that a particular message lied about its purpose — and the erosion was needed, because it strips away everything in the original critique that was contingent on there being a message at all. Underneath sits the claim that survives: that the arrangement of attention is engineered, by parties with measurable interests, at intervals a person cannot perceive. That claim was in Packard, buried under the tricks. It is the one now standing, and it is the one on which the verdict should turn.
