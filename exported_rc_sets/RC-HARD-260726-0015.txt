[PASSAGE]

The Ealing Choral Society lost its second tenor section in 2019, and the minute-book records it drily: "numbers no longer viable." Across British and American cities, community choirs are folding. Decline here means what it usually means — a measurable fall in participation rates, heads counted against heads counted a decade earlier. A modest, sober definition. It will do a great deal of work.

Two explanations dominate. The first is busyness. Rehearsal on Tuesday at seven-thirty, every Tuesday, for thirty-six weeks, is an absurd ask of a person with shift work and a commute; surveys of lapsed members put "no time" at the top, reliably. The second is atomization. People no longer join anything — not the Rotary, not the bowls club, not the choir — because the neighbourhood ties that once produced joiners have thinned. Membership data across voluntary associations tracks together, which is suggestive. Both theses have their partisans, and both are respectable.

Now, I may be missing something, but the numbers are not cooperating with either. If Tuesday evenings are the problem, one wants to know why parkrun and its cousins — Saturday mornings, unforgiving, all-weather — have grown for fifteen straight years, and why amateur gaming leagues demand four hours of synchronised attendance from people who claim to have none. If thinned neighbourhood ties are the problem, one wants to know why Seoul and Berlin, cities not famous for their thick parochial webs, sustain choirs that turn applicants away. And there is a smaller, more awkward fact that neither camp likes to be asked about: the choirs that do survive are not conspicuously stocked with the unbusy or the well-connected. They are stocked with people who came for a specific reason and stayed for a different one. Both theories, granted their best day, predict the wrong survivors.

Here is what they share, and it was handed to them in the first paragraph. To define decline as falling participation is to have already decided that singing together is valuable because of what it produces — cohesion, belonging, the stock of goodwill a neighbourhood can draw down. Bonding-output, measured in bodies. Once that is the yardstick, every explanation of the fall must be a story about obstacles to bonding, and busyness and atomization are simply the two obvious obstacles: no time to bond, no one to bond with. They are not rivals so much as roommates. The argument between them was never adjudicable, because the interesting disagreement lies underneath, where nobody was looking. What I would rather ask about is smaller and, I think, more durable: what happens in the act itself — forty people finding a chord without discussing it, adjusting pitch by a fraction nobody could name, tuning to each other faster than thought. That is not a bonding mechanism. It is a form of coordination we have almost no other occasion to practise. The Ealing minute-book records the numbers. It does not record that the tenors, before they went, could hold a unison without a conductor.
