[PASSAGE]

In 1974, in a free clinic on St. Mark's Place, Herbert Freudenberger watched volunteers who had arrived with enormous energy stop showing up, or show up and speak of the addicts they served with a flat contempt they had not brought with them. He gave the state a name. Fifty years later the name has an address. The Maslach Burnout Inventory, twenty-two items in its original human-services form, scores three dimensions: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, reduced personal accomplishment. Ministries of health commission surveys with it. Hospital systems benchmark departments against it. The World Health Organization placed burnout in ICD-11 as an occupational phenomenon, with a definition specifying that it arises from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. So the object appears fixed: origin story, instrument, classification, literature running to tens of thousands of papers. What remains, on this picture, is the ordinary work of verification — determining how much of the reported epidemic is real and who has been miscounted.

The verification stalls almost immediately. When Renzo Bianchi and colleagues ran the inventory alongside standard depression scales, the exhaustion dimension loaded so heavily onto depressive symptomatology that the two constructs would not separate cleanly. Depersonalization and reduced efficacy behaved less like independent dimensions than like consequences of the first. A reader might expect a contest here, two well-drawn categories disputing a border. But only one of them has a border. Depression has diagnostic criteria, thresholds, treatment trials. Burnout has an inventory with no cut-off score its own authors endorse.

And the classification, which was supposed to settle what the instrument could not, is split down the middle: the WHO states flatly that burnout is not a medical condition, only a phenomenon relating to the occupational context, while in Sweden, the Netherlands, and Germany physicians write sick notes for exhaustion syndromes, insurers reimburse them, and employers count the days — roughly a fifth of long-term absence in some Dutch figures, though the coding varies by canton, by clinic, by year. It looks like an administrative gap. It is easier to read as bad coordination than as an absence of anything for coordination to track.

Here the two failures stop looking separate. Each operational definition of burnout begins by deciding which suffering is chargeable to the employer and which is the worker's own. That decision precedes the measurement; it is not discovered by it. Ask the inventory why a nurse's despair counts and a caregiving daughter's does not, and the answer is not clinical but contractual — one has an employer, a rota, a grievance procedure. The triad does not detect a syndrome. It records a settlement about who owes whom, and the WHO's careful phrasing about the occupational context is that settlement written down.

Which means the sharpening instruments were never lenses. Every refinement drew the line it claimed to find, and the honest cost of saying so is that the exhausted lose a word that made their claim legible; what they keep is the argument the word was standing in for, which was always the harder thing to win, and which no revised inventory was ever going to settle on their behalf.
