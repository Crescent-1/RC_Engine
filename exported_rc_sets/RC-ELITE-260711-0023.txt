[PASSAGE]

I have spent enough years distrusting fashionable ideas to be wary of my own admiration, and so I want to name it plainly before I complicate it: Elinor Ostrom did something rare. She took an orthodoxy that every economist could recite in his sleep — the tragedy of the commons, the pasture ruined because no herdsman owns it — and she dismantled it not with a rival model but with fieldwork. Swiss alpine meadows, Spanish irrigation channels, Japanese village forests, Filipino fisheries: shared resources governed for centuries without either the fence of private property or the hand of the state. That she did this inside a discipline suspicious of case studies, and that the suspicious discipline gave her its highest honor, is not nothing. I do not think it was wrong.

What she offered was, on its face, modest. Out of hundreds of documented cases she drew a typology of eight design principles that long-enduring commons tended to share: clearly bounded resources and clearly bounded membership; rules fitted to local conditions rather than imported wholesale; those affected by the rules having a hand in writing them; monitoring carried out by the users themselves; sanctions that escalate by degrees rather than by decree; cheap and accessible conflict resolution; a recognized right to organize; and, for larger systems, governance arranged in nested layers, each tier accountable to the ones above and below. Laid out as a list, the eight have the tidy completeness of a finished tool — a thing you could pick up and apply to any shared resource, at any size, anywhere.

But look at the eighth principle, nested enterprises, and watch how much weight it is asked to bear the moment someone extends this framework to the atmosphere, to carbon, to the ocean's chemistry. That principle is the hinge on which the whole scaling argument turns — and it is precisely the one her evidence supports least, because her evidence came almost entirely from small communities where everyone could watch everyone else. The seven robust principles are robust because they describe faces: neighbors who see the cheater draw water at night. Nesting is the bridge to the faceless, and the case studies never tested that bridge. The fault, I should be clear, is not hers. She was careful. The fault lies with those who cite her to license a planetary governance she never claimed to have validated.

So the admiration was right and aimed slightly wrong. What Ostrom proved was that the local case could be made empirically at all — that self-governance without owner or sovereign was a documented fact and not a romance — and this is a harder, better achievement than the one her enthusiasts wanted from her. Her work is defended best, I think, by refusing the ambition grafted onto it: it establishes that commons can govern themselves where the governing can be seen, and says almost nothing, honestly, about whether such arrangements compose upward into something that could hold an atmosphere. The price of keeping her achievement intact is surrendering the thing we most wanted it to promise, which leaves the largest commons of all still without a method we can say she ever showed us.
