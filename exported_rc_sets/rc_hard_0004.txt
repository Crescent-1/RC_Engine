# READING COMPREHENSION PASSAGE

For more than a century, the peopling of the Indian subcontinent has been contested less as a factual puzzle than as a proxy war over legitimacy. What is striking, on reviewing this intellectual lineage, is that the history of how the deep past has been imagined and mobilised is nearly as revealing as the deep past itself. The debate now sits at a volatile junction of text-based historiography, ideological anxiety, and a recent "genetic turn" that promises hard clarity while smuggling in fresh epistemological hazards.

The instructive irony is that migration was once uncontroversial. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, colonial administrators and pioneering Indian scholars alike accepted that Indo-Aryan-speaking pastoralists had entered the subcontinent from Central Asia in the second millennium BCE. For these earlier figures, an external influx did not diminish civilisational grandeur; it was simply one of several tributary streams feeding a composite culture. The anxiety came later. The excavation of a sophisticated, literate Indus Valley Civilisation—one predating the Rig Veda—shattered the tidy assumption that Indian civilisation began with the Vedic age. In the vacuum created by this displacement, and amplified by majoritarian politics, an indigenist counter-narrative hardened: the "Out of India" thesis, which inverted the arrow of diffusion and insisted that Vedic culture originated within the subcontinent and radiated outward.

Into this impasse arrived ancient DNA. Population genetics now describes modern South Asians as a stratified product of successive migrations: a foundational layer of "First Indians," admixture with populations related to ancient Iranian agriculturalists, and, after the decline of the Indus cities, an infusion of Central Asian Steppe ancestry around 2000–1500 BCE. To many, this looked like empirical closure—a laboratory verdict silencing the ideologues.

But treating genetic data as a totalising truth invites its own errors. The methodology proceeds by reduction: whole persons become genomes, genomes become fragments analysed at antiseptic remove. Genes can trace biological movement and even date the onset of caste endogamy, yet they are mute on culture, language, ideology, and power—the very forces that animate history. DNA can confirm that populations mixed; it cannot narrate the coercion, prestige, or choice that governed the mixing. To lean too heavily on biological markers is to risk "biologising" identities that are, at root, social constructions—thereby lending pseudo-scientific dress to precisely the essentialisms the data was meant to dissolve.

The corrective, then, is not to dethrone genetics but to relocate it. Its findings are indispensable evidence, but evidence answers only the questions posed to it, and biology poses no questions about meaning. Set against the sheer scale of human movement—the tool-makers of Attirampakkam over 275,000 years ago, the bead-makers of 65,000 years past—modern claims of pristine, single-origin belonging appear not merely mistaken but oddly parochial. Ancient India was never a static monolith awaiting scientific ratification; it was an open crucible whose defining feature was mixture. The genetic turn, properly humbled, corroborates that openness even as it fails to explain it.
