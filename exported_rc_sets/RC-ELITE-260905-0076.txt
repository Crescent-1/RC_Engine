The Society for Mutual Aid Among the Blind of St Petersburg dated its founding to 1881, and its expulsion rules to a revision of 1894. On 14 March 1902, in the Society's rooms on Gorokhovaya Street, its committee voted seven to three to strike the clerk Iosif Gurvich from the rolls. The stated ground was unauthorised publication. No minutes survive. Six accounts do, none written that year, the last given nine years later.

The chairman wrote to a benefactor in 1903. He described the bylaw as a hull, and the Society as a small vessel that must not be holed by any single passenger's enthusiasm. He had regretted the vote; he had followed the rule. The word he used for the offence, *samovol'stvo*, self-will, had come into charitable statutes from military discipline, where it named a soldier acting without orders; the almshouses of the 1860s borrowed it to describe inmates who left grounds unpermitted. In his letter it means publishing a pamphlet.

A committee member who resigned in 1904 wrote his own version the following year, and it is the only one that describes the hour before the vote: the chairman moving along the corridor, asking each man in turn how he would go, a hand on a sleeve. Twice, this account says, Gurvich rose to answer the charge and was ruled out of order. The document is a private memorandum, addressed to nobody, found among papers the writer kept after leaving the Society.

Gurvich published in an émigré paper in 1907. The pamphlet, he wrote, had concerned wages paid in the Society's brush and basket workshops, and everything that followed was retaliation dressed as procedure. He quoted two sentences spoken at an earlier meeting which, he argued, showed the outcome already settled. He conceded, without being pressed, that the bylaw did exist, that he had not sought committee approval, and that he had walked out before the vote was formally read aloud.

A seamstress from the workshop, blind since childhood, attended as an observer and set down her memory in 1909. She says almost nothing about procedure. What she recalls is the silence after — long, and then the scrape of chairs. She names three committee members with confidence and cannot place the rest. Her three do not match the chairman's list of speakers, nor Gurvich's, nor the resigned member's; one of hers is a man the other accounts put elsewhere that week. Her disinterest buys her no better access to the room.

The porter was asked in 1911, and his answer concerns the cold, the samovar not lit, and eleven candles, which he counted because he had been sent for more. Then, near the end, he says that Gurvich shook the chairman's hand on the way out. It is tempting to call this the detail that undoes the other five — but it does not undo them; it simply cannot be fitted to any of them, and none of the five would be false if it were true. Nobody asked him what the handshake meant. He died the next spring, and the eleven candles remain the only figure in the whole record that two witnesses agree on, because only one witness ever mentioned them.
