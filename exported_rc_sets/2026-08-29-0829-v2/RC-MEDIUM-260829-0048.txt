Catastrophes are almost always narrated backwards, and the narration hardens faster than the wreckage cools. That was true on Commercial Street in January 1919. A steel tank stood above the North End waterfront, holding more than two million gallons of molasses. It let go. The wave came down toward the harbor, took out a firehouse and an elevated railway support, and carried horses, carts and people along in it. Workers in the yard heard something first — a report, a rattle like machine-gun fire, a groaning. Residents ran. Rescuers waded into a substance that would not behave like water, that closed around a man's legs and held him. Within a day the roar and the speed had been assembled into a single sentence: the tank exploded. I confess a craftsman's sympathy for that sentence. It is economical, it fits the noise, and it explains why nobody got out of the way. It also settles, in one stroke, precisely the thing the witnesses could not settle.

Take the testimony apart and it stops agreeing with itself. Residents heard a blast. Yard workers remembered molasses seeping through the seams for months, remembered children collecting it in cans, remembered the tank being painted brown. Rescuers described a viscous mass that reached farther and lower than any of them expected. Newspapers pressed all of it into detonation. But leaks, rivet noise and a wave that moved fast and then thickened are better read as a structure tearing progressively — a seam opening, then a plate, then the whole shell — than as one instantaneous burst.

The company's account deserves better than dismissal. Molasses ferments; the tank had recently taken a warm shipment into cold contents; internal pressure and gas were genuinely available as contributing causes, and some witnesses did report something that sounded like an explosion, which cannot simply be edited out. I grant all of it. What the concession buys is uncertainty about the physical trigger, not absolution: a vessel that was never tested with water, whose steel was too thin and whose rivet lines were undersized, will fail under pressures a sound vessel absorbs. Contributing cause and excusing cause are not the same instrument, though they are often held in the same hand.

The auditor appointed in the consolidated litigation, and the Massachusetts courts that followed him in the mid-1920s, took roughly that view: United States Industrial Alcohol was held liable, its own experts' pressure theories notwithstanding, because the evidence of untested, unsupervised, under-specified construction was stronger than the evidence of any sudden internal event. I want to be careful about what that settles. It settles who owed a duty and failed it. It does not reconstruct the collapse second by second, and the record is honest enough to admit as much.

So I will call it a preventable structural failure and not a mystery poised between accident and mischief. The middle position is available and I decline it, because the surviving evidence cannot recover the final mechanical sequence while remaining wholly sufficient to establish neglect — which is why the seams the North End children scraped their cans against still seem to me the most eloquent thing in the file.
