[PASSAGE]

Since the 1980s, when the study of psychological injury acquired its modern instruments, a remarkable pattern has come into view. Across populations that share almost nothing else — soldiers returning from combat, drivers pulled from wrecked cars, survivors of assault, witnesses to sudden death — researchers began to find the same constellation of responses recurring with an almost mechanical regularity: the intrusive memory that arrives unbidden, the state of hyperarousal in which the body treats a slammed door as a gunshot, the avoidance that reroutes a whole life around a single street or sound. The consistency was striking precisely because the events were so various. Whatever else these people had endured, their minds seemed to answer overwhelming experience in one recognizable grammar, as though the psyche, pushed past a certain threshold, broke along predictable seams.

But consider what the finding rests on. The clinician does not observe the seams directly. She asks. The intake questionnaire supplies the categories. Criterion A supplies the qualifying event. The interview names the sensations in advance — Do you re-experience it? Do you feel on edge? — and the patient, hearing the question, learns what to look for. This is not fraud. It is teaching. The checklist trains the eye. It hands both parties a vocabulary, and the vocabulary comes with instructions. None of this makes the racing heart imaginary. The pounding is real. What the instrument shapes is not whether something happened in the body but which parts of that something get gathered up, named, and reported as a symptom rather than as ordinary distress.

Picture a family in a hospital corridor while a relative is resuscitated after a crash — the same ordeal, the same fluorescent hours, the same body on the far side of the door. One daughter is a university lecturer who has spent three years reading students' emails that describe missed deadlines as traumatizing; she has absorbed the word until it is a lens, and she experiences the corridor as an event she will need, later, to process. Beside her, the older mother has no such word available. She waits. She is frightened, exhausted, undone — but she does not experience herself as being wounded in a way that will require repair, because the category that would make that experience legible was never given to her. It is tempting to read this as the young being more fragile than the old. That reading misses the mechanism. The lecturer is not more sensitive; she has been handed an instrument, and the instrument is doing constitutive work — assembling a certain suffering into 'trauma' — that in her mother simply has no assembly point.

Something happened that no vocabulary invented and no silence could dissolve: broken ribs, a shattered face, a body kept alive by machines. That residue is not negotiable, and no critique of the checklist touches it. What the checklist touches is the line drawn through the suffering — the boundary separating trauma proper from grief, fear, and the ordinary catastrophe of loving someone. That line cannot be read off the injury. It can only be read off the language a person carried into the corridor. So the sharper question is not whether trauma is real but which distributions of the word have already settled into a population before its crashes arrive — and that will hold until the public vocabulary shifts again, as it is shifting now, one forwarded student email at a time.
