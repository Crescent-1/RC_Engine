A settled habit of reading holds that the family novel exposes a structure of injury, and that its business is disclosure. Applied to the narrator of one such recent novel, the frame names the damage around her but cannot say why her tending, her fear and her withdrawal move as one conduct.

The received account runs in steps. Edwyn is demanding, much older, crotchety. Neve ministers, nurse-like, to his arthritic hands. That looks like submission. She tells us she loves animals, their natural ways, and keeps humanity at a distance; that looks like retreat. Each step is legible as a symptom. What the sequence never explains is how the tending itself becomes the medium in which the role is held in place, rather than a sign of a role held elsewhere.

More than one marriage rests on the answer. The judgment a reader forms about Neve also governs how responsibility gets apportioned backward, across a family the novel keeps in view. Name her conduct damage and it is excused; name it choice and the conditions that taught it disappear. Riley, stage-managing a combat between her readers' moral judgment and the questionable behavior for which her narrator seeks validation, makes the accounting depend on the form the care takes. It is tempting to treat this as a contest over whether Neve is finally blameworthy or innocent. The novel is doing something narrower and harder: assembling a conduct in which either verdict arrives too late to be useful.

Read closely, the scene of the hands and the scene of Edwyn's boasting belong together. He boasts of his innate nastiness; she hears the low, pleading sounds she has started making whenever he is sharp with her, and finds them frightening, panic-making. "This wasn't how I spoke. (Except it was.) This wasn't me, this crawling, cautious creature. (Except it was.) I defaulted to it very easily. And he let me." Call this sequence a care-script: service, distance and fear reinforcing one another until the default feels native. The parenthesis does the work. It does not report a belief Neve holds; it records the moment a practised role is caught being practised, and the correction follows immediately, because the script supplies its own account of itself.

The care-script has a history in Riley's work. Conflicted feelings about past events first became significant in the third novel, Joshua Spassky (2007), where Natalie remembers pretending, as a child, to shrink from the slipper her divorced father raised above her as a joke — spite, she says, then doubts the spontaneity of it. That is bodily tending turned inward, a child managing an adult by managing herself. The narrator of the later book, rereading the earnest underlinings in her old college books, can say only that there was something she was trying to sound out, and that she had to start with that kind of calling out: the same demand, now interpretive. By First Love it is domestic and nurse-like again. Recurrence of this kind is not a fixed doctrine of family life; the situations differ too much for that. What persists is a practice, inherited and adapted, for converting compromised conduct into a livable account. On that ground the pathology frame should give way, and the question of how far conduct learned as care can be judged at all stays open beneath it.
