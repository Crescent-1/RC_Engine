Can a public service become more efficient by appearing only when someone needs it? Macon-Bibb County’s objection to the old downtown loop was precise: buses were circling through thin ridership, consuming fuel and driver hours for seats left empty. Demand-responsive service promised a vehicle shaped by actual use, rather than by inherited lines on a map.

Demand advocates and route loyalists consequently described the county’s choice in opposite terms. The former saw an advance-booked shuttle as a correction to waste in a city of dispersed neighborhoods and limited funds; the latter saw the fixed loop as a public presence whose value could not be read from full seats alone. County officials answered that the new arrangement still connected south Macon, downtown offices and medical buildings, but by a sequence more exacting than a timetable: a passenger booked, received confirmation, waited for pickup, then transferred when a vehicle’s route required it. The figures that favored the shuttle seemed to settle the practical dispute.

In October, Laverne Cole, seventy-two, booked a morning ride from her apartment in south Macon to a downtown cardiology appointment. Her confirmation gave an 8:15 pickup and a 9:00 arrival, leaving twenty minutes for the clinic’s check-in window. At 8:07, the first van was reassigned to a rider whose wheelchair could not be secured in the smaller vehicle nearby. A replacement was sent from another run. Similar adjustments filled the dispatcher’s morning: a late grocery pickup, a driver waiting for an address correction, a passenger cancelling after the vehicle had turned toward her street. Cole’s van reached her at 8:42. It arrived downtown after 9:20. The clinic marked her late and offered a later opening. The delays looked separate because each had a local explanation. They shared a cause, though: every promised movement depended on a fresh alignment of vehicle, driver, passenger and destination.

The printed trip log, assembled after Cole asked for it, gives the day a peculiar density. At 7:31 her booking entered the system; at 7:33 confirmation went out; at 8:06 the assigned van changed; at 8:39 an automated estimate stated that it was six minutes away. On the dispatcher’s screen, Cole was a blue marker that shifted from “scheduled” to “delayed” and then to “onboard.” At the clinic, she was a late arrival whose slot had passed. Between those descriptions lay three unanswered calls from her phone, two periods of hold music, and a return request placed after the later appointment could no longer be guaranteed. No driver refused her. No dispatcher invented the delay. The shuttle performed its instructions with the small deviations that such instructions continually produce. What was the shuttle actually transporting—the passenger, or the proof that her need was legitimate? Vehicle efficiency counts one kind of distance, while transit may be better measured by the waiting and uncertainty it asks a passenger to absorb.
