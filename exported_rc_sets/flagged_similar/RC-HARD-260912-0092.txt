[PASSAGE]

Every price index rests on a quiet assumption: that the unit in which prices are quoted is stable enough for a change in the number to mean a change in the thing. Carli's eighteenth-century procedure makes the assumption operational in a way anyone can follow. Take a basket of goods — grain, oil, wine. For each good, find its price in the local unit at two dates, compute the percent change, then average those percent changes across the basket. Quote the same goods in bullion — so many grains of silver — and repeat. To a first approximation the two series will run alongside each other, because the coin is the bullion, minted and stamped. Where they part, they part slightly, and the gap can be set aside. That is why the method appears to be a general technique: nothing in the arithmetic announces the conditions under which it holds.

Currencies nominally backed by specie do drift from it. Heavy note issue widens the gap; war widens it faster. But the drift ordinarily stays small — a few percent, a premium a merchant absorbs into his margin. Roughly speaking, the index swallows it as noise. Nominal price still tracks value, and the assumption goes on looking settled.

The Richmond newspapers of the war years, the Whig and the Examiner among them, carried gold-premium tables the way they carried shipping notices: a routine column, updated as the brokers reported. By early 1864 those columns quoted twenty Confederate paper dollars to the gold dollar, then forty, then upward of fifty, while flour in the same city climbed past two hundred paper dollars a barrel. Contemporaries reached, again and again, for a formulation that the price quotations themselves could not express — that prices were not really rising at all, that it was the money that was going. Read charitably, that phrasing is not rhetorical excess but a report of something structural. Ordinary specie drift is the same phenomenon at higher magnitude; here the reference point and the medium being priced had ceased to move together in any respect, so the failure was in the assumption rather than in the numbers the assumption produced.

Which leaves a narrower question, and a harder one. Was there a ratio — ten to one, twenty to one, some figure a bookkeeper could have circled at the time — at which depreciation became collapse? The tables are continuous. They record every intermediate value, and mark none of them.

We are not, now, in the position of a Richmond bookkeeper reconciling two columns for his own account. Consumer price indices set Social Security adjustments, bracket thresholds, and the escalation clauses in wage contracts covering tens of millions of people; a threshold that cannot be seen in advance would not remain a historian's curiosity if a modern currency approached one. What the surviving evidence supports — the broker tables, the merchants' letters, the Treasury's own record of notes issued against redemption it could not promise — is a break locatable only afterward, in the collapse of confidence rather than at any ratio. So the familiar observation that an index measures prices in money is not quite the platitude it sounds like: it is the statement of a condition that the Confederate columns, quietly and for four years, record the exhaustion of.
