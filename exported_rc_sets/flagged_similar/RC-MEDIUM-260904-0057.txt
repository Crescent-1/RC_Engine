[PASSAGE]

The defect account came out of the mid-century reliability engineering that treated a failed part as a flawed part. Something got in. A metal fleck. A void in the coating. A nick in the separator. Find the intruder and you have found the cause. Battery manufacturers inherited that habit whole, and it still explains a great deal: contamination that seeds a short, an electrode film laid unevenly by a coating head running slightly out of true, a separator pierced during winding, an isolated cell that runs away thermally while its neighbours sit cool. The account has the virtue of locality. It puts the fault somewhere, at a moment, on a line, in a shift log.

What unsettles it is a pair of cells drawn from a single batch, put through the same fast-charging regime, and later opened up: one had vented and blackened its jelly roll, the other had merely aged, and yet their construction, their initial measured capacity to within a fraction of a percent, and — this is the awkward part — their final micro-CT scans showed nothing that would let an inspector say which was which without the labels. Identical end-state structure cannot explain divergent outcomes. Two ruins, similarly collapsed, tell you little about which building shook first. And more parties now need the answer than once did: not only cell makers but grid operators, insurers, airport handlers, anyone who charges fast.

So the newer work stopped inspecting corpses. Matched cells were synchronised through repeated cycles until their voltage traces overlaid. Thermocouples and pressure transducers went in at the tab and the can wall. Thermal cameras filmed the surface. Operando X-ray measurement watched the anode while current flowed. Then cells were pulled off the rig at staggered moments — cycle four, cycle eleven, cycle thirty — and sectioned for micro-CT and microscopy.

Read quickly, that is a very expensive search for a hidden speck. It is not. The staggering is the whole design: each sacrificed cell is a stratum, and the stack of them is a sequence, not a snapshot. What the instruments track is amplification — whether a patch of slightly higher local resistance warms, whether the warmth invites plating, whether the plated lithium raises resistance again. The excavator's discipline applies. You do not date a site from its top layer. I said the work stopped inspecting corpses; that is too strong, since the sectioning is still autopsy. What changed is that each autopsy carries a timestamp.

The failing cell, on this record, began with a small difference in lithium distribution well inside the tolerance any factory would pass — not a flaw, a fluctuation. Under fast charge it fed itself. The surviving cell met the same disturbance and spread it out. Nothing in the finished object marked the difference; only the time-resolved record did, which is the vantage this entire reconstruction was written from. The verdict is a history-dependent electrochemical one, and it holds. It does not retire the coating head or the metal fleck; defects remain real causes, and this result still wants replication across batches, chemistries, charge rates. What it does is change what one asks of two cells that came off the same line: not which of them was already spoiled, but which of them, on the eleventh charge, quietly stopped sharing.
