[PASSAGE]

At half past two, the stone façade of a municipal hall can be brighter than the pavement before it. Its floodlights have found no audience but pigeons tucked beneath a cornice, a cleaner’s cart, the occasional taxi. Yet the arrangement persists because light is rarely treated as mere equipment. It is a public promise made visible: the square remains available, the building remains ours, the city has not withdrawn behind its doors. Jane Jacobs’s phrase “eyes on the street” still supplies part of the inheritance, even when the eyes are cameras, passing drivers, or nobody at all. Security explains some of it. Civic pride explains some more. Those of us who planned public works learned to speak of illuminated landmarks as a kind of welcome, a house lamp left on in the front room. But a lamp also announces which arrivals have been anticipated. The monument’s glow can make darkness look like abandonment, and can make the unlit edges of the same district appear to have fallen outside the municipal household.

A lighting engineer and a nocturnal field worker may stand beneath that façade with instruments aimed at the same night and produce incompatible accounts without either falsifying it: the engineer’s meter records the lux reaching a face, a curb, a camera lens, while the field worker’s trap and notebook register the moths drawn out of their routes and the birds altering their flight through the lit air; and although brighter streets can change confidence and movement, they do not reliably convert visibility into safety, particularly where selective illumination makes certain passers-by feel observed rather than sheltered. The measurements are not parallel. One records what human sight is enabled to do; the other records what the light causes living creatures to do.

Those costs include energy spent maintaining a human timetable, but they are not reducible to a bill or an unpleasant view. Moths circle a lamp until dawn; migrating birds meet a bright façade as they would meet a false opening in a house. Dark-sky advocates count such disorientations, while lighting designers count the places where a person can recognise another person’s face. Each camp has a usable measure, and the advocates’ account has the force of damage plainly described. Still, a lit clock tower after a funeral, or a bridge held in light during a winter blackout, can carry memory rather than display. We knew this. A city’s symbols are not simply ornaments appended to utility — no: more precisely, they are among the ways utility has been imagined.

If reassurance is distributed to humans by reorganising the lives of creatures unable to consent, should a city ask what darkness requires before deciding what night is for? Urban branding cannot answer that alone; nocturnal ecology may be better placed to describe the terms, though civic meaning will not disappear merely because its claims are harder to count.
