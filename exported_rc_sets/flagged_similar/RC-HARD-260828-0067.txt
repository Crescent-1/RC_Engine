[PASSAGE]

A parish keeps stranger records than it knows. In one suburban office, a secretary saved the chat logs from every livestreamed Mass of the lockdown spring, exported to spreadsheets and stored on a shared drive under a folder called PRAYERS. There are thousands of lines. Each row carries a display name, a timestamp accurate to the second, and a typed petition: for a mother on a ventilator, for a son who lost his job, for a marriage that had gone quiet. Underneath, in the video, an altar. A single camera locked off at the sanctuary. A priest moving through a rite designed for a room with people in it. Those of us who spent years arguing that liturgical practice survives its media — that the sacraments do not care what wire they travel down — were handed, in that folder, a document we could not read as easily as we expected. The petitions scroll. The Mass proceeds. The two do not touch, and the question the archive forces is not whether the typing was sincere but what the typists believed they were addressing.

The obvious reading is continuity, and it is a good reading. Catholic petition has always been material and slightly cluttered: the candle stand with its rows of guttering flames, the wooden box for intention cards, the parish bulletin that prints a list of the sick in small type. The chat window is that furniture rebuilt in glass. The language supports it — *St Jude, pray for us*, *Blessed Mother, hear my prayer*, *for the repose of the soul of*, formulas so worn they arrive intact through a phone keyboard. Nothing doctrinal has changed, on this account; a house has been re-clad, and the load-bearing walls are where they were.

A second reading treats the window as a pew substitute. The logs show what a votive rack cannot: replies. Someone types a petition for her father and four strangers type *praying with you*, and there are heart reactions, and thumbs, and the small typed amens that accumulate under a request the way flowers accumulate at a roadside. Read that way, the chat is a support thread wearing vestments, and its work is loneliness, not intercession.

Both readings live on content. The timestamps live elsewhere. Sort the logs by time and the petitions cluster in the homily — the long stretch when the priest is talking and the screen is least demanding — and thin out almost to nothing during the Prayers of the Faithful, the one moment the rite actually reserves for exactly this. Easy to call that lag, or distraction, or people who never learned the shape of the Mass. But the address gives it away. *Father, could you remember my aunt.* *You all, please pray.* Second person, plural, human.

Nobody read them. Not the priest, who faced an altar and a camera; not the server; not the secretary until months later. The petitions went out to the only audience present, which was the other typers, and were answered by them, which means what the folder preserves is not prayer and not liturgy but people witnessing each other in the presence of something they had agreed to treat as sacred — a practice with its own integrity, though not, I think, the one we told ourselves had merely moved online.
