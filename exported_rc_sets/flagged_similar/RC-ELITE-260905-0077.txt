[PASSAGE]

Precision is especially persuasive when it promises to spare an expensive disturbance. For a utility short of money, magnetic acoustic loggers attached to fittings, and correlators connected at hydrants, offer an extraordinary bargain: long runs examined without opening the street. Escaping water produces vibrations; software compares their arrival times at separated sensors and places a probable source on a map. Manufacturer manuals call the crucial interval the “time delay”; with pipe length and sound velocity supplied, it becomes distance. We can admire the economy. Another crew might enclose the same run within a district, meter its inflow overnight, subtract expected legitimate demand, then close branch valves successively to see where unexplained flow falls. Both seek escaping water. One offers a point, the other an implicated stretch; a dramatic rupture may make their answers coincide almost exactly.

Around these practices stands something like a classroom in which several pupils approach the same problem from different desks. Pressure transients—brief changes travelling through the main—supply one solution; hydraulic models relating supply, pressure and billed consumption offer another. Satellite moisture imagery contributes a sketch, while crews listen directly at valves. Each pupil has different working. Models inherit uncertain pipe histories; satellites see irrigation and rain as well as leakage; listeners return when traffic subsides, lifting the same stubborn triangular valve cover before dawn. The listening school favours a located sound over a district’s unexplained flow; the balance school favours missing volume over a convincing sound. Neither lacks reasons. At the classroom board, their differing answers seem capable of correcting one another, especially when several approaches indicate the same street.

On plastic mains, however, a substantial leak can lose much of its audible character before reaching a sensor; beside a busy road, vibration can resemble leakage; among properties with ageing customer meters, under-registration can resemble water escaping underground. A quiet trace excludes a detectable source under the listening conditions, an apparently reassuring clearance. It excludes nothing quieter. Consider a district whose overnight excess persists after legitimate night use has been checked, and falls when a plastic branch is isolated, although repeated acoustic visits mark that branch as quiet. The disagreement survives competent work. Sound has travelled poorly; water has continued travelling. Nor do additional maps necessarily supply independent witnesses: shared assumptions about routes, demand and pipe material can align their errors, giving network noise an impressively precise address without identifying its cause.

Acoustic ingenuity earns its place as triage, but the district test must govern the next excavation in that case. Its answer concerns disappearing volume, not an inferior approximation to a sound’s location. District metering carries the older practice of balancing supply against consumption from the whole undertaking into bounded neighbourhoods; sequential valve closure carries it down to individual branches. The gain costs night shifts, service interruptions, working boundary valves and sometimes a trench that settles nothing immediately. Crews still have to dig. Even “loss” changes on this journey from accounts to street: imperfect meters, estimated demand and authorised but unrecorded uses enter a quantity spoken of as escaped water. District testing remains the necessary expense, though the balance it tests cannot be cleaner than the allowances from which it is made.
