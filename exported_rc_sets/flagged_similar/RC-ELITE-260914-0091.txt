In January 1915, at a table in Richmond, Virginia Woolf wrote down that she and Leonard had settled on buying a printing press. The entry is brief. It records the resolution with real pleasure. What it does not record — because there was nothing yet to record — is any step toward the purchase. Two years and three months later the machine arrived. Between the sentence and the machine lies a gap that the surviving papers describe without ever quite explaining.

Trace the press through the letters of those years and you watch it change register, the way a word does when it crosses from one trade into another: to one correspondent it is an amusement, a thing she and Leonard will do when they are idle; to her sister it becomes a practical scheme with rooms and costs attached; to another friend it dwindles into a half-joke, the sort of plan one refers to fondly without expecting to be held to it. What stays constant is only that it keeps being mentioned. During months when her health made nearly every other arrangement conditional, the press served as the one intention she could state without qualification — an unbuilt thing has the advantage of never being postponed, only awaited.

That explanation is tidy enough to stop with, and it stops too soon. Money was short in 1916; money was also spent. Books came into the house. A servant was kept. Small comforts were not refused. A household that could find funds for those could, over two years, have found funds for a secondhand machine costing less than a season's bills. Scarcity explains why the purchase was slow. It does not explain why this purchase, and not the others, was the one that stayed in the future.

Consider what happened the same afternoon the press was resolved upon. A bulldog was also decided on — John. The dog came quickly. Both choices are usually told together, as evidence of a shared impulsive brightness in that household, and the dog's swift arrival is taken as proof that the impulse was real. It proves the opposite about the press. Two resolutions made in one hour, one executed within days, the other for years: they were never the same kind of resolution at all.

A writer who knew them saw it plainly enough. An unbought press is like a manuscript kept in a drawer — a promise made to a later self. Both are useful precisely while unopened. Both confer the standing of the work without its exposure. Both are diminished the moment the drawer is pulled or the platen comes down, because then there is a thing to be judged rather than a thing to be intended.

The trade's version runs otherwise: a machine is ordered, crated, carried, and arrives with a part broken; the broken part is identified, a replacement obtained, the mechanism set square, inked, tested on scrap; only then does it print. Each stage takes its own time and answers to no one's state of mind. Offered as the account that settles the matter, this would have to show that no month of those two years was available for placing the order — and the letters, whatever else they show, do not show that. So the mechanical sequence and the interpretive ones remain side by side, neither absorbing the other, which is where the record leaves them.
