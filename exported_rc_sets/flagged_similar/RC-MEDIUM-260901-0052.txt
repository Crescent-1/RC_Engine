[PASSAGE]

What can a pot tell you about the people who used it? For most of a century, the answer looked easy. A distinctive style — a particular rim, a repeated chevron, a burnished slip — was read as the visible signature of a distinctive people. Find the same decoration across forty sites, draw a line around them, and you had a group with a name, a homeland, and a history. Sherds in the ground, peoples on the map. Few inferences in the human sciences have ever seemed so cheap and so productive.

The confidence had a source, and it was not stupidity. Culture-historical archaeology inherited a mass of undated, undocumented material and needed some way to sort it, so it built bounded units out of recurring traits — pot forms, burial postures, brooch types — and called each bundle a culture. The word did a great deal of quiet work. It named an analytical grouping, a bookkeeping convenience for traits that clustered, and it also named, in ordinary speech, a living community with a language and a sense of itself. When written records were absent and evidence of movement scarce, the slide from one meaning to the other cost nothing anyone could measure.

Follow the chain link by link. Excavation yields types. Types are plotted. Plots become clusters. Clusters are christened cultures, and cultures are then imagined marching across river valleys, displacing one another. Every step is a real operation performed by real hands, and every step is defensible on its own. What broke the chain was petrography: thin-sectioning a sherd and reading its temper and clay against local geology. Pots made from local clay turn up carrying wholly foreign decoration. Imported pots turn up in households that also made their own. A potter three valleys away copies a fashionable rim she has seen once. Local clay proves the vessel was made nearby. It proves nothing about who wanted it or why.

The cost of the broken chain is not confined to the seminar room. Accounts of invasion, of migration, of one people supplanting another rest on precisely the step that will not bear weight, and so do the museum labels that summarise those accounts in forty words, and so do the origin stories that certain states tell about their soil. I grant the counter-case at full strength: styles do cluster, distributions are not random, and a wholesale scepticism would throw away real signal. Nothing here makes ceramics useless. It changes what they are evidence of. They stopped being census returns and became something else.

That something else has a protagonist, and she was always in the room, ignored: the potter at the wheel, the householder in the market. Someone copies a rim because it looks current. Someone buys a jar from a trader and keeps using it for thirty years. Someone inherits a vessel from a grandmother and repurposes it for grain. Pottery is not a passport. It is a record of connection and choice, and it testifies most strongly where those two things meet — in a locally thrown cooking pot whose decoration was learned from a foreign one someone happened to admire.
