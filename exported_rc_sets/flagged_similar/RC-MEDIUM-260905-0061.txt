[PASSAGE]

The doctrine that yellow fever rose from rotting matter in warm cities is older than the U.S. Army's medical department, and it was inherited, not invented. Vector transmission means something narrower than what that doctrine allowed. It means the passage of an infectious agent from one host to another through the body of an intermediary organism, in which the agent spends time. Not proximity. Not season. Not the smell of a wharf in August. The older account was not stupidity; it was faithful bookkeeping of what officers could see. Fever came in hot months, near standing water, in filthy districts, and left when frost arrived. Every association held. What the associations concealed was the animal doing the carrying.

Set the sequence out plainly: a man with fever in his blood, a mosquito that bites him, a wait of some days while the agent matures inside the insect, and then a second man, not immune, bitten. The insect is not a symptom of bad conditions the way a stench is; here the parallel breaks, because a stench cannot be caught, counted, bred in a jar, or withheld. What changed the Army's mind was not the fashion for germs — half the officers had accepted germs for a decade and still blamed the air — but the conversion of a loose pattern into a chain each link of which could be gripped separately and pulled apart.

Havana in 1900 is where the definition earns its keep. Reed's commission did not go looking for a general correlation; it built a hut, screened it, and asked what a man would catch there. Lazear and Carroll took bites deliberately; Lazear died. Volunteers slept in bedding fouled by fever patients and stayed well, which no theory of contaminated air survives. Others, kept in clean quarters and protected from every filthy influence except one screened mosquito, sickened on schedule. Think of a house with a dozen doors and a thief who keeps getting in. Bolt them one at a time. Filth, bolted: he still enters. Foul air, bolted: he still enters. Bolt the insect, and the thefts stop. That is the whole method, and the city then supplied the confirmation at scale. Gorgas's crews poured oil on cisterns, screened barrels, drained puddles; fever fell away in districts whose garbage nobody had touched. Streets stayed dirty. Transmission ended anyway. The old explanation could not tell you why one filthy block emptied of fever while its equally filthy neighbour did too, once both had lost their breeding water.

So I refuse the tidy compromise that both accounts were partly right and therefore equally causal. They were not equally anything. Miasma described the setting in which mosquitoes flourish — warmth, water, crowding — and described it accurately; it never named the mechanism, and a description that names no mechanism buys you nothing at the moment you must decide what to do on Tuesday morning. The Army did not adopt the mosquito because it was elegant. It adopted it because it told a quartermaster what to purchase: wire mesh, kerosene, and lids for the water barrels behind the barracks kitchen.
