Categories of literary form are conveniences of trade: they tell a reader which customs house to present at. Anne Carson's *Nox* arrives as a grey box, and inside it folded pages, torn handwriting, scraps of Latin, seem to promise several familiar kinds of reading without licensing any.

I once held, and held for years, that the box was the least interesting thing about it — a publisher's flourish around an account of a brother's death. That view has real merit, and I still grant it face value in one respect: the packaging does court the collector. But the categories that would have made it a memoir or an elegy fail on their own terms. *Nox* begins in the fact of Michael's death, then withholds the continuous narrative both forms lead one to expect. Classification is not incidental here; it has entered the subject.

The turn came in 2013, reading the Simonides pages against the letters. Where a philologist sees in Carson's word-by-word rendering of the fragment on Catullus 101 a scholarly apparatus, and where a memoirist sees in the photographs and stamped envelopes the ordinary furniture of testimony, neither is authorised by what it stands beside. The Latin does not deliver an ancient analogue that dignifies the loss; the lexicon entries swell into private glosses and drift from the word they define. The letters, few and mostly unanswered, document a silence rather than a life. So the claim sharpens: the work is not translating grief into form, it is turning the mismatch among translation, testimony and evidence into the shape of loss. What the earlier reading called flourish is where the argument lives.

Consider it, then, as an artist's book and as a family archive at once. The accordion fold means the reader handles remnants in sequence, cannot skim backwards easily, must physically unstack a life. Here the temptation is strong to treat the grief as a design effect — the fold as metaphor, the facsimile as mood. That reading is too cheap by half, and its opposite is no better: to call the thing an archive is to mistake a selected trace for neutral preservation, since someone chose which envelope survived. The word *archive* migrated from the magistrate's chamber to the historian's discipline to the gallery, losing at each border its guarantee of custody while keeping its air of proof. *Nox* borrows the air and refuses the guarantee. Its broken form does not merely picture absence; it makes the reader's own effort to assemble a person run into the limits of assembly.

The underlying cause of the classificatory trouble is not hybridity but relation: a brother half-known cannot be contained by genres built to deliver a knowable subject. So the verdict is not that *Nox* succeeds as a hybrid of memoir, elegy, translation and made object, but that it is an elegy whose medium is the failure of those four to hold a relation. Its materials keep documentary force without becoming proof; its Latin reaches toward the dead without claiming recovery. A searchable digital edition, which the market will eventually want, would detach weight, sequence and handling from the words and oblige the argument to be made again on thinner ground. Even then, the judgment stands: this box's material incompleteness is the truest account of mourning it could have given.
