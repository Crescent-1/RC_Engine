[PASSAGE]

A microphone stand in a hotel conference room, adjusted twice before the statement begins: from this small fitting the whole circuit can be traced. Public apology, as the term will be used here, is any expression of regret routed through an audience rather than delivered privately. Such statements sometimes enlarge what they address, since the injured party must now answer both the original harm and a conspicuous display of remorse. Whether this counts as repair that misfires, or as control over the account, is the objection worth taking seriously.

The most careful version of that objection runs roughly so: the failure is technical, not political, and better-drafted apologies would fix it. Given full force, it has weight. Apologies do carry real moral load. Sincerity is not decoration. Refusal can be mere obstinacy. But trace the sequence. An offence is named. A speaker is positioned before witnesses. Recognition of altered standing is requested. Ratification of that alteration is then quietly sought from the one person least free to withhold it. Once closure has been publicly offered, refusal tends to register as excess, and the burden of proof migrates — under most configurations, silently — from the party who caused harm to the party who absorbed it. That migration is not a drafting defect.

What moves along that channel is interpretive authority. To narrate motives, to specify limits, to draw the lesson, is to render the injury legible in vocabulary that leaves the speaker's social identity intact — and this occurs whether or not manipulation is intended. Practitioners in restorative justice mediation, working in rooms where both parties sit, are often trained to keep the harmed person speaking first for precisely this reason: whoever frames the account fixes its scale. Two camps have formed around the same event. One reads defective repair. The other reads successful capture. Both are describing the flow correctly and mislabelling its direction.

At institutional scale the same topology appears with more valves. Workplace apology rituals, university statements, corporate acknowledgements: each can supply a genuine grammar of accountability, a channel where previously there was none, and it would be careless to treat every such protocol as ornament. Yet the same channels tend to reward visible contrition above restitution, and an audience's appetite for emotional resolution can be discharged entirely without any adjustment to procedure, budget or authority. Consider the case that presses hardest: a wholly sincere executive whose statement, broadcast, still shifts attention from a shortfall in wages to the quality of his regret. Sincerity held constant; the redistribution occurred anyway. The location of the fault is not inside the speaker.

The dispute as usually posed — sincere or theatrical, adequate or inadequate — is misconceived, because publicity alters the object undergoing repair. Privately, what is repaired is a relation. Publicly, what is repaired is a reputation, and the relation becomes the instrument. Sincerity remains morally significant; it simply stops determining the outcome once the circuit widens. Zoom out one further step and a culture appears in which recognition is increasingly consumed as an event rather than extended as a practice. Apologies do not fail at that scale. They succeed at something else.
