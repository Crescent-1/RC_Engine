[PASSAGE]

A repeated phrase in a translated dialogue is generally assumed to be one thing appearing several times, and the argument concerns what should be done with it. Two schools have made their positions clear. The leitmotif party — heirs of the Homeric translators who fix "wine-dark sea" once and keep it fixed, Lattimore's practice being the classic instance — holds that recurrence is itself the meaning: vary the wording and the reader loses the thread that binds the scenes. The contextualist party answers that fidelity to a phrase's local sentence outranks fidelity to its earlier selves, that Beckett translating himself between French and English let repeated stage phrases drift when the surrounding rhythm demanded it, and that a translation policed for consistency reads like a machine. I have taught out of both camps and I confess an old loyalty to the first; the apprentice in me still believes that a phrase struck the same way three times is a phrase forged, and I have to keep reminding myself that this is affection, not argument. Set Pavese's *Dialoghi con Leucò* beside these cases and the quarrel comes apart in the hands. "C'è una legge, Issione, cui bisogna ubbidire" surfaces three times in the opening dialogue between the Cloud and Ixion, and it is not doing the same work each time. The first announces that a law has come into being where none was — the emphasis falls on *c'è*, on sheer existence. The second sets that existence against a lawless past, and the weight shifts to the contrast. The third stands alone, stripped, a flat declarative with nothing to lean on. So the schools are not disagreeing about how to handle a recurring unit; they have both assumed there is a recurring unit to handle. Italian syntax makes this easy to miss: *c'è* can carry an existential claim, a temporal one, or almost none at all, and the surface never moves.

This is not a philological nicety for specialists. Most readers now meet the *Dialoghi* only in English, and whether Nephele's speech registers as cosmology or as pious filler depends entirely on those three sentences — a whole dialogue's reputation in anglophone criticism rests on them.

Grant the contextualists their case. The three sentences genuinely differ. Their neighbouring clauses differ. Proctor's shifting renderings, varying even within her own text, are not carelessness; they answer to something real on the page. Adaptive translation here is defensible craft, and I would not want a version that ignored the differences to keep a formula intact.

But the concession does not restore the question, because neither label holds. The refrain is no fixed formula, and calling it free variation loses precisely what makes it feel inevitable on a third reading: it accumulates, each utterance drawing weight from the two behind it, so that the bare third line is heavy only because the first two happened. No single-line choice can register that. Criticism keeps demanding a verdict on which rendering is right, when what such a passage needs from its reviewers is a judgment on the arc all three make together — a small change in what gets compared, and one the existing habits of review could absorb without any new theory at all.
