[PASSAGE]

A refusal to have children can be stated as a conclusion about the world, and in Japan it increasingly is: young people who say, with some elegance, that no one is entitled to gamble another person's existence against the suffering the century is likely to hand out. Bioethicists and the movement's sympathizers take that at face value, as a position arrived at by argument and holding whether or not the arguer is happy. Sociologists of the family hear something else in the same sentences — not a conclusion but a symptom, the philosophical dress worn by a long retreat from marriage and childbearing that was underway before anyone in these groups was old enough to reason about it. One camp grants the argument its dignity. The other grants it a diagnosis. Both assume the sentence is about the world.

I think both have mislaid the subject. They treat reason and symptom as rivals for a single slot: either she means it or she is being explained. But someone who says she cannot see a reason to reproduce is not usually delivering a verdict on the century. She is describing a relationship. Somewhere in the account there is a parent, waiting.

Yukina, a pseudonymous art student and part-time worker in Tokyo, put it more flatly than any bioethicist would. "We have children because of social pressures," she told the anthropologist who interviewed her, and then: "But also because we want our children to replace us." Her own refusal is complete and philosophically framed — she holds there is no good reason to reproduce — yet what she reports is not cosmic pessimism but the moment her parents learned she is a transgender woman with no plans for a family, and their hope collapsed. What had been pinned on grandchildren was continuation: a hedge against ending, which they had quietly filed under her name. The fear of vanishing, relocated onto the child, is the engine here. It does not tell us whether she is right about the world.

And the household is no longer the whole room. What was once a private, slightly embarrassing disappointment at a dinner table now reads as national evidence. A daughter saying no is heard at once as personal betrayal and as a data point in the depopulation panic. There is no register left between those two.

Someone who organizes for Antinatalism Japan, the group that formed in 2021 and has since run monthly street outreach events, could tell you what actually passes between members at these gatherings, and it is not doctrine. Across such groups people recognize each other through shared histories of mental illness, workplace harassment, social ostracization — and through one word, ikizurasa, the pain of living, which they needed and their parents did not. That is why the two camps cannot land a punch on each other. The ethical claim is offered as impersonal and so cannot be audited by asking about anyone's family; the demographic reading explains a curve but not why this cohort required a vocabulary for a refusal that is centuries old. The word is the tell. It is not a premise in an argument about the world's unfitness for life; it names what it costs to be someone's insurance against death and to decline the job, and the stall in the dispute is only the two camps agreeing to look past that.
