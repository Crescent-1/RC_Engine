[PASSAGE]

"Sleep," in the literature of the instruments, has meant whatever the instrument was built to score, and for half a century the scoring has been done by proxy. The dispute is old. From the 1970s, when sleep laboratories began strapping accelerometers to the wrists of subjects whose electrodes had been removed, two habits of mind competed. One trusted the diary: the sleeper's own account, rich, contextual, and warped by mood, by ambition, by the way memory rounds a bad night down. The other trusted the device: consistent, cheap, indifferent to self-flattery, and blind to nearly everything the diary knew. Epidemiological cohorts of the 1990s and 2000s settled the matter administratively rather than intellectually — you cannot wire ten thousand people — and the consumer wearables that followed inherited that settlement without inheriting its caveats.

What the wrist unit does not do is worth setting out. It does not read cortical activity. It does not detect unconsciousness, or the loss of responsiveness, or the disengagement from the room that a person means by falling asleep. It does not observe sleep architecture; it produces a curve labelled with architecture's vocabulary. It is a movement detector carrying sleep's name, and the inference from one to the other rests on an algorithm calibrated against populations who happen to move in ordinary ways when they sleep — and, crucially, who move when they lie awake. Every claim the instrument makes about a night depends on that second half of the assumption, which is the half nobody writes down.

Consider the protocol in one lab running a climate-and-sleep cohort: a wrist unit worn continuously, motion binned into thirty-second epochs, each epoch scored by a proprietary threshold as sleep or wake, the whole night reconstructed from that binary string. The design is careful. Its calibration was validated, its drift checked, its epochs aligned to a clock. During a heat-wave collection run, the analysts noticed something the software could not: long, clean, unbroken sleep bouts in the small hours, from participants whose diaries for those same hours read, in various wordings, that they had lain awake because it was too hot to move.

Read as a calibration curiosity, that mismatch is a footnote. It is not one. Thermal discomfort above roughly body temperature suppresses gross motor activity without inducing sleep — the body becomes still because moving is worse than not moving — and stillness is the only evidence the instrument accepts. So the error is not scattered randomly across nights, cancelling out in aggregate as measurement noise is supposed to do. It concentrates precisely on the hot, humid nights that the cohort exists to study, and it runs in one direction: toward more sleep, better sleep, less harm. The diary, that unreliable thing, was right about the one variable the device was deployed to detect. Every estimate of heat-related sleep loss drawn from actigraphic cohorts is therefore likely conservative, and conservative by an unknown margin, which means the case for trusting the wrist over the sleeper's own report — a case built on the diary's bias, and one this account has otherwise accepted — must be conceded only for nights when nothing external holds the body still, and abandoned for exactly the nights that now matter most.
