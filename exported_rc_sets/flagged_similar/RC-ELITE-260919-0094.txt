A work a person makes about her own life must either report that life or shape it, and a viewer needs to know which before deciding what is owed to it. Two camps, both reasonable, have settled on Charlotte Salomon's painted sequence. The first treats it as evidence. A young woman in Villefranche-sur-Mer, between 1941 and 1942, set down a Berlin household, a line of suicides, an exile, a closing year — a witness's record, to be checked, catalogued, believed. The second treats it as invention. The gouaches are small, fast, ruthless about colour; scenes repeat like refrains; sheets of tracing paper carry captions and speech across the images, and named songs and pieces are called for, to be heard while looking. The names are changed. Painters do that. Both camps can point at the same paper and find their reasons there.

Read the quarrel again and it wobbles. Each side asks what the finished object is and whom it addresses. But the sequence behaves less like an object than like a procedure its maker was conducting while it grew — a way of getting through a year. What the making was doing for her at the time, neither camp touches.

Her title offers two alternatives, and they are not alternatives. On the pages the theatre is the only place where the life can be staged at all, which makes the second term the condition of the first rather than its rival. The apparatus is a production's: a prompter's script on the overlays, a score supplied by the cues, a cast under altered names, a curtain of tracing paper lowered over every image. Productions, though, are mounted for someone, and the house here is dark; no informant can say who was meant to be sitting in it. The late textual admission, the disturbing one near the close, stays unsettled for exactly this reason. A stage can confess without confessing. The confession is performed, and performance is not the opposite of truth, only a different claim on it.

The institutions have each built a shrine of their own. A Jewish museum holds the work as Holocaust-era testimony; a London art academy hung it as painting; archivists number it as a gouache series; directors have made an opera of it; critics file it beside picture-narrative and graphic memoir. Every box answers a question about what a maker claims on a viewer. None was built for work made to stay alive by.

She handed the pages over, according to the account long attached to them, to the friend who had sheltered her, with the words that this was her whole life; a year later she was murdered at Auschwitz. The sentence is reported, not annotated, and it will not do the work a label does. It does not say whether the pages were addressed to a reader, a doctor, a future, or nobody. Leaving the work unclassified is therefore the correct verdict on what survives: the sheets, an ending that is not dated, and no testimony from the one person who could fix the intention. That refusal is not a posture but a limit, and it holds until either a category exists for work performed as a means of continuing to live, or something turns up that settles it — and even then the darkened house she painted towards would still be unaccounted for.
