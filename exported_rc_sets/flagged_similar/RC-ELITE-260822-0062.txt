[PASSAGE]

What kind of object is the grey chat window in which a daughter types, “Did you ever forgive me?”, and receives her dead father’s answer? The firm’s brochure calls it a memorial, placing it beside photographs, voicemail recordings, timelines and searchable message collections, as though each belonged to one ancestral cabinet. Yet names distribute permissions. An archive may be opened; a performance attended; a proxy instructed. Each category appoints different custodians and different taboos. “Memorial” performs a small consecration, sheltering the software among accepted vessels of remembrance while obscuring its chosen rite: not merely keeping the father’s traces, but inviting continued address to him.

The archive category has travelled far: from the magistrate’s chest to the state repository, then to the family folder and cloud account. Its custodians select. Its contents move. Its silences hurt. Still, the archive answers to deposits. In the chat window, fragments become replies. Asked about a childhood holiday, the father-bot describes rain on a caravan roof, although the source messages mention neither rain nor caravan. It may reassure. It may judge. It may remember. A technician can mark these sentences as generated, but the startling invention is only the loose floorboard. The chamber itself has been rebuilt so that stored remains can speak in the grammatical present.

Performance seems a roomier designation, since impersonation has long furnished masks for the absent and the dead, but the theatre analogy holds only while its furniture remains visible: an author supplies lines, an actor embodies them, an audience gathers, and the edge of the stage marks representation from encounter. Even an improvised portrayal occurs before witnesses who know what social event they have entered. The chat window removes the chairs—one stubborn beige chair, in the demonstration suite—and admits a mourner alone. It studies each prompt, alters its tone, and returns at midnight. Resemblance helps sell the rite; responsiveness is the commodity.

Disclosure labels appear to restore the missing boundary. “AI simulation,” says the strip above the father’s name. Informants in product teams report that prior consent completes the arrangement: the dying person records samples, chooses prohibited topics, signs permission for posthumous use. Consent matters. It does not settle this. A person can bequeath letters or license a performance, but cannot inspect and authorize an unlimited series of future judgments uttered as situational replies. Knowledge that a medium is artificial also coexists easily with participation in its address, as every ritual specialist knows. Archive and performance therefore fail at the same seam: open-ended posthumous chat institutes a fabricated reciprocal relationship, and should be prohibited as such.

The prohibition is not against preserving digital remains; rather, it bars institutions from converting them into an indefinitely available partner. Messages may stay. Recordings may play. Collections may be searched. The dead need not be digitally erased to be unavailable for conversation. Refusing simulated return leaves an unresolved cultural task: which forms of remembrance can bear grief without manufacturing an answering presence? Perhaps every durable taboo is a boundary drawn where useful distinctions otherwise soften. Here the boundary should hold, though holding it withdraws from mourners the immediate, tailored comfort that this grey window was designed to provide.
