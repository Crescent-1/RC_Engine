[PASSAGE]

Which came first in Gaza after October 2023 — the dying or the ruin of the work? The standard telling is careful, and I want to grant it what it deserves. Casualty tallies were compiled under conditions that made compilation itself dangerous: killed, wounded, displaced, structures destroyed, each figure hedged and revised. Alongside those tallies, in a smaller register, ran an economic footnote — that the Strip's modest outsourced software sector, a few hundred people writing code for European and Gulf clients, had ceased to exist. Reported side by side, the two facts assumed an order. First the killing and the flight; then, downstream, some months later, the accounting of lost livelihoods. The arrow ran one way, from bodies to income, and nobody had to argue for it.

The arrow does not survive contact with the timeline. Consider a small development team of the kind that existed there in numbers — described to me in the flat sequential manner such people use when reconstructing an outage. The power went. The backup generators had fuel for days, not weeks. Within roughly a week the clients were unreachable, the repository unreachable, the shared channel dark, and the delivery deadlines that sustained a decade of contracts had already been missed — before most of the team had been killed, and before most had been displaced. That order matters beyond Gaza now, because the same fragile arrangement — remote labour reaching wealthy clients through a single contested cable — is how a growing share of the world's poorest skilled workers eat.

So the two destructions share a mechanism. The team's economic existence had always rested on uptime. Uninterrupted uptime. Nothing else held a Rotterdam client for eleven years. But the wires that carried that uptime ran through the same infrastructure that made a neighbourhood addressable, powered, lit, locatable. I am not claiming anyone intended the sector's death, and I am not claiming the counters were indifferent. The claim is narrower and stranger: the network that made these people employable was the network that made them findable, and its severing was, in the same hours, the end of the work and a precondition of the danger. One act. Two ruins. No sequence between them.

Any count must choose a unit. Person, household, structure — these are the available shapes, and each can be verified by someone standing in front of it. None of them is a decade-old client relationship with a firm in Rotterdam. None is a repository three people edited together at two in the morning across two neighbourhoods. That object cannot be killed, because it was never alive; it cannot be displaced, because it had no location; it cannot be wounded. It simply stops appearing. I have no better metric to offer, and I distrust the impulse to supply one, because the trouble is not that the instrument is coarse. A life and a working professional world are not two magnitudes of one quantity. Summing the first with perfect accuracy tells you nothing whatever about the second — the aggregation is not incomplete, it is orthogonal. Every emergency count is built to answer the question standing in front of it. Which leaves open what such an instrument, honestly and precisely operated, is doing to the questions nobody thought to put to it.
