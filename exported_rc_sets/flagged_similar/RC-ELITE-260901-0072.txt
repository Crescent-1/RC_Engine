[PASSAGE]

"Accountability," in the targeting trade, has meant something narrow and auditable: a named person who can be asked why a particular building was struck on a particular afternoon, and who possessed, at the moment of authorization, enough information to answer. By that standard the profession has largely stopped believing that a human sign-off preserves accountability in AI-assisted kill chains. The belief did not die by argument. It died by attrition, as review windows shortened and target lists lengthened, until the signature at the bottom of the strike package certified nothing except that a person had been present. The doctrine remains in the manuals. It stopped paying out years ago.

The obvious objection is that a person who clicks approve has still chosen, and can still be held to the choice, since the machine only nominates. Consider what the choice consists of. An operator with three minutes per nomination — sometimes four seconds, in the systems built for volume — receives a name, a confidence score, a structure, and a collateral estimate, and cannot re-derive any of it. He can decline. He cannot check. When a program officer says, as one did in a public briefing, that "a human is always in the loop," the sentence is true and empty in the same breath: the loop contains a human the way a turnstile contains a passenger. The errors were not caused by human review. They were caused by review that had been hollowed while keeping its name.

So slow the loop, the second objection runs; buy the operator twenty minutes. Time is indeed necessary — nobody verifies anything in four seconds — but the arithmetic runs the wrong way. These systems earn their budgets by fusing signals intelligence, pattern-of-life data, and imagery faster than a staff of analysts could read the summaries, and generating nominations at a rate no staff could adjudicate. Give each nomination twenty honest minutes and the queue collapses to the throughput of the old cell of twelve analysts, which is precisely the throughput the procurement was written to escape. Slowing the loop does not repair the system. It switches it off and leaves the hardware running.

A third objection is harder, and it survives: humans still write the rules of engagement, set the confidence thresholds, define what counts as a military-age male in a compound at dusk, and no model originates those commitments. That is real agency, exercised by identifiable people, and it constrains every strike downstream. What it cannot do is validate a particular strike against particular evidence — a satellite pass eleven days stale, a phone that changed hands, a courtyard reconfigured since the last collection. Upstream rule-setting is necessary for legitimate targeting and cannot stand in for the judgment a specific afternoon requires; when the compound turns out to have held a wedding party, the rule-writer authorized a category and the operator authorized nothing he could have known.

The field was right to stop believing, and the honest successor doctrine is uglier than either camp wants: accountability in these chains is partial, distributed, and in some cases genuinely absent — some strikes are authorized by nobody, in the auditable sense with which this began. There is no midpoint between that admission and the manuals, no formula that splits the difference and leaves the tempo intact. The bill comes as fewer nominations cleared per day, analysts hired back, and an error rate that institutions must own by name rather than assign to a signature that, whatever else it certified, certified no knowledge at all.
