The empathy account of contagious yawning was consolidated in the 1990s and early 2000s, when researchers began sorting yawn responses by the observer's relationship to the yawner. The gradient was orderly. Kin yawned back most, then friends, then acquaintances, then strangers, with the effect thinning as social distance widened. Around the same time the mirror-neuron literature was expanding to cover emotional contagion generally, and the yawn slotted in neatly: a small involuntary rehearsal of another body's state, low-cost, unwilled, and therefore taken as evidence that fellow-feeling runs below deliberation. On this reading, closeness supplied the necessary condition. People who did not yawn back were understood to be people who had not, in the relevant sense, registered the other person at all — and autism and psychopathy research was recruited to firm up the inference.

A quieter tradition treated the same gradient as noise. On this account yawn contagion is a perceptual-motor reflex: a conspicuous facial motion enters the visual system and triggers a matching motor program, in the way a sudden gape or a stretch does, with no representation of the other's inner state involved anywhere. The social gradient survives, but as a by-product of viewing conditions. One looks longer, and more directly, at a spouse across a table than at a stranger on a platform, so the stimulus is delivered more reliably to the people one is close to. Closeness produces looking; looking produces the yawn. Nothing about what the yawn means enters the chain, and the correlation that seemed to license a theory of mind is instead a fact about who shares a room with whom.

Then someone measured the looking. The design was plain. Participants watched short videos — yawns, and control clips of the same faces performing non-yawn mouth movements — while an eye-tracker logged fixation durations region by region, eyes against mouth. Contagion was scored afterward from the participants' own behaviour on tape. The result did not sort the way either camp had prepared for. Time on the mouth predicted subsequent yawning; time on the eyes did not, or did so more weakly. The reflex theorists might claim this, but only by forgetting that their motion-trigger story was indifferent to region, and the empathy theorists had assumed the eyes, being where the state is read, would carry the effect. Both had staked something on gaze landing where their signal lived.

What both had assumed, and neither had examined, is that contagion is caused by taking in a signal about the yawn — its emotional freight or its automatic imitability. Fixation data point instead at something duller: exposure to the motion, unaccompanied by any construal of it, in a region of the face that carries very little social information and a great deal of movement. The gradient can stay; it costs nothing to grant that intimates yawn at each other more. Granting it also concedes that fifty years of sorting subjects by relationship measured the wrong variable, and that the question with a future in it is not what passes between two people when one yawns but why the visual system rates an opening mouth as a category of motion worth copying at all — as it rates a nine-centimetre gape above a chewing jaw filmed from the identical angle.
