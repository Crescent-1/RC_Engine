[PASSAGE]

The practice of issuing bronze citizenship diplomas to discharged auxiliaries seems to begin under Claudius, in the middle of the first century, and it hardened fast. Batches were the thing. An emperor issued a constitutio covering named units in a named province; scribes copied the master text onto paired bronze leaves; the veteran carried his tablet home and, with luck, buried it somewhere a farmer would later strike with a plough. From Britain to Syria the interval reads the same: twenty-five years, then citizenship, then conubium. Hundreds of tablets, one number. Historians reached for coinage as the comparison, and the comparison holds better than most — a denarius struck at Lugdunum and one struck at Rome pass at the same weight because a distant machinery of assay and blank and die keeps them equal, and equality across distance is the only proof of the machinery anyone gets. The diplomas looked like that. Same fineness, same alloy, minted at the edges of a world that had no reason to agree with itself. I should sharpen that. The uniformity was not itself the accomplishment being commemorated; the tablets commemorate individual service, and the flatness of the number across three continents is simply the residue a functioning apparatus leaves behind. Still, it is the flatness historians came to lean on. Twenty-five became a load-bearing fact.

Then a diploma turned up in Egypt, a fragment naming what reads as Cohors I Thebaeorum, granting citizenship after sixteen years. The consular date is worn down the right edge; the unit abbreviation admits at least two expansions; and the first response among papyrologists was that it had to be a fake or a bad reading, which is the response the corpus taught them to give. It is tempting to say the gap was simply too large to credit. But a three-year discrepancy would have been dismissed just as briskly, and a twelve-year one no more so — the scale of the anomaly was doing no work. What did the work was absence. Nothing in the corpus had ever recorded an early grant of any size, and a thing with no comparanda cannot be graded as mildly or wildly improbable. It can only be doubted entire.

The fragment survived checking. An independent roster placed the unit where the tablet places it, and a second fragment carried a matching batch-number, which is not the sort of coincidence a forger manufactures. So the rule held and the rule was broken, and the two facts do not cancel. What had looked like a contest between a rigid norm and a rogue document was never that. The norm was law; the exception was an emperor's authority operating on law without repealing it — a distinction the corpus, being made of outcomes rather than deliberations, is very nearly built to hide. Everything drawn from the tablets about how Rome managed its non-citizens now inherits that hidden layer.

The occasion was plague. A cohort emptied by the Antonine sickness was discharged early so it could be rebuilt, citizenship offered as inducement and recorded as reward. The cost is legible in what we know: that cohort has a history only because a catastrophe was violent enough to break the rule that dissolved everyone else into a number. Anomalies preserve; regularity forgets — though it would be rash to say the archive was designed that way.
