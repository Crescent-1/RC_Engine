[PASSAGE]

When private boatbuilders were licensed to work outside the Arsenale's naval discipline in the sixteenth century, the squeri that spread along Venice's smaller canals inherited, or are generally said to have inherited, a body of rules so stable that the gondola is now described in guild literature and tourist copy alike as eight centuries transmitted intact.

What we can observe at the bench is narrower. A gondola is built crooked on purpose: the hull swells to the right so that a single oar, worked on one side, drives it straight. That much is fixed. The rest is not. The squerarolo working a hull for its second or third life strips back the damaged strakes, decides plank by plank which will take steam and which are past it, and then — this is the part no brochure records — re-establishes the curve by eye, sighting down the length, shaving, sighting again. No template is consulted. The pitch goes on when the humidity and the tide permit, not when the schedule does.

This is where the parallel with Venice's other named heritage crafts fails. Murano glass has recipes: proportions of silica and soda, timings, colours reproducible across generations because the formula is the thing transmitted. A gondola's asymmetry has no such formula, or none that survives contact with a particular boat, because the quantity being reproduced is a balance between a hull, a stretch of water, and one man's weight and stroke — and that balance is not a number anyone has succeeded in writing down. The squerarolo who has worked forty years does not know the correct offset; he knows how to find the correct offset for this boatman, which is a different kind of knowing, and which must be found again when the boatman ages or the boat changes hands. What the label calls tradition maintained is, on the evidence of the bench, judgement re-performed. The rule was never the thing passed on. What was passed on was the capacity to reach a result no rule specifies, under conditions that shift between one restoration and the next.

The squeraroli interviewed in the Venetian craft ethnographies of the last several decades tend to describe the forcola — the carved oarlock, cut to a particular rower — in terms like reading the water off a man's shoulders. It is a good phrase, and it is also a practitioner's account of his own work, which is a weaker sort of evidence than watching him cut. Take the instrument analogy seriously and it cuts in an awkward direction. A violin is tuned to a pitch that exists independently of the tuner. Here there is no reference tone. The craftsman tunes toward a pitch he establishes in the tuning, and the next hull will require a different one, and nothing in the workshop can adjudicate between them except the boat's behaviour on the water afterwards. That is a real achievement and it is not the achievement being advertised. Whether it deserves the word tradition is a question our own instruments handle badly: we have been using continuity of rules as the measure, and this craft does not store its content in rules, so our measure reports an absence where something demonstrably persists — and reports it at the cost of missing what the persistence actually consists of.
