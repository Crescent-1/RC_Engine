Longevity becomes intelligible when duration can be translated into a sequence of biological events. For students of deep-water black corals, the annual-band assumption has offered that translation: successive increments of skeleton become successive years, allowing a living colony to be assigned an age otherwise inaccessible to ordinary observation. The undertaking concerns survival across centuries, sometimes millennia. Or, more carefully, it concerns the conversion by which a claim about survival acquires its numerical scale. Where marine ecology sees endurance under a sparse and intermittent food supply, palaeoclimatology sees a possible succession of past ocean conditions preserved in skeletal material. Both have reason to consult the skeleton's calendar, though they seek different entries. A long-lived colony might preserve changes that no instrument was present to witness; its continued growth might also reveal how animal life persists where meals arrive from far above. A direct test can be considered through a hypothetical calibration study, whose field arrangements and laboratory results are specified here rather than attributed to an actual expedition. Its starting point is living growth under continuing observation.

Divers fix numbered tags and small settlement plates beside twelve colonies at a Hawaiian observatory 320 metres down, leaving current meters, thermistors and sediment traps operating through fourteen years of service visits. Calling this merely a prolonged census mistakes the design: the instruments follow water, temperature and falling food while the tagged colonies supply a common interval of exposure.

In the laboratory, narrow radial wafers come from naturally shed branch tips and two permitted biopsy cores. Researchers polish these pages flat. Reflected and transmitted light photograph the script. Three readers count its density transitions, blind to recovery dates. Like annual entries awaiting translation, the marks retain their order while their dates remain concealed from the readers.

The fourteen known years contain nineteen to twenty-seven single dark-pale transitions. Paired transitions align more closely with recurring late-summer pulses in trapped organic particles. This does not abolish the skeleton's calendar; it locates its uncertain units in the microscopic lines themselves. A seasonal supply of food can leave recurrent marks without making every visible division an anniversary.

The interval is known. The lines exceed it. Pairing changes the fit. Any age or environmental history constructed by carrying the single-line conversion backwards now loses its assumed yearly spacing. A long chronology could contract; a supposed winter signal could move into another season. Yet those consequences attach to a conversion, not indiscriminately to black corals. Where the band reader works outward through successive skeletal additions, isotope dating approaches elapsed time through a different physical process; dates from other sites may still sustain substantial ages. Nor does this interval establish that another species cannot deposit annual bands. The sediment-trap material provides a third comparison, between food arrival and growth, but its closer alignment with paired transitions does not license replacing one universal counting rule with another. For this species at this depth, one visible band cannot presently stand for one year, and ages obtained solely by that multiplication are uncalibrated. The admissible inquiry is how elapsed time maps onto growth within that restricted case, beginning with the nineteen to twenty-seven transitions in these wafers.
