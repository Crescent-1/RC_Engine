[PASSAGE]

Since the postwar decades, when development economists began recommending cadastral surveys to newly independent states, the title deed has been imagined as a key. My own instinct is to distrust that image, and I should say plainly that the instinct is partly nostalgia for older forms of holding land, which I will try not to let do my arguing for me. Formalisation, strictly, means a state-issued and state-registered record of a specific parcel, mapped, indexed, transferable, enforceable in a court that will hear the case. It is not a chief's acknowledgement. It is not a receipt of purchase, nor a lineage's long memory of who planted which hedge. Those are ownership; they are not registration. The distinction now carries more weight than it did in 1960, because far more parties — banks, agencies, buyers from outside the district, wives claiming against husbands — must read the same claim without knowing the village.

The alleged sequence runs as follows. Registration fixes the boundary; a fixed boundary lengthens the farmer's horizon; a long horizon makes terracing and tree planting worth the labour; and a document a lender can seize converts land into collateral, so credit widens. I have stated that too smoothly. The chain has four links, and only the first is performed by the registrar; each of the others requires someone else to act — a farmer with spare labour, a bank with a branch and an appetite for foreclosure on a smallholding it cannot easily resell. Formalisation is not investment. It is not credit. It is the precondition that its advocates have persistently mistaken for the thing itself.

The Kenyan study did not simply compare titled and untitled districts, which would have told us mainly which districts get titled. Rather, villages were assigned different registration dates. Plots were visited before and after. Loan ledgers at local lenders were checked; terraces and bunds were counted on the ground; households were surveyed on disputes, and women were interviewed separately from husbands.

The instances test the definition rather than flattering it. Boundary markers multiplied — stones, planted sisal, cut lines. Terracing rose, modestly. Dispute claims fell, but chiefly where the map had settled a contested edge, not where the quarrel was about inheritance among brothers. Formal borrowing barely moved. Women's recorded rights improved more clearly than any household's income did, and those two findings should not be read as one finding. A farmer who builds a terrace because he is no longer afraid of losing the slope has gained something real. He has not thereby become richer in any measure the survey could find within four years.

So the key opened a gate at the edge of the field, not a vault. That is a smaller claim than sixty years of policy has wanted, and I hold to it: registration is defensibility made legible, and legibility is worth having on its own terms — for the widow especially — but it does not manufacture the lender, the market, or the surplus labour that the older argument quietly assumed. Longer follow-up may yet find credit effects that four years hid. Other credit conditions would have to change first. I am willing to be shown wrong on the timing and not, I think, on the mechanism. A key is only as useful as whatever stands behind the door, and in most of these villages what stands behind it is a field.
