What does it mean for an animal to stop existing when nobody was trying to make it stop? The question sat unasked for most of a century because the case that best posed it was misfiled as something else. Melanoplus spretus, the Rocky Mountain locust, produced the largest insect aggregations ever measured by anyone with an interest in measuring them — the swarm crossing Nebraska in June 1875 was estimated by Albert Child, working from telegraph reports of its width and the time it took to pass, at something on the order of a hundred and twenty billion insects. Then the swarms thinned through the 1880s. The last living specimens were collected in 1902. Not reduced, not scattered into pockets, not surviving in some unsurveyed valley: gone as a taxon, and never seen alive again. That the vanishing followed hard upon the plague years is the first thing anyone notices about it, and the least useful.

The explanation offered at the time was sober and, by the standards of the field, well grounded. Locusts are phase-shifting animals. Crowded, they turn gregarious — darker, longer-winged, migratory; sparse, they revert to a solitary form so unlike the swarming one that early workers classed the two as separate species. Every other locust of consequence, the desert locust above all, had been observed to sink into recession for decades and then return. Spretus, it was assumed, had done the same, retiring to its Rocky Mountain refuges to wait. The parallel was clean. It was also false, because recession requires a place to recede to, and nobody had asked where that place was.

Jeffrey Lockwood's soil cores, taken through the 1980s and after, located it: narrow strips of moist, sandy alluvium along river valleys in the northern Rockies, where the species bred permanently rather than opportunistically. The desert locust recedes across millions of square kilometres of arid ground; spretus recessed onto a handful of riverbank acres. Treat the two as the same animal in different moods and the analogy holds. Treat the ground beneath them and it collapses — one had a continent to hide in, the other had a corridor. Homesteaders plowed and irrigated those exact valleys, because those were the only soils worth farming. The refuge and the farmland were the same dirt.

So the insect was exterminated by the people it ruined, without their knowing they were doing it. That is the usual formulation, and it flatters everyone involved by making the outcome sound like a moral irony rather than a technical one. What the farmers lacked was not conscience but a concept: obligate breeding habitat did not exist as an idea until decades later. No survey existed. No one had reason to think a plague could have an address. The precedent is invoked now in arguments about whether narrowly breeding pests might be deliberately eliminated — or are being eliminated already, unnoticed.

Whether the soil chemistry of those valleys can be reconstructed finely enough to predict which insects sit on similarly thin ground is not a question historical entomology can answer, and I take it that the paleoentomologists working glacier-preserved specimens and the soil scientists reading old alluvium are the ones who should have it. Handing it over costs the historical account its authority over its own best case.
