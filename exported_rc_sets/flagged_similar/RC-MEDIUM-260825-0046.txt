What earns the name spinoff? Not shared characters, not a shared universe, not a nod in the ninth episode. I want the word to do real work, so I fix it narrowly: a spinoff transfers a parent narrative's centre of consequence to an institution the parent kept at its margins, while remaining answerable to the history the parent has already altered. Two conditions, both required. On the face of it Apple TV's Star City satisfies them handsomely — the camera leaves Houston for the Soviet training grounds, and the story's engine shifts from American disappointment to Soviet achievement, which is precisely the kind of relocation I have in mind. Yet the moment you sit with the first two hours, the term starts to strain. Nobody you know from For All Mankind walks through the door. The plot closes on itself; a viewer arriving cold loses almost nothing of the immediate story. Both facts tempt one to say the thing isn't a spinoff at all, merely a new show wearing borrowed livery, and I understand the temptation. But those facts test the wrong half of my definition. Familiar faces were never among the conditions, and self-containment says nothing about answerability. What matters is whether the Soviet institution's decisions carry weight because of a history the parent series bent, and here they plainly do: the lunar race the cosmonauts are running is not the one that happened, and the show never pauses to explain the difference. That silence is the strongest evidence of dependence I can find. The verdict, then, survives — but on narrower ground than the opening resemblance suggested.

Watch the relocation actually operate. Training regimens, engineering rivalries, a KGB officer who reads flight logs as character evidence: each of these belongs comfortably to a genre with its own furniture, the workplace series and the Cold War thriller, and each explains a good part of why the hours pass so easily. I would not argue against either label. They simply operate one storey below the question. Genre describes the texture of scenes; my definition concerns where consequence sits. The alternate lunar timeline is not a flavouring on top of office politics — it is the condition under which an engineer's memo becomes fatal, and no workplace convention supplies that.

Press a case. A cosmonaut rehearses a docking manoeuvre; an engineer bargains with a party official for tooling; a surveillance thread turns professional loyalty into political exposure. Call these workplace, espionage, alternate history — all three fit, and a viewer could name a genre for each without error. Fitting a scene is not classifying a series. The force of these episodes depends on the parent being watched from what it treated as background.

So: a spinoff, yes, but only in the strict structural sense I set out. Not a continuation. Not a reunion. A transfer of narrative centrality, and nothing warmer than that. Star City is less a new branch of For All Mankind than a demonstration, limited but clean, that a franchise can change its historical subject while dragging every gear of the old machine behind it. That machinery is what the cosmonauts are answerable to, whether or not they ever mention Houston.
