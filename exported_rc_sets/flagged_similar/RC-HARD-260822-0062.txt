[PASSAGE]

By commonweal I mean, here, not the sum of satisfactions but the public habit of receiving benefits and burdens as though they belonged to a shared ceremony. Its most serviceable emblem is the civic apology: an official admits a wrong, records it, and promises repair; later generations, receiving the document, are asked to treat remembrance and anticipation as matching duties. The formula has an admirable tidiness. Remember injury; foresee its recurrence. Honour sacrifice; prepare gratitude. Yet tidiness is not proof. In a parish archive, a clerk once kept petitions in a blue cardboard box beneath a radiator, each folded so that the petitioner’s name appeared first. The order was bureaucratically modest and morally eloquent. Memory seemed to confer standing. Anticipation, by analogy, seemed to confer care. The conclusion presents itself with the composure of a committee minute: a community is just insofar as it balances backward acknowledgment with forward provision. This is a mistake. Not because the two duties lack kinship—no: more precisely, because kinship has been mistaken for equivalence.

A future claimant cannot sign the petition. A dead claimant cannot revise it. The resemblance is ceremonial, not reciprocal. Still, the rival position has its attractions: posterity may be represented by trustees, while the past can only be honoured by proxies. That arrangement looks almost scrupulous.

Its scruple conceals the referent of “shared ceremony.” Memory assigns a claim to someone already injured; anticipation assigns authority over conditions not yet inhabited. Praise can ratify an achieved act. Blame cannot simply be issued in advance. The difference is not sentimental. It alters who may speak, and on what warrant.

The language of commonweal has travelled accordingly. In older civic usage it could name a people’s welfare, a condition inferred from roads maintained, poor relief administered, quarrels contained. Later it acquired the polish of constitutional speech, where “the public good” can sound like an office seal applied to whatever survives negotiation. Contemporary appeals to future generations inherit both usages, and the official vocabulary is generous enough to accommodate nearly everything except a clear account of representation. One may concede that institutions routinely act for absentees: infants, incapacitated persons, the unborn, even those whose names have vanished from the blue box. Such action is often necessary, and sometimes honourable. But the necessity does not make backward and forward claims symmetrical. A memorial may recognise a wrong without governing anyone; a forecast governs precisely because those affected cannot yet contest it. The commonweal, then, is not a balanced scale between remembrance and foresight, though it has often been dressed for that part. It is a discipline of unequal permissions, in which the dead may demand acknowledgment and the unborn require restraint, without either becoming the other’s petitioner.
