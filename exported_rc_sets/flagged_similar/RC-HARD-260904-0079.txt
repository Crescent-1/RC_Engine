A record is a machine for producing a certain kind of silence. The Naturalization Act of 1906 built one: a printed federal petition, identical in Boston and in Butte, with fixed fields for name, age, occupation, port of arrival, vessel, date of landing, the names of two witnesses, and the renunciation of allegiance to a named sovereign. Before it, thousands of courts had improvised their own paperwork and kept it, or did not. The argument that followed was usually staged as one about honesty — whether the new form would catch cheats the old ones let through. That framing mistook the instrument for a filter. The form was not primarily an obstacle placed in the path of the fraudulent; it was a device for producing a national quantity where none had existed, and the quantity, once produced, began to be read as a description of something.

The data came out clean. By the 1910s a federal officer could tabulate, by year and by county, exactly how many people had become citizens — a precision the reformers of the period, and the statistical bureaus that consumed their output, treated as civic hygiene made visible. The archivists who later processed these files were less certain. What the form counted, they noticed, was completed petitions. The two are not the same population.

The reform did correct genuine abuses, and this should be said at length. Judges in some jurisdictions had naturalized men in batches in the weeks before elections, minutes apiece, witnesses supplied by the party clerk at the door. Residency requirements were checked with an indulgence that varied by courtroom and by nationality. After 1906 the same claim had to be made twice, years apart, in writing, before a witness who could be recalled. That is a real improvement in the honesty of the record. It is not, however, an improvement in the record's coverage. The instrument became more truthful about what it recorded without becoming more capacious about what it recorded — and the second property, not the first, is what determines whether a count can stand in for a population.

The form imposed a sequence: a declaration of intention, then a waiting period, then a petition for final admission. Missing the second step reads, in the ledger, as an unfinished process. That reading is too quick. The gap was not an interval of becoming; it was an interval in which nothing was asked. Petitioners moved counties, missed hearings, let declarations expire, and no field existed in which a clerk might record why.

One file from a midwestern county court runs this way: a declaration filed, a petition lapsed, a gap of nine years, a second declaration, a petition granted. Between the two dates the man paid monthly dues to a mutual aid society, sent three children to English-language night school, and was vouched for at his hearing by neighbors who described him as having been part of the town for as long as they could remember. None of that has a column. The files are now read by demographers, by genealogists, by algorithms trained to reconstruct migration flows — more readers, and more consequential ones, than the clerk who filed them ever imagined. A count is honest about its own procedure and mute about everything the procedure declined to ask; this one is mute for exactly nine years, between a stamped lapse and a stamped grant.
