[PASSAGE]

A category, in the trade sense that governs a review page, is not a description of a book but a promise about what the reading will do to a child. On that understanding, the picture book in question — some forty pages, perhaps a hundred and twenty words in total, following a bloom of moon jellyfish as it fills a bay between dusk and the following dusk — belongs to none of the three categories a reviewer has ready. This is regularly reported as a defect of the book, and the language of the reporting is instructive: it is called hard to place, or unhelpfully quiet, or lacking a clear hook. The vocabulary is commercial and the fault is assigned to the object. But the surface phenomenon of unplaceability has a cause underneath it, and the cause sits on the shelf rather than in the book. The categories were built to sort narratives of blame and rescue. A bloom is neither, and so the sorting apparatus reports an error where none has occurred.

One might suppose this is merely sloppiness of the ordinary kind: a publisher who declined to pick a lane, an editor who let ambiguity pass for depth, a marketing department that could have fixed the whole matter with a subtitle. Assign the book a firmer badge and the trouble evaporates. The supposition is tidy. It is also wrong, and wrong in a way the pages themselves settle. Nothing here is undecided. The bloom arrives, thickens, disperses. The book knows exactly what it is doing at every spread; what it declines to do is supply the reader with a party to hold responsible.

Two categories present themselves with the confidence of old family friends. The warm bay and the too-still water invite ecological warning; the shimmering column of bells under moonlight invites wonder. Both readings have grown more urgent as blooms have become an economic fact for fishing fleets and coastal power stations, which is precisely why reviewers reach for them. But these are not two moods along one spectrum. Warning requires a cause and a threatened party; wonder requires a beholder whose amazement is the point. The book supplies neither. It is like a summons issued with no defendant named and no offence recited — correctly formatted, ceremonially delivered, and legally empty. The reader who mistakes the two categories for near-synonyms will not notice that the book has evaded each on separate grounds.

The third category fits closest, and closeness is the trap. Blooms are widely tied to the removal of predators, so a cautionary tale about overfishing seems built for this book. The term has travelled: from moral fable, to conservation pamphlet, to the classroom nature unit, to the review column, shedding at each stage a little of what a tale requires. Here it fails on its own terms. No trawler appears. No absence is dramatized. No comeuppance arrives. The jellyfish surge because conditions permit, and disperse when conditions change, and nobody is punished.

So the failure is not the book's. It has found something the categories were never constructed to hold: abundance at population scale, indifferent, with no villain and no victim to award. The price of saying so is that the review page must now justify its own three boxes rather than the book its refusal of them, and that burden falls on whoever next writes the phrase hard to place.
