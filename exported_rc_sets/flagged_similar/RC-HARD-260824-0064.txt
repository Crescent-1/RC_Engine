The vote took four minutes. I have the minutes in front of me — one paragraph, item 7c, "reclassification of after-hours emergency intake, psychiatric presentations only," carried 5–2, with an attached staffing memo about overnight physician coverage that nobody at the table read aloud. I sat in the back row of that room in the spring of 2015 as a stringer for a paper that has since folded, and I remember thinking the whole thing was procedural housekeeping, the sort of item you don't file. For two years afterward I covered the reroutes: ambulances turning north at the county line, the extra nineteen minutes, then the deaths that started appearing in the coroner's summaries with a phrase I learned to look for, "prolonged transport interval." What I did not expect was that the fight would eventually be about paternity. Administrators now say the harm was fathered by a 2017 reimbursement cut and that the 2015 vote was an inert clause until then. Paramedics say the harm was born that night in the boardroom and everything after was weather. Both sides speak of origin, of the first decision, of where the line properly begins. Both assume the question has an answer, and for a long stretch of reporting I assumed it with them, and worked as though the archive would eventually hand one over.

The administrators' file is thick. Their memos show the restriction was copied, almost clause for clause, from a program two counties east that had run three years without a single adverse transport outcome. Projected risk: negligible. They budgeted for a nurse practitioner on overnights. They kept that position filled through 2016. Then the reimbursement schedule changed, two nurses left, the position went unfilled, and the reroute radius widened by eleven miles. Every step is dated. Every step has a signature. Read straight through, the file argues that the 2015 vote was a dormant thing, harmless on its own, and that 2017 is the hinge on which the whole disaster swings — an argument with the considerable advantage of being written down as it happened, by people who did not yet know they would need it.

The paramedics have no memos. They have run sheets. Three deaths in the first sixteen weeks after the vote, each timestamped, each with a dispatch record showing a diverted destination — all of them before the reimbursement schedule was even drafted. A retired field supervisor put it to me flatly: "We didn't need a budget cut to lose those three." The logs are cruder documents than the memos, less complete, keyed to a different kind of accuracy. They are also earlier, and earliness is not the same as sufficiency, though it is easy to hear it that way.

I spent two years treating both files as evidence about causation. They are better evidence about custody. Neither side disputes the sequence. What the county now preserves most carefully is not the sequence at all but the marginalia: both sets of minutes, 2015 and 2017, annotated in two colors of counsel's ink, item 7c circled twice.
