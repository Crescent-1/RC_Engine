[PASSAGE]

Thin sheets are supposed to be weak in bending, and the rule is not arbitrary: a plane of material almost without depth has almost nothing to resist a moment with. We taught it that way for years. Then someone in the back row folds an ordinary sheet of A4 into a fan and lays it across two blocks, and it holds a stack of coins that flattened the same paper a minute earlier. Nothing has been added. No glue, no second sheet, no stiffening size. And yet something has evidently been called upon that the flat sheet had all along and did not spend.

The obvious replies deserve a hearing. Paper has grain; the fibres in a machine-made sheet lie preferentially along the direction of the web, so bending across the grain and along it are not the same act, and one might suspect that the fan happened to be folded the lucky way. Manufacturing varies. Supports slip. A flat sheet resting on two blocks sags at once and may simply have been mounted badly, its edges free to slide inward. All of this is true, and none of it survives contact with the size of the difference: grain and mounting shift the load a flat sheet carries by a small fraction, while the folded sheet carries several times as much.

So the test is arranged carefully. Same ream. Same span. Same weights, added one at a time. Flat first, then accordion, then flat again. Fold count varied. Grain direction reversed. Supports clamped, then not. The effect stays. It is tempting to say the matter is now settled — that watching the fan hold coins has shown us how the fan holds them — but a scale tells you what happened, not why. The procedure is a witness, not an explanation; it can testify that the sheet did not sag, and it cannot testify to the reason, any more than a witness to a house standing through a storm can describe its footings.

Unchanged fibres, several times the load. The reserve the flat sheet never spent was depth. A fold pushes material away from the plane where bending would be resisted least, and stiffness in bending grows with the square of that separation, so a millimetre of height buys far more than a millimetre of thickness would. That is not the same as a thicker sheet. The folds also break the sheet into narrow strips, and a narrow strip cannot buckle into the long wave that carries a wide flat sheet to failure. Geometry, not substance.

The qualification matters. Folds arranged along the span do this work; folds arranged across it do very little. Too many creases and the paper begins to fail at the crease lines themselves. Placement of the supports decides which failure mode is even in play. Still, the verdict holds: the difference is geometric, and material variation cannot account for it. The practice migrates outward, as such practices do — from the paper fan to the corrugated iron roof, from the roof to the ribbed skin of an aircraft wing, from there to floor decking pressed into trapezoidal waves. The cost of accepting this is real, since it means strength is bought by giving up flatness, compactness, and often the ability to fold the thing back into what it was, which for a sheet of paper is trivial and for a wing is not.
