[PASSAGE]

I wonder what survives of ecological resilience in an imagined council leaflet promising swift recovery after floods, whose opening lake diagram recalls the postwar ecology formalised by C. S. Holling in 1973: recovery mattered, but resilience concerned how much disturbance a lake could absorb before feedbacks sustained a turbid rather than clear regime, not the moral toughness of its inhabitants.

The leaflet’s headings could almost supply a travel itinerary: disaster preparedness, adaptable buildings, business continuity, resilient neighbourhoods. As the term passed through disaster planning, architecture and corporate management into municipal strategy, the ecological threshold faded; firms and families acquired the same encouraging adjective. I once liked that capaciousness. Under its cover, checklists replace calculations, slogans occupy the space of spare capacity, and recovery stories conceal unequal exposure. Yet the leaflet’s admission that protection was already incomplete explains the word’s welcome. A firm reopening and a tenant returning to a damp flat are parallel successes only in the reassuring grammar that joins them.

Walls deflect water. Pumps need power. Neighbours knock. In the leaflet’s flood section, that last action feels most persuasive: residents know who cannot manage stairs, who keeps medicines refrigerated, whose silence warrants another visit. My former enthusiasm comes back here, briefly. The adaptation camp can read these pages as practical fellowship; the prevention camp can read the same pages as an invitation to endure official failure. Both find support in the leaflet’s crowded margin, where volunteering receives more space than drainage. River walls can send water elsewhere, duplicate pumps and generators cost money while standing idle, and appeals to courage can make the injured responsible for recovery. Still, the leaflet places maintenance, cooling centres, local knowledge and rapid repair together. Its loosened word earns its keep where that civic conjunction meets the lake’s conditional stability: not in a promise of universal return, but in making dependencies and breaking points discussable through what has to remain usable.

I would once have called that conjunction a remedy. I now call it a useful description of repair, unless the leaflet’s promises have material backing. Flood control, sound housing and emissions reduction retain their separate work; asking flooded tenants to adapt to avoidable damage offers preparedness chiefly at their expense. Nor does the margin’s little drawing of a backup pump establish that duplication settles anything. Suppose the second pump stands beside the first in the same basement, with both sockets below the flood line: the caretaker’s red extension lead, coiled on a hook above them, cannot supply electricity when the street goes dark. Two pumps can fail together. That case presses against my old faith in having more, without making mutual aid an alternative to machinery. A city cannot afford a second version of every bridge, clinic and electrical line; the leaflet becomes credible only where public expenditure supplies spare capacity to services whose loss disables others, pays for rehearsing repairs, and protects residents with little capacity to absorb another loss. Preparation matters, then: here the banality means support arranged beforehand, rather than recovery bought by those hit first and hardest. My verdict is not a compromise between engineering and encouragement: only materially supported, selective resilience deserves the leaflet’s promise.
