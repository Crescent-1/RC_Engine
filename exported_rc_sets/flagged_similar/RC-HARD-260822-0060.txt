[PASSAGE]

In the early 2010s, when digital-executor clauses began appearing beside directions for bank boxes and spare keys, the work was described in modest administrative terms. After a death, an appointed person located devices, preserved telephone numbers, listed subscriptions, and stopped renewals before they became charges or fraud alerts. A kitchen drawer often supplied the first inventory: an old handset, a paper bill with a login address, a notebook beside the router. The online estate appeared to extend familiar paperwork. Keys opened rooms; account details opened accounts; an executor collected both and secured the premises.

Access followed a more particular sequence. Password managers were searched, death certificates requested, security questions answered, and platform-specific verification periods endured. Some services yielded promptly: a bank might recognise an estate representative within days. An abandoned forum profile could resist every request, while an encrypted mailbox remained sealed behind a code known only to its former owner. Possession of a recovered password nevertheless gave the process its apparent centre. It allowed someone to enter, inspect, preserve, cancel, and, where necessary, delete.

Inside the opened accounts, sorting began. Commercial services were cancelled. Photographs were exported. Documents needed by relatives were copied. Direct messages were separated from public posts. Copies were made before deletion, because a joking exchange, trivial on one screen, might be the sole surviving record of a friendship elsewhere. Two practical schools emerged. Continuity custodians retained as much as possible; closure custodians reduced the field to documents, images, and obligations. Each worked like an archivist at a damaged card catalogue, pulling some cards for repair and marking others for withdrawal, though the names on the cards belonged to more than one life.

Platforms offered their own menu: memorialise, freeze, transfer to a legacy contact, download, erase. Each setting silently assigned beneficiaries and exclusions: family members, correspondents, business partners, former lovers, strangers who had followed a public page for years. Apple’s Digital Legacy guidance described a legacy contact as able to “access and download” data from an account, a phrase whose administrative calm concealed a crowd. This was not simply property being sorted—no: more precisely, the account had become a meeting place of partial claims, none sufficient to furnish a whole portrait of its dead owner. Privacy and witness could not be separated by a button.

The appearance of completion was also scheduled to fail, since dormant services would close under their own rules, exported archives would depend on readers and formats that might cease to work, and retained messages could one day be fed to generative tools capable of producing a speaking imitation. Settling the accounts therefore established custodianship rather than a final online identity, but it committed the estate and its chosen keepers to recurrent labour, arguments over access, and fresh decisions whenever a system changed; in those later decisions, made around files whose meanings had already shifted with every copy and omission, the person preserved would be remade again.
