[PASSAGE]

Why did two libraries of comparable ambition, digitising comparable holdings in the same decade, end up giving the public such different things? Both the New York Public Library and the British Library launched major public digital collections in the mid-2000s. Both had prestige, scanning capacity, and deep stacks: maps, playbills, manuscripts, photographs, sound. From the outside the projects looked like siblings. Within a few years they were not behaving alike. One collection kept being picked up, copied, reused by strangers; the other kept growing and staying put. I suspect the difference was already present in how each library described what it held.

Through the 2010s the gap widened. New York released images at high resolution. It published its data. It built small tools that let outsiders pull records out in bulk. The British Library digitised at enormous scale and made much of it viewable, but viewing is not the same as taking. Interfaces changed on both sides, repeatedly, and I confess I first assumed the interface was the story — the cleaner site winning. It wasn't. A well-designed viewing window is still a window.

Copyright and money are the usual explanations, and they are not wrong so much as insufficient; the British Library's holdings carry heavier rights encumbrances, its funding has been squeezed harder, and both facts genuinely narrowed what its staff could do. But constraints shape the expression of a policy, not its premise. The premise lay in the catalogue. The word itself migrated from the bookseller's list to the card drawer to the library record to, finally, the machine-readable file — and at each stage it could be treated either as a finding aid pointing at a finished collection or as an open layer of description through which outsiders make associations the cataloguer never anticipated. New York, gradually and unevenly, chose the second.

The consequence now falls on people who never enter either building. A scan online is not yet public. What is noticeable, combinable, citable is set by the description wrapped around it. A teacher searching for a street name finds nothing if the street was never indexed. A historian cannot aggregate what cannot be exported. An artist cannot remix a file she may only look at. Communities whose photographs sit in either collection find them either working as evidence or held as institutional property with a public-facing shopfront.

The awkward case is the British Library's own crowdsourced tagging work, where volunteers enriched descriptions of illustrations and the collection did become generative — which shows the divergence was never a fixed institutional character, and that my account has to be narrower than openness-versus-closure. Both libraries have since revised metadata, lost and rebuilt platforms, changed licences. Ranking them now would be premature; their standards are still moving, and the cyber-attack that crippled British Library services showed how fragile any such judgement is. That unfinished state does not leave the causal question open. Description that stays revisable, and that invites revision from outside, is what produced the divergence — not scanning volume, not copyright alone. I hold to that, and holding to it costs me the conclusion I would have preferred: that the older, more careful custodial tradition I was trained to admire was doing something other than quietly deciding, on the public's behalf, what the public would be allowed to notice.
