[PASSAGE]

What must "independent" mean if the word is to carry the weight placed on it? Consider where the inspected party pays, picks, or supplies the inspector: manufacturer employees authorized to sign off airframe compliance on the regulator's behalf, rating agencies paid by the issuers whose paper they grade, auditors appointed by the companies whose books they open, private certifiers engaged by the developer whose building they approve. Passengers, bondholders, and occupants absorb the errors. The tally is not an indictment; it specifies a job, which is that a finding must not bend toward what the inspected party would prefer to hear.

Take the first candidate: independence as separate legal employment, the inspector drawing salary from another firm entirely. Follow the money rather than the payslip. The client selects the firm from a shortlist, the firm invoices the client, the engagement partner's standing rests on renewal, and renewal rests on a client who was not embarrassed. So the claim here is not that payroll location is decisive. Nor is it that inspectors are dishonest; nor that a signed conflicts declaration is absent. Something else is doing the damage.

Second candidate: independence as limited tenure. Rotate the team; let no relationship harden. The logic is sound as far as it goes. Press one case against it. A fresh audit or certification team arrives at a complex product whose compliance basis — the accepted methods, the negotiated equivalencies, the history of why a particular analysis was allowed — exists in usable form only in the heads of the manufacturer's own engineers. The newcomers spend their first cycle being taught. They are taught by the party they are inspecting. Beneath the familiarity problem sits a harder one: the inspected holds nearly all the knowledge of the object, so every inspector's competence is borrowed from it, and rotation raises the interest rate on that loan. Rotation still cures something real. It simply cannot be the whole of the definition, because independence was never a matter of emotional distance.

Third candidate: public employment. Grant it properly. A salaried state inspector owes the firm nothing, cannot be dismissed by it, and on the question of who pays is as clean an answer as the problem admits. That much is not in dispute. But the two failure modes are not mirrored. Private inspectors fail by selection — the inspected chooses, so the pliant are chosen. Public inspectors fail by thin budgets, by headcount that never matches the volume of work, and by careers that drift toward the industry they supervise. Those are different injuries and they do not share a cure. The state's depth of technical expertise is also precisely what ran out; delegation began because it did. The argument is not that the work should simply be taken back.

What survives is narrower: the inspected party has no say in who inspects it and no power to reward or punish that choice. Fees go into a pooled fund; an outside allocator assigns the inspector; findings run to the regulator whether or not the client likes them. The price is real — fees higher and less predictable, long working relationships lost, poor matches between assignment and expertise, slower schedules, and an allocator who must himself be kept honest. Accepting that price is the position, and it leaves each party still owing something: firms owe access and disclosure, regulators owe the funding and the enforcement that make the pool credible, and inspectors owe findings that hold without the client's approval.
