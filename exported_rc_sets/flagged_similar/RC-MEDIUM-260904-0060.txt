How did roughly seven hundred people, most of whom could not have seen a gunboat before that morning, come to be standing on the mudbanks of the Combahee at the moment three Union vessels rounded the bends and put out their small boats? The question is not rhetorical. Confederate pickets watched that river; torpedoes had been sunk in it; the tide there is unforgiving. Colonel James Montgomery's report of the raid is brief and pleased with itself, a soldier's document that records burned rice mills and contraband taken aboard. Later recollections, including those Tubman gave to the friends who wrote her life down, give different numbers and a different sequence. The counts do not agree. It is easy, reading the dispatches alone, to take the whole affair as a well-run expedition whose success needs no further explanation than the boats themselves.

That reading answers a question nobody in the mud was asking. The dispatches settle whether the operation was militarily competent; they cannot settle how the operation and the flight became the same event. Tubman's warning about the sunken torpedoes is real and was decisive for the vessels. It explains why the Harriet A. Weed and the John Adams got upriver. It does not explain why anyone was waiting.

Not that the boats were incidental — without them the people on the bank would have been recaptured by evening. But the sequence recorded is odd for a surprise. Gunboats on a narrow tidal river are visible for a long time before they are useful; the pilots needed the channel described to them in advance; and the fugitives came in numbers, with children and cooking pots, from places at some distance from the landings. People carrying a rice pot do not sprint half a mile on an impulse. Whatever moved them had moved before the whistles blew. The discrepancies between Montgomery's tally and Tubman's, and between both and what the surrounding plantations later claimed to have lost, point to the same thing: a distribution of knowledge along the river that no command structure had built and no report could measure.

Something like that knowledge plainly existed. Word travelled between Combahee plantations already, along the paths that carried news of Port Royal and of the Union foothold at Beaufort; the shoreline was worked, not merely inhabited. Yet the raid was one morning. Networks that had circulated news for eighteen months without producing a mass departure produced one within an hour of the vessels' arrival, which is precisely what a standing readiness, by itself, cannot account for.

So the verdict is narrow and, we think, secure: Tubman's intelligence mattered as the hinge between two things that were each insufficient — a naval force that could reach the landings, and a population that already knew the ground and wanted to go. Neither was cause; the fit between them was. What cannot be recovered is the fit's making: who was told, in what words, how many days before; whether the scouts she sent moved among the quarters or only along the banks; what a woman with a pot decided when she first heard the engines. The record keeps the outcome and loses the arrangement, which leaves us asking not whether the raid was planned but how far down the river the planning reached, and by whom it was carried the last mile.
