Philosophy has long held mystical language to a single test: either it yields a proposition that can be assessed, or it belongs to the ineffable and may be set aside with a courteous shrug. Clare Carlisle's Transcendence for Beginners, gathered from her 2024 Gifford Lectures, proposes something more graceful. Where the older religious readers took mystical report as testimony and the literary readers took it as figure, Carlisle takes a life — its texture, its turnings, its documented ordinariness — as the enlarging instrument. Life writing joins philosophy not to soften it but to extend its reach, and the elegance of the arrangement lies in how little it appears to give up: rigour preserved, mystery admitted.

Her procedure is visible in its order. She begins not with a thesis but with a journey to a cave. From the narrative particulars she moves to a question — what does a life reveal, whether it expresses only itself or something beyond itself — and that beyond is then read through Spinoza's panentheism. Biography becomes philosophical evidence.

Call the alternative effect-reading: attention to what a mystical text does to its reader rather than only to what it asserts. Granted, effect-reading names a blind spot the field genuinely has — Christian mystics were forever saying what they did not mean by their reports — but the cases do not all work alike, and that is what the replacement must carry. Eckhart's prose moves; Teresa of Ávila warns against relying on discursive meditation; Thoreau, on Cavell's reading, escapes the conditions of knowledge; Critchley describes hearers being affected. Four different kinds of action, not one method with four illustrations.

Where the argument and the report are treated as parallel — both made of language, both offered to a reader, both citable — the parallel quietly fails, since the argument is built to reach settlement and the report may have been built to unsettle, so that a text engineered as a catalyst enters a philosophical discussion already miscast the moment it is quoted for what it claims. Neither doctrine nor private experience wins here. The asymmetry works earlier than interpretation: importing this writing into philosophy alters what counts as evidence before any reading of it begins.

Critchley puts the demand plainly in his study Mysticism: "Reading [Meister] Eckhart is a question of allowing the movement of his thinking to have an effect on you, an effect which is not reducible to an act of understanding" — a formulation that, set beside Cavell's remark that our primary relation to the world is not one of knowing it, confirms a way of reading without licensing anyone to declare that transcendence has now been redefined as effect.

The friction is the useful thing. Carlisle's argumentative pressure is worth keeping; so is the recognition that a life, an utterance and a reader do not line up as interchangeable evidence, however smoothly a chapter may make them seem to. Effect-reading is not permission to count every response. It is a second obligation laid on top of the first, and obligations do not cancel. Widened from the cave to the page to the institution that trains people to read pages, the field's verdict has to remain a disciplined suspension: an account stays answerable to what a text does, provided it has not stopped being answerable to what the text says.
