[PASSAGE]

A working object can be misclassified by everyone who handles it and still function perfectly, which is why the categories we use to sort musical documents deserve less trust than they get. Consider the fake book that sat on a piano at a Tuesday-night session for eleven years. Its spine gave out around the letter M. Pages from two other books are taped in, one upside down. Chord symbols in pencil sit over printed ones that were wrong; a bandleader's block capitals have been half-covered by a photocopy of somebody else's hand, and the substitution is not marked as a substitution. There are coffee rings on "Body and Soul." The corner of page 214 has been folded so long the fold has gone furry. Archivists who acquire such books catalogue them as ephemera of performance practice; the musicologists who study them treat them as informal notation, a score with the fat trimmed off. Both readings are defensible.

Take the two boxes in turn. A score notates what is to be played. A notebook records what one player thinks or recalls. The fake book has notes on staves, so the first fits. It has private marginalia, so the second fits. Rossini, Ellington, a wedding-band medley: the pages hold all three, annotated by strangers.

The genealogy helps. The term comes from the working "fake sheets" circulated illegally among dance-band players in mid-century America, sold under the counter, each melody stripped to a single line and a chord row. Faking meant supplying what the page withheld. And here is the structural fact that neither box anticipates: a printed score is written so that a stranger can reproduce the music, and a private notebook is written so that only its owner can read it, but the fake book is written to be legible to a stranger who already knows the tune. Three kinds of legibility, not two. Page 214 has four bars of melody and the word "Latin." Someone has drawn an arrow to nothing. The tape has yellowed and lifted at one edge.

So the book cannot be corrected. Add the missing voicings and it becomes a score nobody can turn fast enough. Restrict it to one player's memory and it becomes useless to the bassist reading over a shoulder. Its incompleteness is not untidiness. It is the load-bearing element, sized to a shared partial memory that neither notation nor archiving has a word for.

The tool box fails for the same reason, and this is where the cause sits: manuals are built for completeness and reproducibility, whereas the errors in a fake book are survival marks — a wrong bridge that stayed wrong because the band learned it wrong together. "Folk transcription" comes closer, and still undersells the thing, because a folk transcription can be read at a table by someone who cannot play, while this object requires a reader who is a musician and currently mid-performance. I decline to mint a fourth category. Naming it would recruit it into the family of documents that aim at sufficiency, and sufficiency is exactly what it is engineered to avoid. It is sui generis, not a hybrid. Which leaves the honest question in a different shape: not what kind of text this is, but what a reader must already be able to do before that furry folded corner on page 214 means anything at all.
