[PASSAGE]

The practice of opening a working craft floor to visitors comes to puppetry late, borrowed from glassblowing sheds and print shops that had been charging admission for a century; when the Queens workshop, after decades of locked doors and signed suppliers' agreements, began admitting the public on scheduled mornings, the first thing most visitors saw was a child on a stool being shown how to run her arm inside a live-hand puppet, her fingers finding the seam where the sleeve becomes a glove, and the phrase that circulated afterwards, in the memos and the tour scripts, was "the public hand."

Consider what actually followed. A family tour ran forty minutes late because a costume seam had split and the demonstration piece was the only one dressed. A visiting school group, delightedly, absorbed an entire hour meant to serve four groups, and the three that followed got a talk instead of a table. An apprentice sewing a foam skull was asked, eleven times in an afternoon, what the foam was. Each of these is called an interruption, and they are not the same kind of thing. The first is scheduling. The second is rationing. The third is a transfer of the apprentice's afternoon to the visitors' education. To argue about whether the tours "work" is to argue about the wrong unit; the tours work, differently, on whoever is paying for them.

I grant the tactile case fully. A live-hand puppet on television is a face; in the hand it is weight, balance, a wrist that tires. No screen delivers that. Two views of this benefit circulate among the staff. The demonstration people hold that the encounter is the point and that its depth is beside the point. The fabricators hold that an encounter which stops at the first correct gesture teaches a person mainly that puppetry is easy. Both are right about what they see, because the benefit is largest at the very beginning and flattens fast. The visitor who learns most is the visitor who knew nothing, and that visitor learns something real and small.

A veteran fabricator I spoke with, who had spent years building for one shop and then years explaining that work in a museum gallery, put it plainly: demonstrating is its own trade, and nobody is good at both on the same morning. What makes the access look effortless is the rescheduling, the rehearsed patter, the simplified version of a joining technique that would take three days to do properly, the spare dressed puppet kept on the rack for exactly this. That is the labour the phrase names. "The public hand" is not the visitor's hand at all; it is the workshop's own hand, turned outward, doing work that nobody sees and that comes off the production week.

So the verdict narrows. The opening has made the shop's gestures publicly legible, and it has given some number of first-time visitors a genuine encounter with material resistance, which is more than the finished characters ever gave them. It has not distributed the craft, and it has not changed who can be hired to do it. I would keep the tours; the price is a fabricator's Tuesday, spent teaching foam rather than cutting it, and that hour does not come back.
