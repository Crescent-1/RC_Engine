[PASSAGE]

Every organization I have worked with treats departure as a form of leakage. When a person with decades in the building goes, something is understood to drain out with them, and the standard response is to build a vessel: a system for catching what would otherwise be lost. I believed in the vessels. For about eleven years, professionally, I built them.

The industrial version of this belief is unusually well-developed, because manufacturing has a demographic problem it can count. Plants know when their veteran line workers will reach sixty-five, and they respond with succession planning: shadowing rotations, structured exit interviews, task decomposition, video capture of setup procedures, searchable databases of failure modes. The toolkit is not naive. It was designed by people who had watched documentation fail and had asked why. Their answer — that expertise is capturable if you decompose it finely enough — is sufficient to justify the whole apparatus, and I want to be careful here: I am not yet saying the apparatus fails. It captures a great deal. What it captures is real.

In 2016 I spent six months at a chemical-processing plant in Lorraine, watching one such handoff. The retiring worker was a senior batch operator named Marguerite, thirty-one years on the same reactor train, the last nineteen on nights. The plant's incident rate over those three decades was, by sector standards, remarkable. Management built the handoff to match: a junior engineer, Théo, was assigned to her full-time with a tablet and instructions to record every judgment call she made, the reasoning behind it, and the conditions that triggered it. Six months, roughly nine hundred logged decisions. The design was intelligent. It assumed her expertise consisted of rules she could state if asked patiently enough, and it asked patiently. I should be exact about what that design established, because it is easy to slide: it established that the plant considered her valuable. It did not establish why the plant was safe.

Théo's log is good. Her explicit rules came out cleanly — feed rates, hold times, the temperature bands she treated as narrower than the SOP allowed. He missed one thing. Walking the train, roughly every forty minutes, she would put her right palm flat on the housing of the number-three feed valve and leave it there for two or three seconds. She did not stop walking so much as pause mid-stride. Asked about it, she said the valve "sounds wrong sometimes." Pressed, she could not say what wrong meant. Nobody taught her; she did not know when she started. What she was reading was a vibration signature that preceded overheating in the downstream exchanger by something like three shifts — well before any gauge crossed a threshold. I want to state the finding narrowly. I cannot demonstrate that her hand caused those three decades. I can demonstrate that at least four documented near-misses were interrupted by it, that no instrument in the plant duplicated the function, and that after she left, the exchanger failed twice in fourteen months. So the question worth asking is not whether the handoff captured her rules. It is what else, in that building, was being held together by gestures nobody had a name for and no one had thought to look for.
