[PASSAGE]

In a corridor at Georgetown in the mid-1970s, a card-punch operator kept a hand-lettered sign taped above her desk listing sentences the system had mangled that month. That was the mood. The funding proposals of the previous decade had promised something grander: general-purpose engines that would take any sentence in any source language and return a publishable sentence in any target language, the pairs multiplying like entries in a timetable. What arrived instead was output that had to be rewritten by a human being at roughly the cost of translating from scratch. Reviewers used words like brittle. Idioms collapsed. Pronouns wandered off and attached themselves to the wrong nouns. By the end of the decade the major sponsors had, to a first approximation, stopped believing the promise, and the money went elsewhere.

What survived the retreat were small, ugly, domain-locked systems: bulletins for weather services, standardized phrasing for controllers and pilots, parts catalogues. A critic writing in 1981 — a linguist of real distinction, and not, I think, an unfair man — dismissed the whole surviving lot in a sentence worth keeping: these were "not translation at all, just glorified phrasebooks for diplomats and pilots." He meant it as a burial. He was describing, without quite hearing himself, the thing that had actually been built. A phrasebook does not pretend to give you a language. It gives you a fenced enclosure inside which two strangers can transact, and — this is the part the insult contains — it makes the fence visible. Step outside the phrases and you know instantly that you have stepped outside.

Consider what the aviation systems did. They did not translate what a pilot meant. They constrained what a pilot could say. The vocabulary was closed. Ambiguous constructions were forbidden outright, in both languages. This looks like a scaled-down version of the failed dream, a small clean room inside the collapsed cathedral. It is not. The general engines had been built to substitute one meaning for another and hide the seam. The narrow systems did the opposite work. They marked the seam. When a controller and a pilot ran up against the edge of the permitted set, both parties learned, in the same instant, that agreement had not yet been reached. Nobody designed for that. It was the whole value.

I have been writing from the far side of a distinction that the 1970s proposals could not state, and it is worth saying plainly now what the vantage point is. The evaluation metric of the failed era asked how close a machine's output came to the meaning of the original — a question that treats a translation as a replacement and its quality as a matter of distance. Every scaffold that survived was serving a different question: what does this particular exchange require its participants to notice they do not yet share? A weather bulletin needs almost nothing shared beyond numbers and a handful of nouns. A treaty needs a great deal, and needs to know precisely where the sharing stops. The engineers of the narrow systems had not solved the substitution problem; they had discovered that for most working exchanges it was the wrong problem, and that the useful machine is the one that shows you the gap instead of covering it. Equivalence was never the point. Locating the disagreement was.
