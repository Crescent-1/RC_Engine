[PASSAGE]

In the sorting office at Mount Pleasant there is a photograph, often reproduced, of a picket line in the January cold of 1971: men in donkey jackets, one holding a hand-lettered sign, breath visible. That image has come to carry a whole interpretation on its back. Every account I was raised on tells the same story, and tells it with the confidence of arithmetic. The Union of Post Office Workers came out for seven weeks over a wage claim; it went back with almost nothing on the money, having drained its funds and its members' patience; and yet, so the settled reading goes, it emerged transformed. The strike is remembered as the moment when postal workers stopped being invisible functionaries of the state and became a body that could refuse. Lose the claim, win the dignity. You will find this framing in the standard labour histories, in the anniversary features, in the retirement speeches of general secretaries, in the pub folklore of branches that were not yet born in 1971. It is repeated across generations with the smooth assurance of something no longer up for argument — a defeat redeemed into a moral victory, a stand that shamed a complacent Post Office into eventual reform. Those of us who trained on these books absorbed the verdict before we could weigh it. The immediate loss is conceded freely, almost proudly; the concession is what makes the deeper claim feel honest. It reads like the kind of judgement that has survived scrutiny precisely because it admits its own cost. The question that seems to remain, then, is a natural one: were they right — was the thing they lost worth what they were told they had gained?

But look at where the verdict was actually manufactured. Not in 1971, when contemporaries were divided and glum, but in a cluster of oral-history projects and one union-commissioned pamphlet of 1985, whose framing later historians recycled, citing one another, rarely troubling the strike-pay ledgers or the Post Office board minutes that sat, uncatalogued, a mile away.

So the corroboration was really one voice in a hall of mirrors. And the voice had a further narrowness, easy to miss. Those oral histories were gathered almost wholly from strike organisers and shop stewards still active in the union fourteen years on — the survivors, the believers — while the majority who left postal work after the defeat, older sorters, rural staff, the disillusioned, were never traced and never asked, their absence quietly doing the work of consensus.

I am not here to hand you the opposite verdict. That the sample tilted does not mean the strike was a defeat dressed up; the moral-victory reading may well be true, and I have no ledger that overturns it. What the exhumation reveals is something the labour historians never thought to record: no one ever asked who was eligible to author the memory in the first place — who remained, who could be found, whose testimony the retrieval methods could physically reach. That prior question, of who survives to speak, is not the property of labour history at all. It belongs to the archival sociology of how a record selects its own witnesses, and it is that selection, not the seven weeks, that we should have been studying.
