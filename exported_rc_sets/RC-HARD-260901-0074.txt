[PASSAGE]

Is the number wrong, or is the patient? That is the first question a cross-cover team asks when the overnight panel shows a platelet count of 41,000 in a woman admitted four days earlier for something else entirely, who was 190 the morning before. She has bruising along the forearm where the blood pressure cuff sits. Clumping in the tube can fake a low count; so can a mislabeled draw. So we redraw, this time in a citrate tube, and add fibrinogen, INR, D-dimer, a smear. The redraw costs an hour and rules out the cheapest explanation first, which is the whole discipline of the thing. Order the tests that would embarrass you if you had skipped them. The smear comes back with schistocytes — fragmented red cells, sheared.

The second panel settles it. Fibrinogen 98. INR 1.9. D-dimer high. Platelets 34,000 and still going. This is disseminated intravascular coagulation: clotting factors being consumed faster than the liver can replace them, microthrombi forming, the same cascade that seals a wound turned loose on the whole vasculature. Naming it feels like arriving. It is not. DIC is what a body does in response to something else — sepsis, obstetric catastrophe, crush injury, malignancy. She has no fever, no positive cultures, no new complaint beyond fatigue she attributes to the ward. The mechanism is now fully described and entirely uninformative about its own origin. We have identified the fire's chemistry without locating the match.

So: which trigger? Cultures are drawn from two sites and sent. Imaging is the faster instrument, and a CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis comes back within six hours showing hepatic lesions, retroperitoneal nodes, and a pancreatic mass. Adenocarcinoma, almost certainly, and mucin-producing tumors are a recognized cause of chronic consumptive coagulopathy. The biochemical puzzle closes. What opens is worse. Treating the DIC means treating the cancer, and whether this particular patient can tolerate cytotoxic therapy — with a platelet count in the thirties and a fibrinogen that will not hold a clot — is a judgment no scan makes. It requires an oncologist. We page the service at 2 p.m. The consult is accepted; the attending is in clinic until Thursday.

Cryoprecipitate goes in. Platelets go in. Both are transient; the cascade eats them within hours, and transfusing into active consumption is closer to bailing than to repair. By day three the message is unreturned and there is no plan, only maintenance. I want to be careful here, because the tempting complaint is about handoffs and pagers, and better systems would help at the margins. That is not what stalled. Every instrument the diagnostic apparatus owns has fired correctly and produced an answer that is complete and useless without an act of clinical judgment that is not ours to make, and no protocol specifies how many hours a covering team may hold a bleeding patient in place before it proceeds without the specialist. The comfortable reading is that this is partly a medical limit and partly an administrative one, half biology and half scheduling, and I reject it — the two are not separable here, since the question of who is entitled to decide is itself the diagnostic question at this stage, and I do not know where the entitlement ends.
