Publication, here, means the whole administrative act: a manuscript, an imprimatur, a print run, a distribution. Not a title page. In 1783 a parish priest named Jozef Ignác Bajza carried a Slovak-language manuscript from his rectory desk into a process with several offices in it. Josephine press regulation had recently moved review out of purely ecclesiastical hands and into a state apparatus that still used clerics as readers. The manuscript passed. A Pressburg shop set the type; Bajza paid for it himself. A second part followed in 1784-85. The word then has to stretch: a project of three parts, of which two were printed, is published in one sense and unpublished in another. Every stage of the Slovak literary language after this point is usually read backward through that stretched word.

What survives is thin. The Foreword laments the neglect of the language. A rival's pamphlet attacks Bajza's spelling. A diocesan mention notes the book's reception. A bibliographer records, later, that part three never appeared. Each of these is true. Together they do not say why the third part stopped, and the temptation to read them as a chain is strong precisely because they are individually so clean. This is not the birth of a national epic.

The stall has a cause, but the cause is layered rather than singular: Slovak Catholic clergy in these decades were competing, in print and in correspondence, over whose orthography would count as the written language, so that a book was also a position in a quarrel; ecclesiastical reviewers trained by the Catholic Enlightenment's dogmatic caution read satire on clerical life with a particular attentiveness; and a parish income does not underwrite a three-volume print run. The sequence narrows at each joint. Manuscript submitted, reviewed, printed. Printed, then contested philologically by a man arguing that the spelling itself disqualified it. Contested, then unsold. Unsold, then unfunded. Part three abandoned. No single office closed the door; the door had fewer and fewer hinges left.

Onto this — a foreword, a hostile pamphlet, a missing manuscript — a considerable weight has since settled: national literary historiography claiming a founding text, communist-era readings finding an anticlerical satirist inside a priest, minor-language and postcolonial scholarship treating the case as exemplary of peripheral print, translators preparing editions of a work whose third part nobody has ever read. The interpretive traffic is heavy. The documentary base has not grown by a page.

Return to the Pressburg shop. What was made there is a facade with two wings behind it and, where the third should stand, foundation stones and an intention. The facade is genuine masonry; nobody built a false front. But a building left in that condition is described by what it announces rather than by what it encloses, and the phrase "the first Slovak novel" has always described the announcement. Bajza's contemporaries argued about the dressing of the stone, not the plan. Later readers walked past the gap without seeing it. Until the Trnava and Pressburg holdings yield something they have so far not yielded, the recoverable object is not the vanished third part but the announcement itself — what a stalled building was understood, in 1783 and after, to be claiming.
