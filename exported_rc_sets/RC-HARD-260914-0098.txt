[PASSAGE]

What holds a signed promise together once the paper leaves the hands of the two people who made it? A maker writes a fixed sum, a fixed date, a name. Then the note travels. Each endorser signs the back and passes it on. The promise is one; the promisors multiply.

On the due date the holder presents the note at the place named on it and demands payment. The maker refuses, or cannot be found, or pays part. At this point the holder takes the instrument to a notary, who presents it again in his own person, records the refusal, and draws up a formal certificate of protest under seal. The protest fixes the fact of dishonour beyond later argument: date, place, demand, refusal. It also starts a clock, and the clock governs what the holder must do next.

What he must do next is give notice. A commercial-law treatise of the older kind — the sort that walks the practitioner through each instrument in turn before reaching remedies — describes protest and notice together as machinery that operates without a court. The endorsers are ranked in the order they signed. Loss travels backward along that rank. Nobody sues to start it moving. The treatise calls the arrangement self-executing, meaning the certificate and the letters do the work a judgment would otherwise do.

Merchant bills drawn between inland towns follow it. Bank notes returned unpaid follow it. A bill of exchange accepted at one port and dishonoured at another follows it, with local variation only in how many days the notary may take and whether a copy must be filed.

The uniformity is easy to mistake for strength, and for a long time the mistake cost little, because chains were short and endorsers known to one another. Then notes began circulating further. Six or eight names on the back, spread across three provinces, none of them personally acquainted with the holder. Under those conditions the timely-notice requirement stopped functioning as a formality attending the protest and became the thing that decided whether the paper was worth its face or worth nothing.

Notice fails to arrive in four ways. It may be misdirected, sent to an address the endorser gave when he signed and has since abandoned. The endorser may have moved without leaving word, so that a correctly addressed letter finds no one. The post itself may be slow, arriving after the permitted days have run. Or the notary may miscount the deadline. None of these degrades the endorser's liability by degrees. Each one, alone, discharges him outright. He owes what he owed before he signed, which is nothing.

The machinery does what the treatise says it does, and it does it without a court, and it does it reliably — up to the first name the letters do not reach in time. That endorser is not made harder to pursue; he is gone from the chain, and everyone whose liability ran through his, being endorsers earlier than he, goes with him.
