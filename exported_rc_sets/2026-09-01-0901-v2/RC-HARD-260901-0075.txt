[PASSAGE]

A characteristic earthquake, in the usage that governed field seismology for two decades, means a rupture of near-fixed size that a given stretch of fault produces again and again. The stretch is called a segment. Segments are bounded by geometric interruptions — bends, step-overs, gaps where the fault trace jogs sideways by a kilometre or two. Strain accumulates along the segment. It releases. The segment resets. Under such a scheme, hazard becomes almost an accounting problem: measure the interval, measure the elapsed time, subtract. The boundaries hold the rupture the way a stone wall holds a field. Prediction stops being prophecy and becomes arithmetic.

The evidence arrived from three directions at once. Geodetic instruments on the San Andreas near Parkfield, California, tracked strain loading that was steady enough to look like a mechanism rather than a coincidence; trenches cut across the fault exposed stacked layers offset by past ruptures, and the spacing between those offsets, once dated, suggested a recurrence interval of roughly twenty-two years; and the moderate shocks of the twentieth century, spaced through that same window, seemed to confirm what the trenches implied. On the strength of that convergence, a forecast was issued in 1985 giving a specific window for the next Parkfield event. The interval was treated, in practice, as something close to a schedule.

The rupture came in 2004, more than a decade past the window's close. It began at the wrong end of the segment and propagated in the wrong direction; the slip distribution bore little resemblance to the events reconstructed from earlier cycles. The segment had not repeated itself. It had done something its own recorded history gave no basis for expecting.

Two readings suggest themselves and both are too cheap. Segmentation is not fiction — the step-overs are physically there, and ruptures do arrest at them more often than not. Nor was the failure simply careless work. The trench logs were meticulous; the recurrence pattern was real. Yet the care of the data is exactly what cannot be used as an alibi, because the limit was never in the reading of the layers. It was in what fault geometry constrains, which is less than the model asked of it. Multi-segment events elsewhere had already shown ruptures leaping barriers classified as rupture-stopping. A boundary that usually stops a rupture is a tendency wearing the costume of a law.

So the split hardened into two camps: those treating segment boundaries as the primary architecture of hazard, and those treating them as one variable among many in a cascading system. Neither camp won. Current hazard models retain segment geometry, because it still accounts for most observed arrests, while assigning explicit probabilities to jumps across barriers, because arrest sometimes fails. The word "characteristic" migrated out of mechanics and into statistics, where it now names a distribution rather than a habit. This is not a placeholder pending some truer account. It is the working posture of a field that has decided a locally wrong model, run with its wrongness quantified, tends to be more honest than a locally right one nobody has. Twenty-two years, in the trenches near Parkfield, still means twenty-two years — with the wider bounds now printed beside it.
