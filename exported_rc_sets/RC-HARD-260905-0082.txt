[PASSAGE]

An old man in Redruth, recorded on cassette in the summer of 1987, stopped mid-sentence to knock twice on the arm of his chair before naming a level in Dolcoath where three men had died. He was ninety-one. He had not been underground since 1938. I have listened to that tape perhaps forty times, and for most of my working life I heard in it what my teachers taught me to hear: that the knockers — the small subterranean folk who tapped in the workings, who resented whistling, who were owed a corner of the miner's pasty — were a working occupational safety system in narrative dress. Superstition doing engineering. Not comfort. Not morale. That distinction matters more than I once allowed, and I want to be plain about which side of it I am on. Charming falsehood is easy to defend. What those of us trained in the seventies actually believed was harder: that the lore was instruction, that it transmitted hazard knowledge across generations of men who could not read, and that it worked.

The argument was never that miners believed in fairies and were therefore lucky. It was that the taboo structure encoded pattern. Whistling was forbidden where the ear was the only instrument for judging bad air and moving ground; a man listening cannot be a man whistling. Crumbs left for the knockers imposed a stop. The variance across setts is real, the transcripts are thin in places, the 1987 incident logs cover only four operating and eleven closed workings — and every one of those limits narrows what the correlation could be caused by rather than weakening it. The question is not whether tinners were credulous. It is what a credulous story was carrying.

Consider how a house settles. Cracks appear where the ground gives, not where the plaster is thinnest, and a builder reading the cracks reads the ground. Knocker accounts distribute the same way. They cluster. They cluster where men died. The flat, dry, shallow workings of the eastern moors produced almost no knocker testimony at all, and produced correspondingly few crush and gas fatalities; the deep, wet, unstable setts west of Camborne produced both in quantity. Lore was not evenly spread superstition thinning at the edges. It was thickest exactly where the ground was worst, calibrated sett by sett, level by level, the way a family's warnings about its own staircase are.

The rival account — that all of this came from Cornwall's Germanic mining contacts, knockers being cousins of the kobold, imported wholesale with the technique — is not foolish, and it explains the vocabulary well. It explains the naming. It explains the pasty-corner. It does not explain Dolcoath. If migration carried the belief, the belief should trace migration; instead it tracks fatality geography with an indifference to trade routes that no diffusion model survives. Not that the German connection is fabricated; rather that origin is not function, and the shape of the thing here is Cornish.

So the claim sharpens. Lore did not accompany the knowledge. It was the only channel the knowledge had. Which leaves the thing I cannot yet settle: whether that channel could carry anything a foreman's written notice could not, or whether it was simply what men used because nobody had given them the notice.
