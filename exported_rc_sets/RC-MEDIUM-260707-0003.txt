[PASSAGE]

Consider a young woman from a neighborhood where the local high school's graduation rate hovers below fifty percent. She wins a scholarship to a selective preparatory academy, and once there she studies with a discipline that her teachers describe, without exaggeration, as unusual: four hours of homework nightly, weekends surrendered to problem sets, summers spent finishing coursework other students had abandoned. She graduates first in her cohort and matriculates at an elite university. The story, told this way, arrives pre-packaged with its own moral: talent plus effort overcomes disadvantage, and the rest of us should take note.

Granted, she did do the work no one else was willing to do. But the scholarship she used to enter that academy existed only because a wealthy alumnus, prompted by his own child's teacher to fund "deserving cases," had endowed it two decades earlier. Her opportunity was itself the residue of somebody else's money and somebody else's judgment about who deserved it.

Return again to her trajectory and a further layer surfaces. Her capacity for those four-hour nightly sessions depended on her mother's shift schedule, which happened to allow evenings at home rather than a second job; on a tutoring program at the academy funded by other parents' donations, open to her only because she'd been admitted at all; and on the simple absence of any obligation to earn wages herself, an absence not shared by several of her more exhausted classmates. The discipline was hers. Its preconditions were not distributed at random.

None of this cancels the other. She worked harder than most of her peers; that labor was real and specific, not a fiction laid over passivity. It was also assembled, plank by plank, from conditions she did not build and could not have built alone. The tidy story cracks here.

Now place her twenty years later, a hiring manager who credits her own success entirely to discipline and speaks of it, sincerely, as proof that merit finds a way. Reviewing résumés, she favors candidates who volunteered abroad, who speak in the cadences she recognizes, who mention the same handful of internships obtained through family friends — candidates, in short, who had the leisure her own trajectory required and rarely name as leisure. She is not lying about effort; she is misdiagnosing its source, mistaking a rare alignment of circumstances for a universal method, and hiring, without noticing, for the alignment rather than the method. This is not an argument for discarding merit as a concept; it is an argument that the belief in merit, held this innocently, becomes the mechanism by which the very advantages that produced her get quietly re-certified as qualifications. The myth does not just misdescribe the past. It manufactures the future in its own image, one hiring decision, one endowed scholarship, one grateful alumnus at a time.
