[PASSAGE]

Anthropologists who study expertise have been arguing about the same thing for forty years, and the argument has the stability of a well-set trap. On one side, the apprenticeship school: expert skill is tacit, lodged in the trained body, learned by standing beside a master and absorbing what no manual records. On the other, the codification school: what looks ineffable is only unarticulated rule, latent structure the expert follows without being able to recite, and patient study can write it down. The witnesses are distinguished, the fieldwork on both sides is real, and the verdict never comes.

Examine the exhibits and a pattern emerges that should trouble both counsel. The apprenticeship school's best evidence is the residue: whenever a craft is reduced to protocol, something escapes, and the seasoned practitioner still outperforms the fully instructed novice. The codification school's best evidence is the history of that residue — that yesterday's unteachable knack becomes today's checklist, the ineffable shrinking under study. Neither side notices that these are the same fact told twice. A residue that is always present and always diminishing is exactly what each school requires: it hands the first its mystery and the second its progress, in perpetuity. The dispute does not persist despite the evidence. It persists because the evidence was built to feed both mouths.

The reason the trap holds is a premise the two schools share and neither inspects: that expertise is a possession, something an individual has, the only question being where inside the expert it sits — in the hand or in an unspoken rulebook. Set that premise beside the fieldwork and it wobbles. The diagnostician who is unmistakably expert in her own clinic becomes a merely careful stranger when she locums across town; the skill that seemed to travel in her body stayed behind with her instruments, her colleagues, her nurses' half-second glances, the deference of patients who knew her name. Expertise in these records is less a thing carried than a standing conferred, assembled at a site and dismantled when the site changes. If that is right, the residue and the checklist are both taking inventory of a warehouse that was never where the goods were kept.

This is not a ruling for the relational account. That the container premise wobbles is not proof that expertise is nothing but arrangement, and the ethnographies that would decide it have not been done. The point is narrower and less convenient: the standing dispute cannot be settled from within, because both parties are underwritten by the assumption whose abandonment any settlement requires, and each is professionally built on it — the romantics of the workshop and the engineers of the protocol alike need the expert to be a container. Until one of them consents to empty it, the case cannot close. The ledger stays open, its two columns balanced against each other and neither against the world.
