Political words acquire their authority less from definition than from carriage. They bring an older institution into a new room and let it keep its furniture. Representation arrived in Philadelphia that way, freighted. On the stage the word had meant making present what was absent: a player stands where a king is not. In the councils of the Church it meant something heavier, since a body of Christians was held to be actually present in the persons who deliberated for it, embodied rather than merely signalled. English corporations took the term for their own uses. A borough sent burgesses, and for the purposes of the writ the burgesses were the borough, so that their consent bound the men who had stayed home. Colonial assemblies inherited the practice and added instruction: a town might tell its deputy how to vote and expect obedience, an odd demand to make of a man supposed to be you already. By the summer of 1787 the word had been let out and taken in so many times that the seams showed. It was a garment cut for corporate bodies — towns, guilds, boroughs, chartered colonies — and the delegates were fitting it to a body nobody had yet measured. The shoulders had been made for communities with boundaries and charters. The new wearer was, in the phrase some of them preferred, a people. A tailor can refit a coat. He cannot unmake what it was first cut to fit, and the old darts show under the new ones. The men in that room had no vocabulary for what they wanted that was not already somebody else's.

So the vote of 16 July was not a choice between people and states. The Connecticut proposal did not choose. It divided the word: proportional numbers in one chamber, equal state voices in the other, both called representation, both inside one instrument. The Virginia Plan had asked for proportionality throughout. The New Jersey Plan had answered with equality throughout. A committee sat through the adjournment over the Fourth of July and came back with the split. Madison took it down; Yates, who distrusted where the whole business was tending, took it down differently. Later summaries keep the two plans and lose the committee. What carried on 16 July, by one state's margin, was not a reconciliation of two meanings but the installation of both.

Both sides spoke in the same sentences. Equality was the word on either side of the table; so was representation of the represented. The large-state men meant persons, counted. The small-state men meant political communities, bounded and chartered. The parallel holds in the grammar and nowhere beneath it. Nothing in the surviving notes shows the delegates deciding which object the word named. They decided the seats.

Two traditions have since claimed the settlement as ancestor: one that reads it as balance among constituted communities, another as the arithmetic of persons, imperfectly served but promised. Both are legitimate heirs, which leaves us a lineage that forks. The institutional result is not the part that wants explaining. How a divided meaning came to be spoken of afterward as a single one is the more revealing object, though most of that translation happened far from the room where the division was made.
