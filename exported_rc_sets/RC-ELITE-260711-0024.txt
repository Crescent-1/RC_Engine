[PASSAGE]

I used to argue this at some length, and I was right, which is the part that still surprises me. Officiating technology has made elite sport more just, and the evidence is not subtle. Goals once chalked off for phantom offsides now stand. Penalties invented by a linesman's bad angle now go unawarded. The ball that a bowler swore had crossed the boundary rope is shown, at thirty frames a second, to have stopped short. For a century we accepted an error rate we would have found intolerable in any other institution that decided who won and who lost, and we dressed that tolerance up in a phrase, the human element, as though fallibility were a flavour rather than a failure. I believed that phrase once. I said it warmly. But when I finally counted the reversed injustices — the correct goals restored, the correct dismissals confirmed — the romance thinned. Most of what we mourned as the poetry of the game was simply injustice that had happened to fall in a direction we liked, or against a team we didn't. The cameras took that away. They were supposed to.

And yet the controversy did not shrink; it moved. It migrated from the honest fog of a missed call into the false clarity of a drawn line, the offside decided by an armpit, the frame that might have been the previous frame. The shrug is gone. Nobody shakes hands over a four-minute review.

Here is where my old argument holds and turns on me at once. The technology did deliver correctness, and I still defend that. What it could not deliver was the thing sportsmanship was actually protecting, which was never accuracy — it was a shared vocabulary for accepting an outcome as final. We spoke of fairness as if we all meant the same word. We did not. One fairness means the ruling matched reality; the other means both sides agreed to be bound by a fallible human and walked off together anyway. The cameras chose the first fairness, decisively, and in choosing it they revealed that our real quarrel was never about millimetres. It was about which of the two fairnesses we were willing to keep speaking.

So the verdict stands. Sport is more just now. The price is exact: we bought correctness with the currency of assent. The handshake survives only as choreography. Watch it — the two centre-backs at the monitor, arms folded, waiting to be told what they saw.
