[PASSAGE]

There is a species of literary piety that treats every translation as a small catastrophe, a leaking vessel from which meaning drips away between languages. Something is always lost, the saying goes, as if loss were the natural condition of the work rather than the alibi of the lazy. I have never trusted it. The word *translate* descends from the Latin *translatus*, the participle of *transferre*, to carry across; a load carried across a river need not spill. A competent porter delivers the crate intact. Most of what passes for untranslatability, on inspection, is simply a translator who has not yet found the right word.

The trouble is that the right word keeps refusing to exist. Take the German *gemütlich*, or the Portuguese *saudade*, or the way Homer's *polytropos* holds within one adjective both cunning and much-travelled — no single English coin carries the same weight, and the translator must strike a new one, choosing which face to show. Rhythm compounds the problem: a line that scans in the source falls flat rendered word for word, so the porter must repack the crate to make it balance. I concede this, reluctantly, because the examples give me no choice. What survives the concession, I told myself, is still a craft of approximation — skilled compromise, the disciplined narrowing of loss, the technician's art of getting as close as the two languages permit. The translator judges; but he judges toward a fixed target, and his judgments are corrections, not inventions.

Then one notices what the receiving language actually keeps. The King James renderings of the Psalms are studied as English literature; Fitzgerald's *Rubáiyát* outlived, in influence, the Persian quatrains it loosely quarried; Schlegel's Shakespeare became a German possession, read as German. These are not approximations that got unusually near their targets. They are new verbal artifacts with aesthetic lives their originals never predicted — objects that later writers imitate, that shape a literature's own sense of its cadences. The buried layer here is the old word *auctor*, from *augere*, to make grow, to originate. A translation that a tradition builds upon is not a copy shading toward its source; it originates. And once that is admitted, the technician's model collapses. The translator is not narrowing loss against a fixed target. He is authoring.

So I grant it: translation is authorship, and the porter was always a fiction. But an admission of this size arrives with an invoice, and the invoice falls due on a matter I had filed as trivial — the name on the spine. If the translator originates, then his name must carry an author's freight, an author's credit and an author's liability, printed as large and answered to as fully. That is the price, and it is steeper than it looks, because it revokes the reader's oldest comfort: the belief that in reading Tolstoy in English one is reading Tolstoy, unmediated, and not the second author whose invisibility was the whole condition of the illusion.
