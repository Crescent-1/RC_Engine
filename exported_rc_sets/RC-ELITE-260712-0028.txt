The oboist did nothing loud. Between the second and third movements, in a hall that had grown warm under a full house, she sounded the A again, softly, and tuned to it. Her colleagues split at once. Some called it vanity dressed as diligence, a soloist's flourish in a section role. Others called it plain responsiveness: the strings had drifted sharp, the winds flat, and the printed pitch of the opening no longer held. The conductor said nothing. The concertmaster, later, said a great deal. And what began as a gesture lasting four seconds became, in the green room, a quarrel that no one present could quite finish.

The record shows this fault-line is neither new nor local. German ensembles have long tolerated mid-work adjustment as ordinary craft; several British orchestras treat it as a breach; period-instrument groups retune openly and often. No governing body, in any archive one can consult, has ever written the rule down. The practice survives entirely as inherited habit.

The stakes have since outgrown the etiquette committee. Recording contracts now specify, in clauses once reserved for edits, whether audible retuning may remain on the master. Critics have taken sides with a heat the matter seems not to warrant, one faction charging that a fixed reference pitch honors the composer's intention, the other that refusing to correct a drifting hall cheats the listener of the sound actually meant. Each accuses the other of betrayal. It would be easy to read this as a turf war over who governs an orchestra, a contest of authority between players, conductors, and labels. That reading mistakes the noise for the signal.

For the two camps are not disagreeing about pitch. They are asserting, through it, two incompatible accounts of what a musical work is: a fixed text that performance merely transmits, or an unrepeatable occasion that the score only initiates. Every act of retuning, or of refusing, quietly files a brief for one ontology against the other. One might expect the honest verdict here to be that both accounts have merits and neither should displace the other.

That expectation should be resisted. The commitment each musician holds was formed before argument, in the years of training that taught the hand how a score is to be obeyed or served; it is constitutive, not chosen, and so cannot be defended without circularity, since every reason one might offer already assumes the position it means to prove. Direct debate about what music is collapses into assertion answered by assertion. This is precisely why the proxy forms: the pitch question is the only ground on which the deeper claim can be made without dissolving. And on that deeper claim the record does, in the end, favor one side, for the fixed-text account cannot explain why a drifting hall should ever be corrected, while the occasion account explains both the correction and the refusal as competing fidelities to a single living event—which suggests, though the etiquette will never say so, that the work was always the performance, provided one grants that a recipe is not yet a meal.
