What did the postwar planner believe he was doing when he condemned a street? Not, on the evidence of the minute books, punishing its inhabitants: he believed that overcrowded districts were defective physical environments, and that fire, tuberculosis, insecure tenancy and shared privies could be cleared away with the buildings that housed them.

The belief held for perhaps twenty years. Then it began to leak. Estates rose; families were dispersed; the rehousing lists lengthened. By the later 1960s the profession was discovering rehabilitation — patching roofs, installing bathrooms, leaving the street plan alone — and discovering it not through revelation but through arithmetic. Planning changed because its own remedy produced an invoice nobody had budgeted for.

Consider the remedy as a course of surgery undertaken on a patient who was never asked which limb he minded losing. The operation succeeded; the ward emptied; the follow-up notes recorded that the recovered patient had moved eleven miles and lost his job. That accumulation of follow-up notes, not any single conversion, drove the reversal. Tenants' associations obstructed compulsory purchase orders. Displaced households demonstrated, by simply being traceable, what relocation cost in kin, credit and casual work. Municipal treasurers found the schemes unaffordable. Jane Jacobs is customarily credited with the whole change, and her prose is certainly the most quotable evidence of it — which is precisely the difficulty, since a book articulates a shift more visibly than it causes one. She accompanied the argument; the rent strikes conducted it.

I have described this as a change of method, which understates it; the vocabulary changed too, and the vocabulary is where the trouble now lives. Consultation, mixed use, phased regeneration, community-led renewal: the language is impeccable, and a scheme documented under it can preserve a street's social fabric admirably. It can also raise land values until improvement performs the eviction that demolition used to perform openly. A borough's own regeneration framework may promise, at considerable length, to "work with residents to secure a sustainable future for the estate" — a sentence in which every word is honourable and none is a guarantee of tenancy. Participation is not the settlement of the ethical problem; it is a procedure that can be conducted around it.

The reversal is nonetheless genuine, and ought to be credited as such: no planner today may assume, as a starting presumption, that knocking a district down improves it. What survives is the older habit of treating the people in the way as a variable in someone else's design. So the burden of proof has properly moved, and should stay moved, onto those proposing removal, who must now demonstrate durable gains for the households actually resident — right of return, rents they can pay, the same schools — rather than promise urban efficiency to nobody in particular. Preservation is not automatically justice; a decayed block held intact can be its own sentence. But displacement has lost its standing as the neutral price of progress, and that gain is worth its cost, which is that some genuinely useful schemes will die in committee while a caseworker itemises, flat by flat, what the sixty-four households on the second and third landings would lose.
