The scene repeats. In a rented flat in Toronto in the early 1990s, a woman from Braga is explaining saudade to a Canadian colleague over the last of the dinner, and the explanation is going badly, as it was always going to. She tries "missing something." Too thin. She tries "the feeling you get hearing fado," which only relocates the problem into a genre of music the colleague has not heard. Then she does what nearly everyone in her position eventually does: she says that the word cannot be translated, and she says it with a certain satisfaction, as though the failure were a small inheritance she had just been able to display. That satisfaction is worth pausing over. By the twentieth century the untranslatability of saudade had hardened into a national commonplace in Portugal and Galicia alike — repeated in schoolbooks, in émigré newspapers, in the liner notes of records sold to tourists — and had acquired the function that such commonplaces usually acquire, which is less to describe than to distinguish. The word became a boundary marker. Foreigners were told, kindly, that they could learn to pronounce it and never quite to feel it. Anyone examining the period from outside would be tempted to file the whole business under cultural self-fashioning, and to note that Portuguese has a word doing roughly the work that nostalgia does in English, with a fado singer's rasp attached. The temptation should be recorded and then set aside. What the dinner-table failure exposes is not that Portuguese has a prettier term for a common feeling; it is that the colleague and the woman from Braga were, without either noticing, reaching for two differently shaped things, and that the shape of the second one is what the conversation could not carry across.

Begin with the obvious rendering: nostalgia. It surrenders a great deal at once. Nostalgia has a grammar of prior possession — the village, the childhood, the marriage, held and then not held. The ache is a subtraction. But saudade can attach to a country the speaker has never seen, a life not lived, a child not born, an emigration that was never undertaken. Sailors' families in the sixteenth century felt it for men still on shore. Nostalgia requires a before; saudade does not. Whatever the closest English word is, it is not this one, and the failure is not a matter of degree.

Try a tighter fit: longing. Less is lost. Longing tolerates objects that were never possessed, so the first surrender is recovered. Still it points forward, toward getting, toward the object arriving. Saudade can sit down in the absence and stay there, unhurried, wanting nothing to be restored — the missing thing not a destination but a room one keeps.

So the pattern of failure is itself the finding. Each substitute needs the absence to be a stage in something — before loss, before return — while saudade treats absence as a place one can inhabit. English's emotional vocabulary was built for loss-then-recovery. This is not a gap awaiting a loanword. Across four centuries and two continents the word has been carried by people who had no intention of coming back, and it kept its shape.
