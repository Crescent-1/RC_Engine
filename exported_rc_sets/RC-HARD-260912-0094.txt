The account I learned came from Mincer and Polachek in the 1970s and after, and it was elegant. A woman expects to leave the labour force for children. Anticipating that, she picks work where absence costs less: fields with slower skill obsolescence, lower returns to continuous tenure, flatter wage paths. Then she leaves, and while she is gone the human capital she has accumulated depreciates — training goes stale, firm-specific knowledge dissolves, the network thins. She returns behind where she stopped. The wage gap after a birth is that depreciation, visible on a payslip. And because skills rebuild, the gap should narrow with each year back at work. I taught this. I believed it had the shape of the world in it.

What the account was really pointing at, I came to think, was not the leave at all but the shadow it cast backward: choices made at twenty-three about a child not yet conceived. That mattered more as the policy landscape changed. Denmark, Sweden, Germany and eventually much of the OECD expanded leave entitlements and subsidised childcare precisely to shorten the interruption; if depreciation drove the penalty, the penalty should have shrunk. It didn't much. And somewhere in those years I noticed something I had been looking past: the earnings series after a birth had no recovery segment in it. None. It fell and then it simply ran level, year after year, which is not what depreciating capital does.

Credit where it is owed — and it is owed to the very work that undid me. The Danish study by Kleven, Landais and Søgaard, drawing on tax and employment registers covering essentially the entire population, did what survey data never could: it tracked the same women through the years surrounding a first birth, with childless women of similar age and earnings as the comparison, so that the timing of the child rather than the type of woman was doing the identifying. The design was scrupulous about confounds I had waved at. It could see gradual recovery if gradual recovery existed. That is exactly why its finding carried.

Run the same event study on Danish registers, on American, British and Austrian data, and the picture repeats with a monotony that is hard to argue with: earnings drop by something on the order of a fifth to a third at the birth of a first child, and then hold there — five years out, ten, twenty — never bending back toward the trajectory of women who had no children. The parallel I had assumed was false. Depreciation and permanent reallocation both produce a drop; only one of them produces a drop that heals. What the registers show is women moving into smaller and lower-paying firms, into fewer contracted hours, into occupations with accommodating schedules and low ceilings, and staying. Nothing depreciates because nothing was lost; something was traded. I still think the anticipation story was true — girls do plan around futures they have not chosen yet — but it was a precondition mistaken for an engine. What the reallocation account cannot yet tell us is why the trade falls where it does, since fathers in the same registers, facing the same children, show essentially no penalty at all.
