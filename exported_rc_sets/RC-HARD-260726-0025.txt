[PASSAGE]

In a rented parish hall in Dunstable, a choir of about sixty rehearsed on Tuesdays for eleven years and sounded, by the account of most of its own members, thin. Flat on descending lines. Sopranos scooping. Then, at a Christmas concert in what members remember as the twelfth year, the sound arrived — round, in tune, frighteningly quiet where quiet was asked for. Members have explanations, and they are good stories. The new conductor, they say, has an ear that catches a sixteenth of a semitone. An alto joined from a cathedral choir and the section reorganised itself around her. The hall was reroofed and someone stripped the felt from the rear wall, and after that the sound simply came back at them.

Each of these can be checked, and the checking is unkind. Parish records date the conductor's appointment two years and four months before the concert, which is to say she directed roughly ninety rehearsals during the thin period, and no one recalls those years as promising. The alto's first attendance is logged in a term when amateur recordings — phone recordings, poor ones, but adequate for pitch analysis — already show measurable narrowing of intonation spread. The roof work postdates all of it. What the timeline permits is not a smaller version of the same story, a slower conductor effect, a delayed catalysis; it forbids catalysis as a category, because for each candidate cause the improvement is already underway when the cause arrives.

What we can document, from attendance books and from what perhaps a dozen members will say when asked directly, is duller. Somewhere in the eighth or ninth year, singers began arriving early and holding intervals against a tuning drone from a phone speaker on the piano lid. Two did it, then five, then most of the tenors. No one called a meeting. No one proposed it. The habit spread by the ordinary mechanism of watching someone else do something and doing it too. Whether this generalises to smaller ensembles, or to choirs organised around soloists, we have no evidence either way — but the absence of evidence elsewhere does not soften what the Dunstable case shows, which is a transformation with no author.

So the question that matters is not what happened there. It is what a choirmaster elsewhere is supposed to do with it. There is no event to import. No person to hire. No room to book. The fifteen-minute drone habit can, of course, be instituted from the front of the room on a Tuesday — and instituted habits are a different object from grown ones, because the thing that made Dunstable work was that each singer had, over three or four years, privately internalised a reference pitch, which is not a policy but a slow change in what a body finds normal. It cannot be scheduled. It cannot be audited quarterly. And nobody can be thanked for it. Ask who did this and the honest answer distributes across sixty people and eleven years, which is to say it lands on no one. That parish hall in Dunstable, the one where the sound suddenly arrived, is not a place where something happened; it is a place where nothing sufficiently large happened for long enough that the sound had no choice.
