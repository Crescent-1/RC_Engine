[PASSAGE]

The short diagnostic in fractions descends, more or less directly, from the item-analysis habits American testing acquired in the 1920s: eight questions, a key, and a decision at the end. It is a good little machine. It sorts. When the results come back weak, the repairs on offer are the familiar ones, and I want to grant them their best day. Extra worksheets do produce practice, and practice is not nothing. Timed fluency drills do build the automaticity that frees a child's attention for harder work. Taking away the calculator does expose whether a student can subtract at all. My complaint is not that these things fail — half the time they help — but that each of them arrives after the diagnostic has already told the teacher what kind of error it was, and the diagnostic is very sure. I said "after," which is too tidy. The trouble is not the sequence but the description the sequence inherits: a child who writes 1/3 + 1/4 = 2/7 has been coded as inattentive, so she gets more of what she already does fluently, and the belief that adding across is legal survives every worksheet handed to her.

Consider what converges at that eight-question script. The child has spent two years cutting paper strips and shading rectangles, and has now been handed compressed symbols that behave nothing like the objects. Eight questions cannot sample many forms of reasoning. Thirty scripts must be sorted tonight. The key rewards correctness and has no column for the route. So the failure sits at the moment of coding — not in the child's effort, and not, whatever the strips and rectangles suggest, in abstraction itself, since abstraction would defeat the diagnostic equally if the key recorded reasoning.

Now, the word "careless." Teachers who use it are not lazy and not contemptuous; the label is logistics. It protects fifteen minutes of conference time for the child who needs it most, it spares this child a verdict about her mind, and it turns an untidy pile into three workable groups. So the motive is decent, and the label is still doing damage, because it is no longer a shorthand for a busy Tuesday — it travels. It appears in the parent email, then in next year's handover note, then in the placement meeting, then in the child's own account of herself. That is a larger audience than it once had. What began as a provisional description of one script has quietly become a claim about what sort of learner she is.

The repair is small and it holds. Before any remedy is assigned, ask the child to place the disputed fraction on a number line and talk through the comparison while the teacher writes down the steps rather than the verdict — so that the girl who wrote 2/7, asked where her answer sits relative to 1/3, hears herself say it is smaller, and the whole misconception surfaces in the thirty seconds the old procedure never had, provided someone is writing.
