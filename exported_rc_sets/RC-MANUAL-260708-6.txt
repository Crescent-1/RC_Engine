[PASSAGE]

The settlements I have been describing — firms, faculties, one ministry of middling size — share a ceremony marking the departure of a member. In the week before a worker leaves, a functionary from the personnel priesthood summons her to a closed room for what the tribe calls the exit interview. The stated purpose is instrumental: the departing member, freed from ordinary caution, will yield candid knowledge, and the settlement will use it to improve itself. The ceremony is scheduled late, usually the final afternoon, after the farewells; the badge is surrendered on the way out.

By its own measure the rite fails, and not marginally. Informants inside the priesthood report that transcripts are typed, tagged, and deposited in a shared repository; the access logs, which two settlements allowed me to inspect, show the files are almost never reopened. The same complaints recur across cohorts, sometimes verbatim — the manager who does not delegate, the promotion path that forks into fog — and the studied settlements changed no practice traceable to an interview in the periods observed. Both parties appear to know this. Departing members perform graciousness rather than candor; roughly a third of my informants admitted withholding their true reasons for leaving, wary of the small world of their trade. A ceremony that gathers guarded testimony for an unread archive should, on instrumental logic, have been abandoned generations of hires ago. It has instead spread.

What the rite reliably accomplishes is something no memo asks of it. It converts a rupture into an event with a form. Grievance is spoken once, in an enclosed space, to a listener designated for the purpose, and is thereby discharged — the tribe's version of a confession that ends a matter rather than opening one. The leaver is transmuted from defector into alumnus, a kin category with obligations running both ways. I found no evidence of design; the functionaries believe sincerely in the data, and their sincerity is, as in most liturgies, part of the apparatus.

It is tempting to file this as hypocrisy — a failure wearing the costume of a purpose. The field notes point elsewhere. In these settlements a stated goal operates less as a blueprint than as a license: a purpose respectable enough to convene the room, the listener, the hour. And the failure is not incidental to the success but its enabling condition. Were the transcripts read and acted upon, candor would acquire consequences; the sealed room would unseal; the confession would become testimony, which is a different and more dangerous genre. The rite works because its official product is inert.

Which returns me to the repository, the detail I had filed as an embarrassment. The question worth the fieldwork is not why the interview fails to improve retention — on the evidence, it was never positioned to — but what an unread archive does for those who remain. It certifies to the settlement's living members that leaving is a speakable act with a container: one may go, say the unsayable once, and be filed rather than feared. The archive is less a database than a reliquary, and its power seems to depend on staying closed. When I asked one functionary whether anyone would ever read the files, she said she hoped not, and logged my visit in the register by the door.
