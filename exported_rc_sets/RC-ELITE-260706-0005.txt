[PASSAGE]

I stood on the batture below St. Louis with a hydrologist who kept scraping the mud with the toe of his boot, as if he could read it. The river here has forgotten how to spill. A century of levees, he said, has cut the water from its old bottomlands, starving the marshes of silt and driving the fish and the wading birds toward a quiet vanishing. Reconnect the floodplain, and the river remembers itself.

The engineers I met three weeks later did not speak of memory. They spoke of freeboard, of design storms, of the elevation certificates taped to refrigerator doors in the low wards. In a union hall in a district that floods on a bad spring, a woman from the residents' association laid out the arithmetic plainly: no insurance her neighbors could afford, no house they could sell, no second address to retreat to. Take down a wall, or even set one back, and you are not restoring a marsh in the abstract; you are choosing which occupied kitchens take the water first. Their claim was not a smaller version of the ecologists' claim. It ran on a different clock and answered to a different creature.

The compromise everyone reaches for is the hybrid: setback levees that give the river a little room, engineered wetlands sculpted where the old marsh used to breathe. On the drawings it looks like reconciliation, green hatching between two blue lines. But a setback levee sets back from something and toward something else. The room you hand the river is somebody's field, somebody's block, somebody's cemetery. I watched a planner in Louisville trace a proposed berm with a pencil, and where the pencil lifted was where the water would now be permitted to stand. The engineered wetland does not abolish the risk any more than a detour abolishes the traffic; it moves the load to a road with fewer cars, or fewer voters, or less standing to object. Every elegant design I saw still contained, buried in its cross-section, an answer to the question of whose ground floods first. It merely stopped saying so out loud.

So the tally. On one side, the households who cannot move, whose flooding is a matter of this decade and this rain. On the other, the herons and the paddlefish and the sediment, whose accounting runs in centuries and cannot file a claim. Downstream, a third party: the cities that inherit whatever pulse we release upriver, unconsulted. Three ledgers. Three clocks. The marsh's clock is slow and cannot speak. The engineer's clock is fast and speaks in certificates. Which of these should govern the river? I have written it as if the ledgers could be laid side by side and read against one another, as if welfare and biodiversity and downstream inheritance were sums in a common currency. They are not. That is the trouble with the way I have set it down here — the trouble with the very act of tallying. To count them together is already to assume they can be compared, and it is exactly that assumption the mud keeps refusing to confirm. The river does not add.
