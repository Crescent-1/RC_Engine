[PASSAGE]

Consider the seed that no one meant to plant. A wheat harvest passes over a field; a fraction of grain escapes the combine, settles into the soil, and waits. The following season the field is dressed for something else entirely — soy, perhaps, or fallow — and up comes the wheat regardless, uninvited, punctual, correct in every botanical particular. Agronomists call this plant a volunteer, though they cannot agree on much beyond the name. It is not a weed in the ordinary sense, being genetically identical to a variety men have spent decades perfecting. It is not the crop, either, for no one asked it to appear. It arrives, one might say, without an invitation and yet in full evening dress.

The obvious courtesy is to admit it to the category of crop. The volunteer is, after all, the very species the breeder prized, often indistinguishable in the row from its sanctioned neighbors, and there is a tidy managerial logic in simply folding it into the harvest ledger as a wayward member of the family. The logic is tidy and it is wrong. A crop is not merely a plant of the right pedigree; it is a plant that received a schedule — a chosen spacing, a sowing date, a calibrated regimen of water and input, all the small ceremonies of husbandry the volunteer conspicuously did without. To honor it as crop is to overlook that it competes with the sanctioned planting for light and nitrogen, and that it contaminates the purity on which the crop's market value depends. It has the bloodline and none of the appointment.

The opposite courtesy is to enter it in the register of weeds, and here the case is stronger, for the volunteer is genuinely unwanted, must genuinely be controlled, and, where it descends from a herbicide-tolerant planting of a prior year, carries that tolerance forward into a field where it is now a hazard rather than a convenience — an unwelcome plant by every practical measure, which is precisely what a weed is. Yet the twinning of the two terms conceals their incompatibility: a weed is presumed foreign to the field's intended lineage, an intruder from some other stock, whereas the volunteer is nothing other than the field's own former self, its last crop returning a season late, so that to file it under weeds is to declare a field's past a trespasser upon its present — and the difficulty, one begins to suspect, lies not in the plant's divided nature but in a system that insists on treating one field, one season, one harvest as the unit within which everything must be sorted.

Agricultural classification was assembled around a single line: the deliberate act of sowing, which separated what a farmer meant from what merely happened. The line served admirably in a world where nearly everything green in a field could be traced to somebody's decision, and it smuggled in, unremarked, the premise that seasons come discretely and intentions expire cleanly with the harvest that expressed them. The volunteer withdraws that premise. Where land is worked continuously and viable seed accumulates underground for years, the field carries a memory longer than any single planting, and last spring's will is still germinating under this spring's. The temptation now is to legislate a third category, a formal seat for the returning grain between crop and weed — a courtesy that would fail on schedule, because the flaw was never the shortage of categories but the discrete field-season into which they were poured. The volunteer is not a hard case for the crop-and-weed distinction; it is the discovery that the distinction never described a plant at all, only the intention of the last person to touch the ground. What wants classifying, then, is not the seed but the interval — the years the soil holds an instruction after the hand that gave it has forgotten, a span in which the field remembers what its owner has ceased to mean.
